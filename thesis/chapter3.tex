\chpt{Experimental verification of the digital history teaching methodology} \label{ch:results}

\section{Baseline stage results} \label{sec:baseline}

\subsection{Enrollment dynamics and cohort composition}

The course officially began on October 12, 2024, following the creation of the Viber communication group on October 1, 2024. The enrollment timeline (Figure~\ref{fig:enrollment}) exhibits three distinct phases:

\textbf{Phase 1: Rapid initial formation (October 1--25, 2024).} Eleven students joined within the first three weeks, forming the core group. This rapid enrollment coincided with the announcement of instructor assignments and schedule information through the CDPO administrative channel.

\textbf{Phase 2: Gradual expansion (November 1 -- November 13, 2024).} Six additional students enrolled during November, bringing the total to 17. These mid-course joiners had access to all previously shared materials through the Google Drive folder.

\textbf{Phase 3: Late enrollment (January 22 -- May 11, 2025).} Six more students joined during the second half of the course, including the final participant on April 11, 2025~-- just two days before the last recorded session. This late enrollment pattern reflects the flexible nature of CDPO preparatory courses.

Enrollment rates display a marked temporal asymmetry. Phases~1 and~2 both exhibited rates of approximately 0.46~joins per day (11~students over 24~days and 6~students over 13~days, respectively). The 70-day gap between Phase~2 and Phase~3 (November~13, 2024 -- January~22, 2025) coincides with the winter months when infrastructure disruptions were most severe (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}). Phase~3's drastically lower rate of 0.08~joins per day (6~students over 80~days) reflects both the advanced stage of the course and the cumulative effect of wartime barriers on new recruitment. The full enrollment breakdown by phase is presented in Table~\ref{tab:enrollment-detail}.

The final enrollment of 23 unique participants is consistent with the typical size of CDPO preparatory course groups~\cite{KSPU}. The rolling enrollment pattern, while creating analytical challenges (different students were exposed to different portions of the course), accurately reflects the real-world conditions of pre-university preparatory education in Ukraine~\cite{MON}. This authenticity of the educational setting, though limiting internal validity, is characteristic of design-based research~\cite{Reeves2003, creswell, Sanina2020}.

The cohort size (N=23, below the corpus median of N=33.5; Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}) is contextualised in the research design discussion (Section~\ref{sec:organisation}). Importantly, rolling enrollment creates differential exposure: Phase~1 students had potential access to all 40~seminars and 7~modular controls, while Phase~3 late joiners participated in as few as 1--15~seminars. This differential shapes the individual trajectory analysis in Section~\ref{sec:formative} and motivates the within-subject analytical approach described in Section~\ref{sec:data-analysis}.

\subsection{Baseline assessment profile}

The first three seminars and the first modular control (MC1) constitute the baseline assessment window, spanning Phase~1 (October~12 -- November~3, 2024). These data establish the cohort's starting performance before the course methodology fully stabilised and before the gamification intervention was introduced.

\textbf{Phase~1 seminar characterisation.} The three Phase~1 seminars yielded a mean score of 66.7\% with an average participation of N$\approx$14.3 (as reported in Section~\ref{sec:organisation}). Individual seminar statistics are presented in Table~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}; here we highlight the distributional features relevant to baseline interpretation. Within-group variability was substantial: standard deviations ranged from 20.0~pp (Seminar~3) to 28.9~pp (Seminar~2), indicating that the cohort was far from homogeneous in prior knowledge. Participation declined from N=18 (Seminar~1) to N=8 (Seminar~3) even within Phase~1, demonstrating that not all enrolled students completed every assessment~-- a pattern that persisted throughout the course (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}).

The mean--median relationship shifted across seminars: in Seminars~1 and~3, the median exceeded the mean (67.2\% vs.\ 65.0\% and 74.5\% vs.\ 70.7\%, respectively), indicating left-skewed distributions where a few low performers pulled the mean downward. In Seminar~2, the reverse pattern held (mean 64.3\% vs.\ median 53.3\%), suggesting right-skew. This instability in distributional shape reflects a heterogeneous cohort with no stable performance profile in the early weeks~-- a finding consistent with open-enrollment preparatory courses where participants enter with widely varying prior knowledge~\cite{CohenManionMorrison2018}. The high within-group variability aligns with findings from the systematic review (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}), where individual differences were rarely analysed despite being pervasive~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\textbf{MC1 as first summative checkpoint.} The first modular control (MC1, November~3, N=19, mean 77.2\%, median 88.2\%, SD=28.4~pp) provides the first standardised summative measure. Participation (N=19) exceeded the Phase~1 seminar mean of 14.3, likely because the modular control carried greater perceived weight than weekly seminars. The mean--median gap of 11~percentage points signals a left-skewed distribution: a cluster of strong performers scoring near 90\% alongside a weaker tail pulling the mean to 77.2\%. This bimodal tendency is consistent with the heterogeneity observed in the seminars.

MC1 preceded the gamification intervention by 28~days, establishing a clean pre-intervention summative benchmark. The full modular control results across all seven assessments are presented in Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats} (Section~\ref{sec:formative}).

\textbf{Interpretation.} Three baseline characteristics condition all subsequent analyses in this chapter: (i)~high within-group heterogeneity (SDs of 20--29~pp), consistent with the open-enrollment nature of CDPO courses where no prior-knowledge screening is applied; (ii)~a bimodal tendency (high median, lower mean) suggesting a prepared core of students alongside less-prepared participants; and (iii)~moderate and variable participation (N=8--19 out of 11~enrolled), indicating voluntary engagement patterns even in the earliest phase. These characteristics motivate the individual trajectory approach adopted in Section~\ref{sec:formative}, which tracks within-subject change over time rather than relying on aggregate means that mask the underlying heterogeneity~\cite{ary, CohenManionMorrison2018}.

\subsection{Pre-gamification reference period}

Phase~2 (5~seminars, November~10 -- November~30, 2024) represents the fully operational pre-gamification course. By this point the instructional format, assessment approach, and instructor--student interaction rhythm had been established. As defined in Section~\ref{sec:organisation}, Phase~2 serves as the primary pre-gamification baseline for comparison with the post-gamification Phase~4.

\textbf{Phase~2 aggregate characterisation.} Mean participation rose to N$\approx$15.8 (from 14.3 in Phase~1), reflecting the expanding cohort, while mean performance improved to 71.3\%~-- a gain of +4.6~pp over Phase~1, consistent with the expected instruction effect as students became familiar with the assessment format and accumulated content knowledge. Standard deviations remained high (14.5--25.8~pp across Seminars~4--7 and~9), confirming that within-group heterogeneity persisted beyond the initial weeks. Individual seminar values are reported in Table~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}.

\textbf{MC2 as summative reference.} The second modular control (MC2, December~1, N=27, mean 84.6\%, median 88.4\%, SD=23.8~pp) was the highest-participation assessment in the entire course and was administered on the same day gamification was introduced via the HistoryLikeGame platform~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Participation (N=27) exceeded the cumulative enrollment of 17 at that date, suggesting that some respondents accessed the Google Forms link through CDPO administrative channels beyond the core Viber group. The performance improvement from MC1 (77.2\%) to MC2 (84.6\%)~-- a gain of +7.4~pp~-- demonstrates measurable learning gains during the pre-gamification instructional period. MC2 establishes the performance ceiling against which later modular control scores should be interpreted (Section~\ref{sec:formative}; Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}).

\textbf{Interpretive context.} The upward baseline trajectory~-- from Phase~1 to Phase~2 in seminar means (66.7\% $\to$ 71.3\%) and from MC1 to MC2 in summative assessments (77.2\% $\to$ 84.6\%)~-- is a critical interpretive context for the remainder of this chapter. Any post-gamification decline in scores must be weighed against: (a)~regression toward the mean from the MC2 peak; (b)~increasing content difficulty as the curriculum progressed to later historical periods (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); and (c)~the confounding effects of wartime infrastructure disruptions on participation and performance (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}). The gamification intervention was introduced at the baseline performance peak, not at a trough~-- a circumstance that shapes the expected direction of post-intervention change~\cite{Hamari2014, DichevDicheva2017}.

\subsection{Introductory assessment and baseline synthesis}

An introductory test was administered on October~13, 2024 (the first day of instruction) via Google Forms, intended as a baseline diagnostic assessment of participants' prior knowledge of Ukrainian history~\cite{Hattie2009, Clark2016}. Response data for this test are not available in the dataset, as the test preceded the systematic response collection that began with Seminar~25. Consequently, Phase~1 seminar performance (mean 66.7\%) serves as the de facto baseline proxy. While not a pure pre-instruction diagnostic~-- the seminars assessed material from the first weeks of instruction~-- it provides the earliest available quantitative evidence. This limitation is not uncommon: the systematic review found that only 31.1\% of gamification studies in history education included pre-intervention baseline measures (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base})~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

Three seminars require interpretive caveats before the aggregate patterns are examined. Seminar~13 (N\,=\,2, max\_possible\,=\,131 points) employed an anomalous format with substantially more items than other seminars; its statistics should be treated as indicative rather than directly comparable (see Section~\ref{sec:scoring} for scoring methodology). Seminars~28 and~30 each attracted only a single respondent; these data points represent individual performance rather than group statistics, and standard deviations are undefined. These three seminars are retained in the tables for completeness but should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The post-gamification period contains two structurally distinct sub-periods. Seminars~25--30 (January--March 2025) experienced severely depressed participation (N\,=\,6, 3, 2, 1, 3, 1; mean N\,=\,2.7), coinciding with the peak of winter wartime disruptions documented in Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context} and the delayed arrival of Phase~3 enrollees. Seminars~31--39 (March--May 2025) saw a marked recovery (N\,=\,12, 3, 7, 3, 5, 15, 12; mean N\,=\,8.1), driven by both NMT proximity and the integration of Phase~3 participants into the assessment cycle (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}). The aggregate post-gamification mean of N\,=\,5.6 therefore conflates a deep trough with a recovery phase, and any interpretation of post-gamification trends must account for this structural break.

Across the full course, three tendencies stand out. First, participation was generally higher pre-gamification (mean N=12.9) than post-gamification (mean N=5.6), with a partial recovery in the final seminars possibly reflecting NMT proximity \cite{Hamari2014, KoivistoHamari2019}. Second, the pre-gamification mean (70.7\%) exceeds the post-gamification mean (63.4\%), but this comparison is confounded by content difficulty escalation, assessment format change, and cohort composition shifts \cite{ary, CohenRosenzweig2006, Torgerson202237}. Third, a factual-recall advantage is evident: topic-specific seminars (e.g., Seminars~31--32: 80.8--83.3\%) consistently outperformed comprehensive NMT-format assessments (e.g., Seminars~36--37: 50.7--59.6\%), consistent with the content-specificity of historical knowledge \cite{Wineburg2001, Reisman2012}.

Figure~\ref{fig:progression} displays the trend across all 36 seminars, with post-gamification seminars showing a U-shaped pattern consistent with the progressive difficulty design \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Within-seminar SDs (Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}--\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post}) remained persistently high across both periods, suggesting that gamification did not substantially alter the group's dispersion structure.

In aggregate, the eight profiled students fall into four performance clusters: consistent mastery ($>$79\% mean) accounts for 3 of 8 students (37.5\%: Viktoriia~S., Alina~B., Mariia~S.); topic-variable performance (75--80\%) for 2 of 8 (25.0\%: Anastasiia~K., Veronika~H.); persistent struggle ($<$50\%) for 2 of 8 (25.0\%: Olha~K., Violetta~P.); and progressive decline for 1 of 8 (12.5\%: Anastasiia~N.). This unequal distribution mirrors the baseline heterogeneity documented in Section~\ref{sec:baseline} and is consistent with the high within-group variability ($I^2 > 80\%$) reported in gamification meta-analyses \cite{SailerHomner2020, BaiHewHuang2020}. The qualitative profiles underlying these clusters are detailed below.

Several distinct performance profiles are visible in the trajectory data, consistent with individual differences observed in gamified learning environments \cite{Qian2016, Lampropoulos2024, Schafer2017}:

Viktoriia S. exemplified consistent mastery, maintaining scores between 77.3\% and 93.5\% across 9 seminars (the maximum number of data points for any student). Her mean of 86.9\% indicates sustained engagement throughout the second half of the course, consistent with the mastery learning paradigm \cite{Hattie2009}. Alina B., while appearing in only 3 seminars, achieved uniformly high scores (82.1\%--100.0\%, mean 90.6\%), and Mariia S. similarly maintained strong performance (75.0\%--87.5\%, mean 79.5\%) across her 3 appearances. Such consistent high performance in gamified environments has been associated with intrinsic motivation and sustained engagement \cite{Sailer2017, Hamari2014}.

A different pattern characterised students whose competence was strong but whose scores varied by topic. Anastasiia K. (4 seminars, mean 79.7\%) and Veronika H. (4 seminars, mean 75.1\%) showed wider score ranges. Veronika H.'s trajectory is particularly instructive: she scored 100\% on Seminar 31, dropped to 45.7\% on the more difficult Seminar 36, recovered to 86.7\% on Seminar 37 (an NMT-format comprehensive test), and finished at 67.8\% on Seminar 39. This pattern suggests that topic-specific difficulty rather than overall competence drives score variation for this group, a finding consistent with the content-specificity of historical knowledge assessments \cite{Reisman2012, MonteSano2012, Ethier202311}.

Can struggling students improve through gamified practice? Olha K. (5 seminars, mean 47.8\%) showed a distinctive pattern: a low initial score (30.8\% on Seminar 31), a strong improvement to 81.4\% (Seminar 33), followed by a decline in later comprehensive assessments (43.5\%--47.8\%). Similarly, Violetta P. (5 seminars, mean 47.0\%) showed high variability (23.3\%--73.9\%), with her best performance on a seminar focused on a specific historical period (Seminar 36: 73.9\%) and her weakest on the comprehensive NMT-format test (Seminar 37: 23.3\%). The gap between topic-specific and comprehensive assessment performance aligns with research on transfer of learning in gamified environments \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012, 44Shiue2016}.

Not all trajectories pointed upward. Anastasiia N. appeared in 4 seminars with a concerning downward trend: 53.3\% (Seminar 26) $\rightarrow$ 69.2\% (Seminar 31) $\rightarrow$ 32.6\% (Seminar 33) $\rightarrow$ 29.9\% (Seminar 39). Although her Seminar 31 score represented a temporary improvement, the overall trajectory shows declining performance, possibly related to increasing course difficulty or competing demands. Such declining trajectories may reflect motivational challenges, as the gamification literature notes that initial engagement effects can wane over extended implementations \cite{Hamari2014, KoivistoHamari2019, DichevDicheva2017}.

One student merits particular attention. Danylo T. submitted 7 responses to Seminar 37 (the 2016 NMT comprehensive test), with scores progressively improving from 18.9\% to 94.4\% (17, 24, 24, 38, 44, 59, and 85 out of 90 points). This pattern of repeated attempts with steady improvement illustrates how the feedback mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:principle-grounding} enabled self-regulated learning \cite{Bonwell1991}. The student used each attempt as a formative learning opportunity~-- a pattern consistent with the testing effect \cite{RoedigerButler2011, Dunlosky2013} and the mastery learning approach \cite{Hattie2009, HattieTimperley2007}. On the subsequent Seminar 39, this student scored 82.8\%, demonstrating retention of the knowledge gained through iterative practice \cite{Sailer2017, 17Manzanares2019, Zhonggen2019}.

Of the 17 named participants in the post-gamification period, 8 appeared in 3 or more seminars (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}), while 9 appeared in only 1--2 datasets. The low appearance rate of many students may reflect: (a) irregular attendance at synchronous sessions, (b) inconsistent provision of names in quiz forms (the name field was optional), or (c) late enrollment limiting the number of available assessments. Such variable participation rates are a common challenge in distance education research \cite{UNESCO2023} and in gamification studies conducted in naturalistic settings \cite{Deterding2011, Hung201757}. Of the 23 enrolled students, 6 never appear in any post-gamification assessment; the trajectory analysis thus covers 17 of 23 (73.9\%) of the cohort, with detailed profiling possible for only 8 of 23 (34.8\%). This coverage rate exceeds the 31.1\% baseline-reporting rate found in the systematic review of gamification assessment quality \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} but falls short of ideal standards identified in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}, constraining generalisability and motivating cautious interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The modular control data exhibit three salient features. First, participation declined steadily from 27 respondents (MC~2) to 7--9 (MCs~5--7), reflecting both the general attrition common in extended distance courses and the increasing impact of wartime disruptions in the later months \cite{UNESCO2023}. Second, mean scores were highest in MC~2 (84.6\%) and MC~4 (83.6\%), with a notable dip in MC~5 (63.8\%) that coincides with the transition to 20th-century content covering complex political events (World War~II, Soviet period, independence movement); the recovery in MCs~6 and 7 (72.1\% and 74.3\%) suggests adaptation to this content difficulty. Third, the consistently high standard deviations (14.84--28.39) confirm the substantial individual variation observed in the seminar data, consistent with the heterogeneous group composition.

The MC~5 dip merits closer examination because it aligns with concurrent seminar evidence. MC~5 (63.8\%, March 30) coincided with Seminars 36 (59.6\%) and 37 (50.7\%)~-- convergent low scores across both formative and summative instruments for the same content period, confirming that the decline reflects content difficulty rather than a format artefact. Conversely, MC~4 (83.6\%, February 23) aligned with Seminars 31 (80.8\%) and 32 (83.3\%), where both instruments produced convergent high scores. The MC~5$\rightarrow$MC~7 recovery of +10.5 percentage points over six weeks (63.8\%$\rightarrow$74.3\%) parallels the seminar recovery from Seminar 37 to 39 (+12.8~pp), reinforcing the progressive difficulty design principle (Section~\ref{sec:principle-grounding}). Nevertheless, MC~7 (74.3\%) did not recover to the MC~2 ceiling of 84.6\% established in the baseline period (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-summary}), suggesting that cumulative content difficulty imposed a lasting performance constraint.

The U-shaped pattern in modular control performance is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:mc-progression}, which plots mean scores with respondent counts.

The modular controls were emphasised as ``mandatory for all'' (``ОБОВ'ЯЗКОВО до виконання'' / ``Проходження обов'язкове''), distinguishing them from the optional seminar quizzes. This distinction between mandatory summative and optional formative assessments aligns with assessment design principles in educational research \cite{Hattie2009, Bonwell1991}. The mandatory nature is reflected in quantifiably higher participation: post-gamification modular controls (MCs~3--7) attracted a mean of N\,=\,10.6 respondents, compared with N\,=\,5.6 for post-gamification seminars~-- an 89\% difference. The contrast was most pronounced during the Seminar 25--30 participation trough (mean N\,=\,2.7), when MC~3 (N\,=\,15) and MC~4 (N\,=\,13) drew 4.8--5.6 times more respondents than concurrent seminars. This pattern indicates that the mandatory framing effectively counteracted the voluntary attrition observed in formative assessments (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rationale}), consistent with the role of accountability structures in sustaining engagement \cite{Hattie2009}.

The formative data converge on three patterns. First, the participation structural break indicates that aggregate post-gamification statistics conflate the Seminars~25--30 trough with the subsequent recovery phase. Second, the four trajectory clusters mirror the baseline heterogeneity documented in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}. Third, convergent evidence from modular controls and concurrent seminars confirms that late-period score declines reflect content difficulty rather than assessment format artefacts. These performance patterns must be understood against the backdrop of the wartime conditions that shaped participation throughout the course.

This section examines the classification and temporal distribution of reported absences, the intersection of enrollment dynamics with performance trajectories, and the course timeline as an integrative temporal frame.

\textit{Cross-referencing absences with performance.} Comparing the absence data with the individual trajectory data (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) reveals four distinct profiles.

\textbf{Oleksandra Z.} (7 absences: internet, family, unspecified) achieved MC1\,=\,79.4\% and MC2\,=\,83.7\% and appeared in Seminar~17 (60.5\%) during the pre-gamification phase, but recorded zero post-gamification seminar or assessment appearances. Her escalating absences~-- infrastructure-driven in December and family-attributed in February--March~-- accompanied a complete withdrawal from post-gamification learning activities.

\textbf{Nataliia B.} (5 absences, predominantly infrastructure) recorded zero seminar appearances across the entire dataset, yet achieved MC1\,=\,100.0\%, MC3\,=\,97.5\%, MC4\,=\,96.3\%, and MC5\,=\,72.3\%, for an MC mean of 91.5\%~-- the highest among all frequently absent students. Her MC5 dip to 72.3\% parallels the cohort-wide MC5 mean of 63.8\% (Section~\ref{sec:formative}), confirming that the decline reflects content difficulty rather than disengagement. This profile demonstrates that mandatory summative structures sustain academic performance even under severe infrastructure disruption \cite{Martynets202428}.

\textbf{Yana M.} (5 absences: internet, family, power, unspecified) presents the most analytically informative profile. She appeared in four seminars (Seminar~16: 44.0\%, Seminar~21: 23.5\%, Seminar~24: 64.3\%, Seminar~31: 69.2\%; seminar mean 50.2\%) and five modular controls (MC1: 91.2\%, MC2: 86.0\%, MC3: 82.5\%, MC4: 64.8\%, MC6: 96.7\%; MC mean 84.2\%). The resulting mandatory--voluntary differential of 34.0 percentage points is the largest among all profiled students and provides individual-level confirmation of the cohort-level pattern documented in Section~\ref{sec:formative} \cite{Zamkova2023299}.

\textbf{Violetta P.} (3 absences: internet, late arrival, unspecified) appeared in five post-gamification seminars with a mean score of 47.0\%, demonstrating sustained voluntary engagement despite synchronous interruptions. Her continued seminar participation contrasts with Oleksandra Z.'s post-gamification withdrawal, suggesting that lower cumulative absence frequency and the availability of gamified assessment alternatives together supported continued engagement.

\textit{Engagement typology under wartime disruption.} Four engagement modes can be distinguished among frequent absentees, each shaped differently by wartime conditions. Nataliia B. represents \emph{exclusively summative} engagement: zero seminar appearances yet the highest MC mean (91.5\%) among frequent absentees, demonstrating that mandatory assessment structures sustain academic performance even when synchronous attendance is impossible \cite{Hattie2009}. Yana M. exhibits an \emph{MC-dominant with sporadic seminars} pattern (MC mean 84.2\%, seminar mean 50.2\%), reflecting the cohort-level mandatory--voluntary differential at the individual level. Violetta P. demonstrates \emph{sustained voluntary} engagement across five post-gamification seminars despite synchronous interruptions. Oleksandra Z. exhibits \emph{post-gamification disengagement}, with all activity confined to the pre-gamification period. All three ``present'' modes were enabled by the asynchronous delivery infrastructure described in Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}, which decoupled participation from synchronous attendance.

That these frequently absent students remained enrolled and continued submitting quiz responses suggests that asynchronous materials (recorded lectures, shared quiz links, Google Drive resources) sustained their engagement despite synchronous disruptions~-- consistent with research on the role of digital technologies in maintaining educational continuity during crises \cite{UNESCO2023, Striuk_2022}.

\subsection{Course timeline overview}

To place the attendance and performance findings in temporal perspective, a course timeline was reconstructed from the chat log. Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} maps all assessment activities across the seven-month course (October 2024 -- May 2025), distinguishing between seminar quizzes (24), modular controls (7), lectures (24, of which 3 included quiz assessments), and the introductory test.

The timeline confirms that assessment frequency remained relatively consistent throughout the course, with seminars held approximately weekly and modular controls distributed across the seven-month period (7 controls: November 2024 -- May 2025). A brief reduction in activity around late December 2024 -- early January 2025 (winter holidays) was followed by intensified activity, and the gamification platform \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Titova2025cte}, introduced at the course midpoint (December~1, 2024) and remaining in its original v1 configuration throughout the course, provided supplementary assessment and practice opportunities for the second half.

The combined timeline evidence delineates three operational phases. Phase~1 (October--November 2024) established the baseline cohort with consistent participation. The transition period (December 2024 -- January 2025) brought three concurrent developments: HistoryLikeGame's introduction, the December absence peak, and the 70-day enrollment gap. Phase~3 (February--May 2025) combined the trough-and-recovery pattern documented in Section~\ref{sec:formative} with the highest total absence frequency of any three-month span (19 of 33 reports).

These three factors~-- infrastructure-attributable absences, enrollment suspension, and chronic-absence concentration among early enrollees~-- collectively define the environmental constraints within which the course operated.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of findings in context of the systematic reviews}

Seminar performance across 36 sessions, modular control trajectories, individual learning profiles, and attendance patterns under wartime conditions can be organised along three interpretive threads, each connected to a distinct component of the theoretical framework established in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}.

The first thread concerns \emph{engagement and its motivational mechanisms}. The 70.3\% engagement-positive rate from the gamification review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} and the 100\% engagement-positive rate from the digital history review \cite{Korniienko2025cte} benchmark this study's participation patterns. \citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}'s self-determination theory~\cite{RyanDeci2000} and \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}'s flow theory~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} furnish the motivational interpretation of the mandatory--voluntary differential and the score variance distribution.

The second thread concerns \emph{learning outcomes and within-group heterogeneity}. The 51.4\%/37.8\%/10.8\% outcome distribution and the \(d\,=\,0.28\)--\(0.61\) effect-size range from the gamification review (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}) frame the factual/interpretive score differential and the trajectory cluster analysis.

The third thread concerns \emph{methodological positioning}. The quality gap documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}~-- with definitional clarity decoupled from rigour~-- defines the context for this study's design choices and acknowledged limitations, treated in Sections~3.4.2--3.4.3.

\textit{Engagement outcomes and motivational mechanisms.} The most consistently supported finding in the gamification literature is a positive effect on engagement: 70.3\% of the 74 reviewed studies reported this outcome \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, replicating the meta-analytic consensus established by \citeauthor{Hamari2014}~\cite{Hamari2014} and \citeauthor{Sailer2017}'s experimental need-satisfaction model~\cite{Sailer2017}. The digital history review by \citeauthor{Korniienko2025cte}~\cite{Korniienko2025cte} found an even stronger signal (100\% of 6 studies reporting positive engagement), suggesting that history-specific gamification may be particularly well-matched to sustained motivation. The present study is broadly consistent: 73.9\% of enrolled students appeared in at least one post-gamification assessment despite the wartime disruptions documented in Section~\ref{sec:attendance}. The mandatory--voluntary participation differential (89\%, intensifying to a 5.6-fold ratio during the Seminars~25--30 trough) indicates that assessment stakes rather than platform availability drove participation under environmental stress.

\citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}'s self-determination theory~\cite{RyanDeci2000} (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) provides a motivational framework for this differential. The mandatory MC framing reflects \emph{controlled motivation} (external obligation), while voluntary seminar engagement reflects \emph{autonomous motivation} (intrinsic interest or self-regulation). The four engagement modes identified in Section~\ref{sec:attendance} map onto this spectrum: Nataliia~B.'s and Yana~M.'s MC-dominant profiles reflect controlled motivation structures, Violetta~P.'s sustained voluntary participation represents autonomous motivation persisting under disruption, and Oleksandra~Z.'s disengagement illustrates a failure mode where neither orientation was sufficient. \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}'s flow theory~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} offers a complementary lens: the progressive difficulty structure was designed to maintain challenge--skill balance, but the substantial baseline heterogeneity (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}) meant that the same assessment represented an optimal challenge for some students and either excessive difficulty or insufficient challenge for others.

\textit{Learning outcomes and the factual--interpretive differential.} The systematic review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} found positive learning effects in 51.4\% of studies ($d\,{=}\,0.28$--$0.61$), with stronger effects for factual recall than interpretive understanding; \citeauthor{BaiHewHuang2020}~\cite{BaiHewHuang2020} confirmed this pattern meta-analytically. The present study replicates the differential within a single course: period-specific post-gamification seminars (Seminars~31--32: 80.8\%--83.3\%) systematically outperformed comprehensive NMT-format assessments (Seminars~36--37: 50.7\%--59.6\%). The aggregate pre-post comparison (70.7\% vs.\ 63.4\%) is substantially confounded by the three factors disaggregated in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}; the comprehensive-format score pattern places this study among the 37.8\% reporting no significant advantage on broader learning outcomes.

A persistent feature that the reviewed literature rarely examines is within-group heterogeneity \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Within-group SDs remained at 20--29\,pp across both periods, and the four trajectory clusters (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) coexisted throughout the course. The \citeauthor{SailerHomner2020}~\cite{SailerHomner2020} meta-analysis confirms that effect sizes are substantially moderated by individual learner characteristics that gamification design alone cannot neutralise. The consistent conclusion across both the present data and the meta-analytic literature is that gamification supports continued engagement and factual-level learning but does not uniformly elevate all learners \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}.

\textit{Longitudinal depth and what extended observation makes visible.} Only 4 of the 74 reviewed studies employed implementations lasting more than one week \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}; the majority involved interventions of 20 minutes or less. The seven-month implementation enables three observations invisible in brief designs. First, the participation structural break is a dynamic pattern requiring at least three months to detect. Second, the four trajectory clusters require multi-point observation to distinguish; a two-time-point design collapses all four into a single misleading contrast. Third, the MC U-shaped arc (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) would appear as only a terminal data point in a single-assessment design. The iterative mastery mechanism similarly requires an extended course to confirm as durable rather than transient. The 7-month duration is therefore both a naturalistic feature and a methodological contribution: it demonstrates what longitudinal commitment makes analytically visible in gamification research.

\textit{Methodological positioning.} The quality gap documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}~-- mean MRQS 4.31/10, with definitional clarity and rigour decoupled \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}~-- defines the context for this study's design choices. The present study aims to achieve both simultaneously through operational gamification definition (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}), full data-source preservation, explicit limitation acknowledgement, descriptive analysis without unsupported causal claims, and within-group variability reporting alongside means. The MRQS self-assessment (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}) places the study in the upper 16.2\% of the reviewed literature.

\subsection{Pre-gamification vs post-gamification comparison}

The aggregate pre-post comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:pre-post}) shows a mean score 7.3\,pp higher in the pre-gamification period (70.7\% vs.\ 63.4\%) and substantially higher average participation (N\,=\,12.9 vs.\ N\,=\,5.6). These aggregate figures are not reliable indicators of gamification effectiveness: three structurally distinct sources of variance operate jointly on the post-gamification period, and each can be quantified from the data in Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre} and~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post}. The figure should be read alongside the disaggregation below.

\textit{Confound~1: Assessment format shift.} All 24 pre-gamification seminars (Seminars~1--24) used period-specific formats covering a single delimited historical unit. The post-gamification period introduced comprehensive NMT-format assessments spanning multiple periods simultaneously (Seminars~36, 37, 39), which are structurally more demanding: they require cross-period synthesis and recall rather than within-unit retrieval \cite{Wineburg2001}. When post-gamification scores are disaggregated by format, the contrast is unambiguous: the five period-specific post-gamification seminars (Seminars~25, 31--33, 35) yield a mean of 78.8\%~-- \emph{8.1\,pp above} the pre-gamification mean of 70.7\%~-- while the three comprehensive NMT-format seminars (Seminars~36, 37, 39) yield a mean of 57.9\%. The aggregate post-gamification mean of 63.4\% is therefore a weighted average of these two structurally incommensurable assessment types, not a measure of gamification's effect on learning \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}.

\textit{Confound~2: Content difficulty escalation.} The post-gamification period covers 20th-century and contemporary Ukrainian history~-- the most demanding portion of the NMT curriculum, characterised by greater event density and interpretive complexity than the ancient-to-early-modern content assessed pre-gamification. The modular control sequence provides within-course difficulty calibration on a consistent format: MC1 (November 2024, medieval and Cossack content, N\,=\,19) achieved a group mean of 77.2\%, while MC5 (March 2025, 20th-century content, N\,=\,9) dropped to 63.8\%~-- a 13.4\,pp decline on assessments with \emph{identical format} and the same gamification infrastructure fully in place. This within-MC gradient confirms that content complexity is the primary driver of the downward pressure on late-course scores.

\textit{Confound~3: Participation composition and a controlled comparator.} Post-gamification voluntary seminar participation (mean N\,=\,5.6) reflects a wartime-disrupted, self-selected subset, while pre-gamification participation (mean N\,=\,12.9) reflects a more stable, obligation-framed early cohort (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}). The modular controls maintained consistent accountability framing throughout the course and sustained substantially higher participation (post-gamification MC mean N\,=\,10.6). Using the MC sequence as a controlled comparator, the pre-post gap narrows markedly: MC1 (pre-gamification, 77.2\%) versus MC2--MC7 (post-gamification, mean 75.6\%) yields a difference of only 1.6\,pp~-- versus the 7.3\,pp gap in the confounded seminar data. When assessment format and participation mode are held approximately constant, the apparent performance decline largely disappears. These findings remain descriptive and interpretive rather than causal (Limitation~3) \cite{creswell, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

The modular control participation data provide the clearest controlled engagement signal. MC2~-- administered on the same day HistoryLikeGame v1 was deployed (Section~\ref{sec:platform-evolution})~-- achieved the highest participation and the highest score of any assessment across the entire course (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}). This confluence at the precise moment of gamification introduction is the most direct positive signal in the dataset \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, and grounds a critical observation: the post-gamification period began with the course's best result, not a decline.

The six design principles identified in the systematic review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} were implemented in this study, drawing on evidence from both the gamification literature \cite{Sailer2017, Qian2016} and digital history pedagogy \cite{Korniienko2025cte}. All six principles were implemented simultaneously, whereas the majority of gamification implementations reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review} \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} typically employed only 1--3 principles. For example, \cite{1Barbatsis201159} focused primarily on narrative integration, while \citeauthor{16Chen201447}~\cite{16Chen201447} emphasised social elements, and \cite{55Squire2004} combined historical authenticity with narrative integration. The simultaneous implementation of all six principles in the present study reflects the guidelines developed in Section~\ref{sec:digcomp} \cite{Titova2025cte} and aligned with DigComp~2.0 \cite{Vuorikari2016}.

Beyond documenting that all six principles were implemented, the empirical record contains observable traces of each principle's activation. \textit{Historical authenticity and progressive difficulty} are jointly confirmed by the content-specificity of score patterns: period-specific post-gamification seminars outperformed the pre-gamification mean, while the MC U-shaped arc maps directly onto the curriculum difficulty gradient. \textit{Immediate feedback} is evidenced at group level (MC2's peak coinciding with HistoryLikeGame's deployment) and individual level (the iterative mastery case discussed below). \textit{Social elements} are evidenced by the accountability-driven MC participation during the Seminars~25--30 trough, when mandatory controls attracted participants at multiples of the voluntary seminar rate \cite{Sailer2017}. \textit{Narrative integration} is evidenced by students' chat acknowledgements of quiz naming themes. \textit{Multiple modalities} are evidenced by the 62-module catalogue's structural heterogeneity (Table~\ref{tab:game-modules}), consistent with \citeauthor{Qian2016}'s~\cite{Qian2016} finding that modality diversity correlates with broader engagement profiles.

The modular control trajectory (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) provides the most interpretable longitudinal performance signal because MC assessments maintain consistent format across all seven administrations. Three features carry interpretive weight. First, the MC1-to-MC2 transition~-- the only controlled pre-to-post comparison available~-- shows a positive shift of +7.4\,pp, the \emph{opposite direction} from the confounded seminar comparison. Second, the MC5 trough aligns with the most demanding content unit (20th-century Ukrainian history), and the MC6--MC7 recovery is consistent with a difficulty--consolidation dynamic. Third, the post-gamification MC average (75.6\%) differs from MC1 by only 1.6\,pp: when format is controlled, there is no material performance decline across the course. This pattern aligns with findings from \cite{8Yu2014} and \cite{6Czerwinski2018} and with the systematic review's factual-recall advantage \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

Danylo~T.'s seven-attempt sequence at Seminar~37 (Section~\ref{sec:formative}) illustrates at the individual level how the feedback principle generates learning progression. Two theoretically grounded mechanisms explain the 18.9\%$\to$94.4\% trajectory: the \emph{testing effect} \cite{RoedigerButler2011, Dunlosky2013}, whereby each re-attempt constituted a targeted retrieval episode with error correction, and the \emph{formative feedback loop} \cite{HattieTimperley2007}, iterated to mastery threshold \cite{Hattie2009}. The durability is confirmed by Seminar~39 (82.8\%), where his retention score exceeded the group mean by 17.9\,pp~-- consistent with the long-term advantage predicted by retrieval-practice theory. Similar repeated-attempt designs appear in \cite{5kazanidis2018alpha} and \cite{50Lye2013}, but neither provides multi-session retention evidence; the present study's longitudinal duration enables confirmation of the mechanism as durable.

\textit{Synthesis.} The properly disaggregated pre-post comparison supports a more substantiated conclusion than the aggregate mean contrast. The 7.3\,pp decline is primarily explained by the three confounds above: period-specific post-gamification seminars outperformed the pre-gamification mean by 8.1\,pp, and the controlled MC comparator narrows the gap to 1.6\,pp. The six design principles leave observable traces across participation, score, and individual behaviour data: the MC trajectory maps onto progressive difficulty; immediate feedback enables iterative mastery (confirmed as durable through Danylo~T.'s delayed retention); social accountability sustains MC participation during voluntary seminar collapse; narrative integration generates documentable affective engagement in the chat record. These findings are consistent with the field's engagement-positive and factual-recall advantage patterns \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The design constraints documented in Limitation~3 preclude causal attribution; the evidence is descriptive, internally consistent, and theoretically interpretable within the framework of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}.

\subsection{Wartime education implications}

To the author's knowledge, this study represents the first documented implementation of gamified digital history education conducted under active armed conflict conditions, contributing to a context that empirical research has rarely examined \cite{Burde2017, Kirk2007}. The ``Education in Emergencies'' research tradition \cite{Burde2017} has identified three domains requiring empirical documentation in conflict-affected educational settings: infrastructure resilience mechanisms, motivational dynamics under sustained disruption, and learner trajectory divergence. This section addresses all three from a gamification-specific perspective, connecting the empirical findings of Section~\ref{sec:attendance} with the Ukrainian wartime education literature \cite{Martynets202428, Ovcharuk2023, Zamkova2023299}. The wartime context functions in the present analysis as an analytically informative moderating variable: by observing how the five structural layers of the course responded differently to the same infrastructure disruptions, the data reveal which educational mechanisms are genuinely wartime-resilient and which are wartime-fragile.

\textbf{Infrastructure disruptions: quantified and compared.} The 33.3\% infrastructure-attributable absence rate (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}) closely parallels findings from \cite{Semeniv2025}, who documented internet barriers in 37.5\% and electricity outages in 33.3\% of Ukrainian higher education participants during the same conflict period, confirming that infrastructure disruption constitutes a \emph{systemic} barrier \cite{Martynets202428, Vovchasta2024}. Notably, the instructor also experienced complete internet failures (December~8 and~26, 2024), documenting that wartime instability is a bilateral constraint. Infrastructure absences clustered in December 2024 and April 2025, corresponding to periods of intensified attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure \cite{Humeniuk2022113}; this external military targeting is why absence data cannot be treated as a standard methodological covariate.

A fourth wartime effect is rarely discussed: \emph{enrollment suspension}. The 71-day enrollment gap (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}) coincided precisely with the December infrastructure crisis and the onset of the participation trough, meaning that the trough received no compensating fresh-cohort effect. The five-layer resilience architecture (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}; Figure~\ref{fig:resilience-architecture}) was validated under these conditions through the principle of redundancy \cite{Tamim2011}: when the synchronous layer failed, the remaining four layers remained accessible~-- an architecture that emerged from pedagogical diversity rather than wartime planning \cite{Hlianenko2024, Zamkova2023299}.

\textbf{Wartime-mediated trajectory divergence.} Cross-referencing the four engagement modes (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}) with performance data reveals a consistent pattern: mandatory assessment structures sustained academic performance even when voluntary participation collapsed. The three most informative individual profiles~-- Nataliia~B.'s exclusively summative engagement with the highest MC mean among frequent absentees, Yana~M.'s 34.0\,pp mandatory--voluntary differential, and Oleksandra~Z.'s complete post-gamification disengagement (all detailed in Section~\ref{sec:attendance})~-- instantiate a fundamental tension in wartime distance education: mandatory summative structures are wartime-\emph{resilient}; voluntary engagement is wartime-\emph{fragile}, collapsing when cumulative disruption exceeds a threshold. The mandatory--voluntary participation ratio during the deepest trough is the cohort-level measure of this differential; the three individual profiles are its individual-level instantiation \cite{Martynets202428}.

\textbf{Gamification as the most wartime-resilient educational layer.} HistoryLikeGame's static architecture~-- requiring no server communication after initial load (Section~\ref{sec:platform-evolution})~-- was empirically validated during the April~13, 2025 session, the course's worst single-session disruption (7 absence reports from 5 students). Quiz links remained functional entirely offline, confirming that the gamification layer degrades gracefully to asynchronous mode. Unlike VR/AR implementations requiring high-bandwidth streaming and specialised hardware \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, 13Humpire2022, 15Slater2022}, HistoryLikeGame remained accessible throughout~-- inverting the common assumption that gamification requires \emph{more} technology than traditional instruction. \citeauthor{Zhumbei2025}~\cite{Zhumbei2025} found, in a direct parallel from Ukrainian wartime higher education, that gamified elements ``proved effective not only in learning but also in providing emotional support''~-- consistent with HistoryLikeGame's dual role \cite{Vovchasta2024}.

\textbf{Safety-first pedagogy as a documented institutional norm.} The instructor's February~2, 2025 safety communication (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}) documents a shift from mandatory synchronous attendance \cite{MON} to a safety-prioritised, asynchronous-default model \cite{UNESCO2023}. \cite{Burde2017} identify this repositioning as foundational for educational continuity in conflict zones. By decoupling access from synchronous presence, the policy enabled the ``sustained voluntary'' and ``exclusively summative'' engagement modes, serving as the structural precondition for the resilience patterns documented above \cite{Ovcharuk2023}.

\textbf{Community cohesion under wartime stress.} The 33 absence notifications constitute evidence of a behavioural norm: students consistently chose to inform the group rather than simply not appearing, even under severe disruptions. \cite{Burde2017} identify social cohesion as the primary predictor of educational continuity in conflict-affected settings; the notification behaviour provides micro-level evidence of this mechanism. The Viber group simultaneously served as a persistent asynchronous resource archive~-- quiz links, recordings, and platform links remained accessible under any connectivity condition \cite{Sailer2017, Hlianenko2024}.

\textbf{Implications for conflict-zone research design.} Standard research quality criteria~-- participation rates, completion rates, sample stability~-- become confounded variables rather than quality indicators in conflict-affected settings \cite{Burde2017, Kirk2007}. The mandatory--voluntary participation differential quantifies wartime's motivational reorganisation; the enrollment suspension reflects institutional capacity disruption; the infrastructure-attributable absence rate constitutes a primary context data point rather than merely a validity threat. The MRQS framework \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}, designed for controlled contexts, assigns high risk to small variable samples~-- but in a conflict zone, sample variability \emph{is} the data. The longitudinal design enabled the disaggregation of disruption types, the detection of resilience differentials between mandatory and voluntary structures, and the documentation of individual trajectory divergence under cumulative stress \cite{Burde2017, Kirk2007}.

\textit{Synthesis.} The wartime context produces three findings unavailable from standard gamification research. First, gamified digital history education \emph{can} sustain academically meaningful learning under active armed conflict, as demonstrated by the MC series and Nataliia~B.'s profile. Second, mandatory assessment structures are wartime's most resilient educational format, sustaining participation at multiples of the voluntary rate during the deepest disruption. Third, lightweight platform design (HistoryLikeGame: $<$30~KB per page, offline-capable) constitutes a conflict-zone \emph{inclusivity and accessibility requirement}. These findings respond to \citeauthor{Burde2017}'s~\cite{Burde2017} call for empirical documentation of educational delivery mechanisms under conflict conditions and extend the Ukrainian wartime education literature \cite{Martynets202428, Ovcharuk2023, Zhumbei2025} with gamification-specific data collected under active military operations.

\subsection{Limitations} \label{sec:limitations}

The following twelve limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. They are organised to distinguish methodological constraints intrinsic to single-group educational research designs from context-specific constraints arising from Ukrainian wartime conditions, as both categories are present and their interaction compounds the inferential challenges. For comparative context, the MRQS systematic review by \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} found that only 47.3\% of 74 gamification studies explicitly discussed limitations; each limitation below is quantified where possible and accompanied by the mitigating strategies applied.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Data coverage and pre-gamification granularity}: Response data are available for 36 of the 40 numbered seminars (Seminars~1--39, excluding Seminars~8, 34, 38, and~40) and all 7 modular controls, covering approximately 90\% of total instructional events. The pre-gamification data were recovered from multi-sheet XLSX files with aggregated per-student averages rather than raw item-level response logs, limiting granularity in that period: response patterns by question type, historical sub-period, or revision attempt are unavailable for Seminars~1--24. A structural asymmetry compounds this: 52.8\% of covered seminars (19 of 36) had fewer than 10 respondents (mean $N = 10.3$, median $N \approx 9.5$), meaning that individual-seminar scores carry high sampling uncertainty. Direct before-after comparison is further confounded by the simultaneous variation of topic difficulty, assessment format, and cohort composition \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. \textit{Mitigation:} all analyses report $N$ per seminar; claims about group-level trends are restricted to periods with $N \geq 5$.

\item \textbf{Small and variable sample sizes}: Respondents per seminar ranged from 1 to 27 (mean $N = 10.3$; median $N \approx 9.5$), with 52.8\% of seminars (19/36) falling below $N = 10$ and two seminars (Seminars~28 and~30) having only a single respondent each \cite{Borenstein2009, Korniienko2025mrqs}. The modular control series shows an analogous pattern: participation declined 74\% from MC2 ($N = 27$) to MC6 ($N = 7$). For reference, a two-group pre-post design requires $N \approx 64$ per group to achieve 80\% power at $d = 0.5$, $\alpha = .05$ \cite{Borenstein2009}; no single seminar approaches that threshold, and this study shares the underpowered pattern of the field: the MRQS corpus median was 33.5 participants, with only 9.5\% of studies reporting effect sizes \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. \textit{Mitigation:} only descriptive statistics are reported; inferential claims and causal language are systematically avoided.

\item \textbf{Absence of control group}: The single-group design precludes causal attribution of outcomes to the gamification intervention \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, creswell}. This is not anomalous: the MRQS review by \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} found that 68.9\% of 74 history gamification studies were single-group designs and only one study employed a fully randomised design. In the present context, a control group would have required withholding a free educational platform from enrolled NMT candidates during active wartime~-- ethically impermissible \cite{Reeves2003} and practically impossible given the open-enrollment, asynchronous-access model of the CDPO. \textit{Mitigation:} the study is framed as exploratory and descriptive throughout; causal language is avoided; modular controls with consistent format serve as the closest available within-series comparison.

\item \textbf{Self-selection and attrition bias}: Students who completed quizzes may differ systematically from those who did not, inflating apparent performance \cite{ary, creswell}. The scale of this bias can be partially quantified: of approximately 90 members identifiable in the Viber chat history, only 62 could be matched to any quiz response, leaving approximately 28 students ($\approx$31\%) with no individual-level academic record. Within the 62 matched students, 53 appear in pre-gamification data; of these, 26 (49\%) are absent from all post-gamification records~-- representing within-study attrition comparable to the field's overall dropout rates in distance education contexts. Paired pre-post data therefore exist for only approximately 27 students ($\approx$30\% of the Viber cohort), substantially restricting the interpretable base for longitudinal comparisons. High-performing students tended to participate across more seminars (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}), consistent with self-selection amplifying observed mean scores \cite{KoivistoHamari2019}. \textit{Mitigation:} trajectory analyses are reported separately for the full response sample and the matched-subsample; dropout is documented in Table~\ref{tab:individual-absence}.

\item \textbf{Rolling enrollment and unequal exposure}: New student enrollment comprised 23 documented joins across three phases: 17 early (October--November~2024), 4 mid-course (January--February~2025), and 2 late (March--May~2025). An institutional enrollment suspension of 71 days (November~13 -- January~22) coincided with the December~2024 infrastructure crisis peak. Six late enrollees (26\% of documented joins) had zero exposure to pre-gamification baseline conditions and are structurally ineligible for within-subject comparisons; students joining in April~2025 received only the final five to six weeks of course access, versus seven months for October enrollees. This heterogeneity creates exposure inequity that cannot be resolved post hoc without individual enrollment-date covariates. \textit{Mitigation:} enrollment phase is documented in Table~\ref{tab:individual-absence} and used as a stratifying variable in trajectory interpretation.

\item \textbf{Limited demographic and baseline data}: Participants are identified only by name form submitted in quiz responses; no individual-level background data were collected. Missing variables include: prior NMT score history, current school grade, geographic location within Ukraine, conflict-zone proximity, displacement status, household technology infrastructure (device type, internet quality), digital literacy level (directly relevant given the DigComp~2.0 framework invoked in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}), prior gamification experience, and psychological burden measures. The absence of conflict-proximity and displacement data is a wartime-specific gap: \cite{UNESCO2023} and \cite{Humeniuk2022113} identify these as primary moderators of educational engagement in conflict-affected settings that standardised demographic surveys do not capture. The importance of individual-difference variables as moderators of gamification effectiveness has been consistently emphasised by \citeauthor{KoivistoHamari2019}~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019} and \citeauthor{Hamari2014}~\cite{Hamari2014}. \textit{Mitigation:} absence of demographic data defines the ceiling on interpretive depth; future replications should incorporate a baseline demographic and digital-literacy survey.

\item \textbf{Name inconsistency and identity resolution limits}: Some students submitted responses under different name formats (e.g., ``Анастасія~К.'' vs.\ the diminutive ``Настя~К.''; ``Софія~В.'' in given-name-first vs.\ surname-first formats; ``Єлизавета'' vs.\ ``Ліза''). All recoverable cases were resolved through conservative manual matching using submission timing, Viber chat mentions, and participation patterns. However, the residual gap of approximately 28 students between Viber cohort size and matched individual records is partly attributable to irresolvable name ambiguity and anonymous submissions, reducing the effective $N$ for within-subject analyses. \textit{Mitigation:} matching decisions are documented in the analysis scripts; all ambiguous cases were left unlinked rather than speculatively assigned.

\item \textbf{Gamification platform usage data}: The HistoryLikeGame platform, comprising static HTML files designed for offline-accessible delivery, collects no user interaction data by architecture: session duration, module visit counts, quiz re-attempt frequency, and time-on-task are unavailable. Only 21.6\% of studies in the MRQS corpus used validated engagement measures \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}; this study shares the field's predominant pattern of relying on quiz score proxies rather than direct interaction logging. Platform engagement is therefore inferred from quiz completion records, score trajectories, and Viber chat mentions of specific modules. \textit{Mitigation:} the Viber chat log (Table~\ref{tab:design-principles-impl}) was systematically coded for platform mentions; quiz completion counts per student provide a behavioural engagement indicator, albeit indirect.

\item \textbf{Researcher-as-instructor dual role}: The researcher who designed the intervention also delivered all 38 course sessions and administered all assessments, creating two overlapping bias risks. First, \textit{observation bias}: the instructor's expectations may have influenced instructional emphasis or pacing in ways not captured by course materials, which could have advantaged or disadvantaged gamified relative to non-gamified sessions. Second, \textit{social desirability effects}: students may have perceived quiz responses as visible to the instructor and adjusted their effort accordingly, even though the Google Forms platform records responses under students' own names \cite{creswell}. Both risks are documented in educational action research and design-based research \cite{Reeves2003}. \textit{Mitigation:} assessment scoring was fully automated (no subjective grading by the instructor); the curriculum was externally fixed by the NMT examination format, limiting instructor discretion over what knowledge was assessed; the reproducible analysis pipeline is independently verifiable.

\item \textbf{Topic difficulty as an irresolvable confound}: The pre-gamification period (Seminars~1--24) covered early and medieval Ukrainian history, while the post-gamification period (Seminars~25--39) covered 20th-century history including World Wars, the Holodomor, the Soviet period, and the post-2014 conflict~-- content that generates lower NMT scores due to greater factual volume, more complex interpretive demands, and fewer visual source question types than medieval content. The 7.3\,pp aggregate score gap (70.7\% pre vs.\ 63.4\% post) consequently cannot be attributed to gamification, topic difficulty, or their interaction without a cross-topic control group \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. \textit{Mitigation:} modular controls (MC1--MC7), which span both periods with standardised format, serve as the primary difficulty-controlled comparison; the topic difficulty confound is flagged in all score-trend discussions; period-specific post-gamification seminars are analysed separately in Section~\ref{sec:formative}.

\item \textbf{Absence of a pre-course knowledge baseline}: No standardised pre-test was administered at course entry. Seminar~1 (N\,=\,18, mean 65.0\%) serves as a de facto group-level proxy baseline but does not constitute a validated individual pre-test: it confounds prior knowledge with response to Seminar~1's specific content and format. Without pre-test data, individual-level learning gain cannot be computed, and within-student knowledge growth can only be inferred from seminar-to-seminar progression for students with multiple entries. Only 39.2\% of gamification studies in the MRQS corpus used pre-post longitudinal measurement \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}; the absence of a validated entry measure is among the most consequential and most common methodological gaps in the field. \textit{Mitigation:} the modular control series (MC1--MC7, all 7 administrations) provides a within-course longitudinal record across standardised assessments; individual trajectory analysis (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) partially substitutes for pre-post design by documenting score change over time.

\item \textbf{Wartime conditions as unquantified moderating confound}: The study was conducted during active armed conflict (October~2024 -- May~2025), with documented air-raid alerts, infrastructure disruptions, and institutional suspension periods. Psychological stressors associated with wartime~-- chronic anxiety, grief, displacement, and safety uncertainty~-- function as moderating variables that influence both learning outcomes and participation behaviour \cite{UNESCO2023, Humeniuk2022113, Burde2017} but are unquantified in this dataset: no validated psychological well-being instrument was administered, displacement status was not recorded, and the severity of individual participants' wartime exposure cannot be reconstructed from available data. The infrastructure-attributable absence data (33.3\% of documented absence reasons: 24.2\% internet failures, 9.1\% power outages; Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}) capture only the logistical surface of wartime impact, not the deeper cognitive and motivational dimensions documented in the Ukrainian wartime education literature \cite{Martynets202428, Ovcharuk2023, Zamkova2023299}. The decision not to administer psychological instruments was deliberate: in the active-conflict context, such instruments carry ethical risks of re-traumatisation that were judged to outweigh measurement benefits. \textit{Mitigation:} the wartime context is documented longitudinally throughout Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}; absence typology is classified and quantified (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}); wartime disruption is treated as a structural explanatory variable rather than noise in all interpretations.

\end{enumerate}

These twelve limitations are reported in full accordance with PRISMA~2020 \cite{Page2021, Moher2009PRISMA} and CONSORT \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT} transparency standards. Items~1--5 and~8 are characteristic of the quality gap documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}; the four wartime-specific limitations (Items~6, 9~[partial], 11, and~12) have no established mitigating protocol in the gamification literature and are documented here following \citeauthor{Burde2017}~\cite{Burde2017} and \cite{Kirk2007}. Together, the twelve limitations rule out causal attribution, high statistical power, and demographic generalisability, while leaving intact the descriptive, longitudinal, and context-specific contributions. Each limitation is accompanied by a stated mitigation strategy and a quantitative bound where data permit. A formal MRQS self-assessment follows in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}.

The three ROBIS-aligned domains reveal a \textbf{structural asymmetry} that the aggregate MRQS score partially obscures. The two researcher-controlled domains~-- data collection and interpretation~-- are both rated low risk. Domain~2 (low risk, top 12.2\% of field) reflects the combination of NMT-format automatic scoring, standardised modular control format across all seven administrations, and a fully reproducible Python analysis pipeline with preserved intermediate outputs. Domain~3 (low risk, top 17.6\%) reflects the systematic avoidance of causal language, the foregrounding of the format-controlled MC comparison over the confounded seminar aggregate, and the twelve quantified limitations exceeding the 47.3\% field prevalence for limitation disclosure \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. By contrast, Domain~1~-- participant selection~-- is rated moderate risk (top 8.1\%), driven not by reporting deficiency but by a structural constraint: demographic representativeness is unavailable under active armed conflict regardless of sample size, because random or stratified sampling from a displacement-distributed population is impossible \cite{Burde2017}.

This asymmetry is the methodological signature of conflict-zone educational research: researchers can maximise what is controllable while remaining unable to overcome the structural constraints of the conflict environment. The MRQS/ROBIS framework correctly identifies \emph{where} the ceiling lies and \emph{where} the study exceeds field norms~-- a distinction that a single aggregate risk-of-bias label would collapse \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Whiting2016}.

Translating this profile into \citeauthor{GuyattGRADE2008}'s GRADE evidence certainty framework~\cite{GuyattGRADE2008}, the study supports ``low to moderate certainty'' for its descriptive claims. Certainty is limited by the single-group design, small $N$, and absence of causal identification; it is elevated by the 7-month longitudinal duration, the 7 standardised modular controls, the reproducible analysis pipeline, and the 12 quantified limitations bounding the inferential claims. In a field where the quality gap documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} places 83.8\% of published research at high overall risk of bias, this evidence profile approaches the methodological limits of a single-group conflict-zone cohort design \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsection{Comparison with the gamification evidence base}

The 74-study corpus reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} by \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}
provides a multi-dimensional reference frame against which the present study can be
positioned across five analytical dimensions: (1) implementation duration and longitudinal
depth; (2) sample size and assessment volume; (3) learning outcomes against the field's
effect-size distribution; (4) technology taxonomy and platform design; and (5) engagement
evidence and wartime attendance patterns. Each dimension reveals both where this study
falls within the field distribution and what it contributes to the gaps identified in
Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}~-- gaps that the MRQS analysis \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} has
shown to be systematic rather than incidental.

\textbf{Dimension~1~-- Implementation duration and longitudinal depth.}
The seven-month implementation (October~2024 -- May~2025) substantially exceeds the
field norm. Among the 17 studies in the 74-study corpus that reported specific intervention
durations, 8 (47.1\%) involved sessions of 20 minutes or less
\cite{11Ternblad2023115, 21Ghulamani2017, 1Barbatsis201159}, and only 4 documented
implementations exceeding one week \cite{55Squire2004, 46Stirling2022, 9Lin2018,
48Moketar2021}. The present study's 30-week span~-- 36 formative seminar sessions and
7 summative modular controls, totalling 43 distinct assessment events~-- makes it
among the longest documented gamification implementations in the history education corpus by both
calendar duration and number of assessment events.
This temporal depth enables trend analyses structurally impossible in brief interventions:
the identification of period-specific performance peaks and troughs
(Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}--\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post}),
the mapping of participation dynamics across 36 sessions (Section~\ref{sec:formative}),
and the separation of gamification effects from topic-difficulty confounds through the
modular control series (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}).
\citeauthor{KoivistoHamari2019}~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019} identified the short-term focus of most gamification research
as the field's most critical methodological gap; \cite{Hernandez2017} reached the same
conclusion in the adjacent software engineering gamification literature.
The 30-week implementation directly addresses this gap by providing the longitudinal
record that enables within-course temporal analysis.

\textbf{Dimension~2~-- Sample size and assessment volume.}
At $N\,{=}\,23$, the enrolled cohort falls below the corpus median of approximately 34
participants \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} and below the M2 threshold of 30.
However, sample size alone understates the study's empirical depth.
The 36 seminar sessions and 7 modular controls generate 358 individual data points ---
a volume substantially exceeding the empirical record of the single-group pre-post
designs that dominate the corpus (68.9\%; \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}), where
observable data typically amount to $2N \approx 46$--68 data points for comparable
cohort sizes \cite{16Chen201447, 6Czerwinski2018, 8Yu2014}.
The participation patterns documented in Section~\ref{sec:formative}~-- with the mandatory--voluntary differential confirmed by the modular controls (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats})~-- indicate that the post-gamification seminar decline reflects the shift to voluntary formative participation rather than cohort attrition.
This variable-$N$ structure, while imposing statistical constraints (Table~\ref{tab:mrqs-items}), reflects the authentic dynamics of open-enrollment wartime
education and provides a richer longitudinal picture than a fixed-panel design with the
same total $N$.

\textbf{Dimension~3~-- Learning outcomes against the field's effect-size distribution.}
The corpus reported positive learning effects in 85.1\% of studies ($d\,{=}\,0.28$--$0.61$
for factual knowledge; smaller for interpretive outcomes) \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012,
KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The format-controlled MC comparison (1.6\,pp gap;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) positions this study in the ``negligible to small effect''
region ($d\,{\lesssim}\,0.1$--$0.2$), consistent with the lower bound of the field
distribution and the study's exploratory design \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025mrqs}.
The factual-recall advantage documented in Section~\ref{sec:formative}~-- bounded topics
consistently outperforming comprehensive assessments, confirmed by both seminar and MC
data~-- reproduces the effect-size pattern documented across the corpus \cite{Clark2016,
Connolly2012} and in history-specific implementations \cite{53Perez2014, 54Gibson2007,
59Kee2014270}.

The HistoryLikeGame platform occupies the web-based category (13.5\%) but differs from

First, breadth of game mechanics: 13 distinct game types (quiz, falling-answer,

Second, resilience architecture: the HTML5-based design with no server requirements
and no installation enables operation under low-bandwidth and intermittent-connectivity

Third, browser-based AR: 10 A-Frame scenes using A-Frame and AR.js provide

The full system (v2: 62 modules; size details in Section~\ref{sec:platform-evolution}) represents one of the most extensively documented

The iterative design trajectory from v1 (December~2024: 2 functional quiz modules, 205\,KB)
to v2 (current: 62 modules, 13 game types) documents a practitioner design-based
research cycle \cite{Reeves2003} absent from the vast majority of reviewed studies,
where single-version platforms were evaluated without documented iteration.

\textbf{Dimension~5~-- Engagement evidence and wartime attendance patterns.}
The 74-study corpus reported positive engagement effects in 70.3\% of studies, most
frequently evidenced through self-reported motivation surveys or controlled pre-post
engagement scales \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Hamari2014, Sailer2017}.
The present study contributes a qualitatively different engagement record: longitudinal
and behavioural rather than self-reported, consisting of participation rates across
36 sessions, documented absence reasons from the Viber group chat
(Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}), and platform architecture as engagement infrastructure.

The 33 documented absences (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}) reveal a wartime participation structure qualitatively different from the stable institutional settings assumed by most reviewed studies: the majority of absences were attributable to external causes unrelated to academic engagement \cite{Burde2017, UNESCO2023}. In a stable-context study, this profile would signal high Domain~1 risk; in the wartime context, it instead documents the resilience mechanisms that enabled the core cohort to maintain modular control participation throughout.

\citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}'s self-determination theory framework~\cite{RyanDeci2000} predicts that the HistoryLikeGame's autonomy, competence, and relatedness design features generate sustained engagement over long interventions~-- a prediction confirmed by \citeauthor{vanRoyZaman2019}~\cite{vanRoyZaman2019} as the strongest predictor of positive long-term gamification outcomes. The observed participation pattern~-- stable MC attendance despite declining voluntary seminar participation~-- is consistent with this prediction and with the substitution hypothesis (Section~\ref{sec:future-research})~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019}.

\textbf{Definitional and theoretical contribution.}
The study applies the \citeauthor{Deterding2011}~\cite{Deterding2011} taxonomy~-- present in only 14.9\% of the corpus \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~-- to distinguish three simultaneously operational components: \emph{structural gamification}, \emph{content gamification}, and \emph{game-based learning}. This tri-component operationalisation demonstrates that real-world implementations rarely map onto a single category \cite{SEABORN201514, Zhonggen2019, Unkelos-Shpigel2016}. The theoretical grounding (\citeauthor{Werbach2012}'s DMC pyramid~\cite{Werbach2012} and DigComp~2.0~\cite{Vuorikari2016}; Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}) aligns gamification design with history-specific competence outcomes across all five DigComp~2.0 areas.

The five-dimensional comparison reveals the same structural asymmetry identified in the MRQS/ROBIS analysis: structurally constrained on dimensions most visible to conventional quality metrics (sample size, control group, effect sizes) while exceeding field norms on researcher-controlled dimensions (longitudinal depth, technology breadth, definitional clarity, theoretical grounding, transparent reporting). This asymmetry reflects the quality gap documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} and illustrates the practical limits of methodological rigour within a conflict-zone single-group design.

\subsection{Implications for digital history methodology}

\label{sec:dh-methodology}

The empirical record presented above instantiates the digital history
pedagogy framework developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}, with implications
for digital history methodology that extend beyond gamification to the broader challenge
of designing technology-enhanced history education under adverse infrastructure conditions.
Building on the nascent digital history pedagogy evidence base (6/46 studies; Section~\ref{sec:dh-review}), the present study extends the empirical record in five ways.

\textbf{The four-type taxonomy fully instantiated.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} proposed four digital history approach types: archival-analytical \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, Crymble2021}, creative-productive \cite{guiliano}, collaborative-communicative \cite{Siemens2004, Wright-Maley2018}, and immersive-experiential \cite{Connolly2012, Qian2016, Prensky2001}. The present study instantiates all four simultaneously: Google Forms quizzes operationalise archival-analytical historical thinking \cite{Wineburg2001, Seixas2013}; the HistoryLikeGame platform instantiates the immersive-experiential approach; the Viber group enacts collaborative-communicative practice \cite{Siemens2004}; and creative quiz naming (Section~\ref{sec:quiz-naming}) represents the creative-productive approach. This four-type integration is among the few documented instances of all approaches operating simultaneously within a single course. Crucially, these approaches rest on a foundational layer of factual knowledge reinforcement at Bloom's \textit{Remember} and \textit{Understand} levels \cite{Anderson2001}, addressing the learning losses documented by PISA~2022 \cite{OECDPISA2023} and NMT results \cite{UCEQA2024, UCEQA2025} before students engage with higher-order tasks (Section~\ref{subsec:dh-intersection}).

\textbf{Constructivist and connectivist grounding in the observed data.}
Vygotsky's zone of proximal development (ZPD) \cite{Vygotsky1978} predicts that learning is maximised when tasks are scaffolded at the boundary of current competence. The quiz structure operationalised this: single-period seminars provided tractable challenges near the upper ZPD boundary, while comprehensive assessments exceeded it, producing the floor-effect pattern documented in Section~\ref{sec:formative}. \citeauthor{Tilak2023}~\cite{Tilak2023} demonstrated analogous ZPD dynamics in a digital social studies curriculum; the present pattern replicates this mechanism in a Ukrainian NMT context.
\citeauthor{Siemens2004}'s~\cite{Siemens2004} connectivist model situates learning in networks rather than individual minds. The three-wave enrollment trajectory (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}) reflects exactly the open-network pattern that connectivist design
accommodates: knowledge previously distributed across the Viber group, Google Drive
materials, and HistoryLikeGame modules was accessible to late entrants without
institutional penalty, enabling meaningful participation from first contact.

\textbf{TPACK model applied to design decisions.}
The TPACK framework (Section~\ref{subsec:dh-frameworks}; \cite{Mishra2006, Koehler2013})
is operationalised here as follows.
CK encompasses 9 historical periods, NMT curriculum alignment,
and periodisation-specific competences; PK encompasses the formative--summative
differential (voluntary seminars vs.\ mandatory MCs), the safety-first communication
culture, and rolling-enrollment accommodation; TK encompasses the HistoryLikeGame
static HTML architecture (offline-capable; Section~\ref{sec:platform-evolution}),
the 5-layer resilience design (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}), and Google Forms
quiz deployment.
The creative quiz naming convention (Section~\ref{sec:quiz-naming}) is the canonical
TPACK product: it transforms assessment format (PK) by applying thematic game-world
naming (TK) to historical content (CK), producing a qualitatively new pedagogical
object~-- a quiz that functions simultaneously as a formative assessment instrument
and a narrative gamification element.

\textbf{DigComp 2.0 with empirical anchors.}
Section~\ref{sec:digcomp} aligned the course design with all five DigComp~2.0 competence areas \cite{Vuorikari2016, Titova2025cte}; the empirical data now provide quantitative anchors for each: \emph{information literacy} through 156 Google Forms quizzes requiring structured evaluation; \emph{communication} through the Viber group and Zoom channels across 30 weeks; \emph{digital content creation} through 62 HistoryLikeGame modules and 10 A-Frame AR scenes; \emph{safety} through the documented asynchronous-first protocol; and \emph{problem solving} through 13 game-type mechanics mapping to ascending Bloom's taxonomy levels.

\textbf{Resilience architecture as a replicable model.}
The five-layer architecture (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}, Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology})
is a directly transferable practical contribution to digital history methodology in
conflict-affected and infrastructure-poor settings \cite{Burde2017, UNESCO2023}.
The architecture's effectiveness is documented empirically: despite the external-cause
absence pattern (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}), the compulsory assessment layer
remained accessible across all 7 modular controls spanning 7 months, confirming
the resilience documented by \citeauthor{Martynets202428}~\cite{Martynets202428} and \cite{Zamkova2023299}.
The HistoryLikeGame layer's lightweight architecture (Section~\ref{sec:platform-evolution}; offline-capable after initial load) is a directly
transferable design principle for any digital history implementation in low-bandwidth
or politically disrupted educational environments \cite{Hlianenko2024}.

These five contributions collectively define a \emph{digital history methodology model}: a multi-approach, theoretically grounded, infrastructure-adaptive system treating digital tools as the primary medium for historical learning, assessment, and community \cite{Ertmer1999, Humeniuk2022113, Nunez202329}. The contribution lies not in individual components but in their simultaneous, documented integration under active armed conflict, where the five-layer resilience architecture makes the difference between continuity and interruption~\cite{Korniienko2025aredu}.

\subsection{Future research directions}

\label{sec:future-research}

The findings, limitations, and field-positioning analysis (Sections~3.4.5--3.4.6)
converge on a structured research agenda organised in three priority tiers:
Tier~1 addresses foundational gaps that constrain interpretation of the present results;
Tier~2 builds directly on the longitudinal record generated here;
Tier~3 addresses scaling and cross-context generalisation.

\paragraph{Tier~1~-- Foundational gaps (required before causal claims can be made).}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Anonymous learning analytics (HistoryLikeGame v3).}
The substitution hypothesis~-- that seminar participation declined because students shifted to asynchronous HistoryLikeGame practice~-- cannot be evaluated without platform usage data. Version~3 should add lightweight, privacy-preserving analytics via browser \texttt{localStorage} (module~ID, time-on-task, attempt count, score), with opt-in export. This preserves the zero-registration, offline-capable design while providing evidence to determine whether the participation trough represents disengagement or modality shift \cite{KoivistoHamari2019, Sailer2017}.

\item \textbf{Quasi-experimental controlled comparison.}
The recommended next implementation is a quasi-experimental design across two parallel CDPO sections (2026--2027): one receiving the full gamification intervention, one the identical curriculum without gamification. Course-section-level assignment controls for the three confounds identified in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} (instructor, difficulty, disruption). Pre-registration would satisfy MRQS criterion M8, currently unmet in 68.9\% of the corpus \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Schulz2010CONSORT, creswell}.

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Tier~2~-- Building from the longitudinal record.}

\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}

\item \textbf{NMT/ZNO score follow-up.}
Linking CDPO enrollment records to May~2025 NMT results would provide a direct downstream outcome measure currently absent from the corpus \cite{Korniienko2025cte}~-- a direct link between formative MC participation and summative national examination performance. The analytical target is the correlation between individual MC mean score and NMT History score ($N\,{=}\,10$--$15$), feasible within the existing KDPU institutional framework \cite{Page2021}.

\item \textbf{Design-Based Research iteration (v3 platform cycle).}
The v1$\to$v2 trajectory constitutes the initial two DBR cycles \cite{Reeves2003}; v3 should be theory-driven by the findings. Specific targets: (a)~built-in session analytics (enabling Tier~1); (b)~multiplayer mode addressing the voluntary-seminar participation trough; (c)~a teacher dashboard surfacing substitution and disengagement patterns; (d)~adaptive difficulty adjustment instantiating ZPD scaffolding~\cite{Vygotsky1978} computationally.

\item \textbf{Phenomenographic study of wartime student experience.}
The 24.2\% unspecified absence category (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}) is
the study's most opaque data point: these 8 absences may reflect displacement,
psychological stress, military service, or other circumstances that participants
were unwilling or unable to document in a group chat.
A phenomenographic interview study \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Hamari2014} with
8--10 consenting former participants (15--20 minutes each) would capture the
variation in experience of gamified distance learning under conditions of armed
conflict~-- a dimension that quantitative assessment data cannot access.
\citeauthor{Ditlmann2025}~\cite{Ditlmann2025} demonstrated that digital history participation mobilises
psychological responses (symbolic justice, intergroup relations improvement)
beyond the learning outcomes typically measured; the present study's cohort,
operating in an active warzone, likely generated analogous psychological dynamics
that interview data could document \cite{Zamkova2023299, Martynets202428}.

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Tier~3~-- Scaling and cross-context generalisation.}

\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{5}

\item \textbf{Multi-institution samples.}
Scaling to 3--5 CDPO institutions across Ukrainian oblasts (frontline, mid-country, western rear) would address the M2 threshold \cite{Borenstein2009}, enable geographic subgroup analysis relevant to wartime education equity, and test whether the resilience architecture generalises across disruption profiles \cite{UNESCO2023, Striuk_2022}.

\item \textbf{Cross-domain NMT subjects.}
The NMT assessment infrastructure is operationally transferable to Mathematics or Language NMT preparation \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}. A parallel implementation would test whether the factual-recall advantage (bounded topics outperforming comprehensive
assessments) is a domain-general feature of Google Forms gamification or
specific to history's periodisation structure \cite{Wright-Maley2018, Qian2016}.

\item \textbf{LLM-assisted ongoing quality monitoring.}
The LLM reliability profile established by \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}~-- substantial agreement for factual extraction, slight for risk-of-bias~-- supports a hybrid monitoring architecture: automated pre-screening for factual descriptors with human review reserved for bias assessment. This would transform the one-time quality audit into ongoing field surveillance \cite{TamimMMRQG2021}.

\end{enumerate}

The three-tier research agenda above~-- from foundational measurement fixes through
longitudinal follow-up to scaling~-- provides a cumulative programme in which each
tier's outputs directly enable the next.
The five principal findings from the empirical and analytical work of this chapter
are synthesised in Section~\ref{sec:ch3-conclusions}.

\section{Conclusions to Chapter~\ref{ch:results}} \label{sec:ch3-conclusions}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} has presented results and discussion across four analytical layers:
baseline and formative assessment data (Sections~\ref{sec:baseline}--\ref{sec:formative}),
attendance and wartime context analysis (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}),
and a seven-subsection discussion that positions the findings within the 74-study
gamification evidence base and the nascent digital history pedagogy literature.
Five principal findings emerge.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Longitudinal performance patterns: factual-recall advantage confirmed.}
The 36-seminar series produced a stable factual-recall advantage: bounded single-period assessments consistently outperformed comprehensive multi-period assessments by $\approx$30 percentage points (Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}--\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post}). The MC series traces a U-shaped arc from the MC2 baseline peak through the MC5 trough to partial MC7 recovery (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}). Within-group heterogeneity persisted throughout, and the four trajectory clusters (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) coexisted across both course periods, consistent with the meta-analytic finding \cite{SailerHomner2020, BaiHewHuang2020} that individual learner characteristics moderate gamification effects more strongly than design features alone.

\item \textbf{Behavioural substitution pattern: bifurcation between voluntary and compulsory engagement.}
The post-gamification period exhibited a structural bifurcation: voluntary seminar participation declined sharply while compulsory MC participation remained stable across all 7 controls (Section~\ref{sec:formative}). This is consistent with a substitution hypothesis~-- asynchronous HistoryLikeGame practice in lieu of synchronous seminars~-- but unconfirmed without platform analytics (Section~\ref{sec:future-research}). The engagement floor (73.9\% post-gamification assessment participation) indicates that the core cohort remained connected despite wartime disruptions.

\item \textbf{Wartime resilience: five-layer architecture maintained compulsory participation.}
Despite the predominantly external-cause absence pattern (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}), the MC series maintained participation across all 7 events, confirming that the asynchronous-first, minimal-bandwidth five-layer architecture (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}) achieved its design objective \cite{Burde2017, UNESCO2023}. Compulsory assessment sustained across 7 months of active armed conflict constitutes the study's most directly replicable practical contribution to digital history methodology in conflict-zone settings \cite{Martynets202428, Zamkova2023299, Hlianenko2024}.

\item \textbf{Methodological positioning: transparent constraint documentation places study in top quality tier.}
The MRQS/ROBIS analysis (Table~\ref{tab:mrqs-items}) positioned the study among the top 16.2\% of the 74-study corpus, satisfying the six MRQS criteria most frequently absent (definition, theory, sample size, limitations, transparency, hypotheses). The format-controlled MC comparison yields a 1.6\,pp learning outcome signal~-- positioned in the ``negligible to small effect'' region at the lower bound of the field's effect-size distribution \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\item \textbf{Digital history methodology model: all four approach types and all five DigComp areas instantiated.}
The course simultaneously instantiates all four digital history approach types
from the Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} taxonomy: archival-analytical (NMT-format
quizzes), collaborative-communicative (Viber group interaction, networked open
enrollment across 3 waves), immersive-experiential (62 game modules, 10 A-Frame
AR scenes, 13 game-type formats), and creative-productive (thematic quiz naming,
historical narrative integration through period structure).
All five DigComp~2.0 competence areas \cite{Vuorikari2016} are empirically
anchored (Section~\ref{sec:dh-methodology}).
The HistoryLikeGame DBR trajectory (v1$\,\to\,$v2: 23$\,\to\,$62 modules;
1$\,\to\,$13 game types; 205\,KB$\,\to\,$869\,KB) documents one of the few iterative
practitioner design-based research cycles \cite{Reeves2003} in the digital history
pedagogy literature, embedding the study within the ``pedagogical turn'' identified
in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} as the field's most recent and consequential development.

\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Responses to secondary research questions.}
(SRQ1) The Vygotsky--Siemens--TPACK--DigComp~2.0 multi-framework model (Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}) provides conceptual scaffolding empirically evidenced in the data.
(SRQ2) Transparent documentation constitutes a methodological contribution independent of effect-size estimates \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.
(SRQ3) Wartime conditions are documented through the 5-layer resilience architecture and the absence record \cite{UNESCO2023, Martynets202428}.
(SRQ4) The six design principles (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}) were operationalised and evidenced in Table~\ref{tab:design-principles-impl}.

\textbf{Status of the research hypothesis.}
The primary hypothesis receives partial support: engagement evidence (73.9\% post-gamification participation, stable MC~$N$) is consistent with the field's 70.3\% engagement-positive rate \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, but in the absence of self-report data, mechanisms remain behavioural rather than motivationally confirmed. Learning outcome evidence is limited to the 1.6\,pp format-controlled MC gap, which cannot support causal attribution~-- addressed by the Tier~1 research agenda above. These findings establish the empirical and methodological groundwork for the synthesis developed in the Conclusion, where the five findings are integrated with the Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} theoretical framework and the Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} methodological contributions.
