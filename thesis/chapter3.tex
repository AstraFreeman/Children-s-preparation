\chpt{Results and discussion} \label{ch:results}

\section{Baseline stage results} \label{sec:baseline}

\subsection{Enrollment dynamics and cohort composition}

The course officially began on October 12, 2024, following the creation of the Viber communication group on October 1, 2024. Analysis of the enrollment timeline (Figure~\ref{fig:enrollment}) reveals three distinct enrollment phases:

\textbf{Phase 1: Rapid initial formation (October 1--25, 2024).} Eleven students joined within the first three weeks, forming the core group. This rapid enrollment coincided with the announcement of instructor assignments and schedule information through the CDPO administrative channel.

\textbf{Phase 2: Gradual expansion (November 1 -- November 13, 2024).} Six additional students enrolled during November, bringing the total to 17. These mid-course joiners had access to all previously shared materials through the Google Drive folder.

\textbf{Phase 3: Late enrollment (January 22 -- May 11, 2025).} Six more students joined during the second half of the course, including the final participant on April 11, 2025 --- just two days before the last recorded session. This late enrollment pattern reflects the flexible nature of CDPO preparatory courses.

Enrollment rate analysis reveals a striking temporal asymmetry. Phases~1 and~2 both exhibited rates of approximately 0.46~joins per day (11~students over 24~days and 6~students over 13~days, respectively). The 70-day gap between Phase~2 and Phase~3 (November~13, 2024 -- January~22, 2025) coincides with the winter months when infrastructure disruptions were most severe (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}). Phase~3's drastically lower rate of 0.08~joins per day (6~students over 80~days) reflects both the advanced stage of the course and the cumulative effect of wartime barriers on new recruitment. The full enrollment breakdown by phase is presented in Table~\ref{tab:enrollment-detail}.

The final enrollment of 23 unique participants is consistent with the typical size of CDPO preparatory course groups~\cite{KSPU}. The rolling enrollment pattern, while creating analytical challenges (different students were exposed to different portions of the course), accurately reflects the real-world conditions of pre-university preparatory education in Ukraine~\cite{MON}. This ecological validity, though limiting internal validity, is characteristic of design-based research in educational settings~\cite{Reeves2003, creswell, Sanina2020}.

The cohort size (N=23, below the corpus median of N=33.5; Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}) is contextualised in the research design discussion (Section~\ref{sec:organisation}). Importantly, rolling enrollment creates differential exposure: Phase~1 students had potential access to all 40~seminars and 7~modular controls, while Phase~3 late joiners participated in as few as 1--15~seminars. This differential shapes the individual trajectory analysis in Section~\ref{sec:formative} and motivates the within-subject analytical approach described in Section~\ref{sec:data-analysis}.

\subsection{Baseline assessment profile}

The first three seminars and the first modular control (MC1) constitute the baseline assessment window, spanning Phase~1 (October~12 -- November~3, 2024). These data establish the cohort's starting performance before the course methodology fully stabilised and before the gamification intervention was introduced.

\textbf{Phase~1 seminar characterisation.} The three Phase~1 seminars yielded a mean score of 66.7\% with an average participation of N$\approx$14.3 (as reported in Section~\ref{sec:organisation}). Individual seminar statistics are presented in Table~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}; here we highlight the distributional features relevant to baseline interpretation. Within-group variability was substantial: standard deviations ranged from 20.0~pp (Seminar~3) to 28.9~pp (Seminar~2), indicating that the cohort was far from homogeneous in prior knowledge. Participation declined from N=18 (Seminar~1) to N=8 (Seminar~3) even within Phase~1, demonstrating that not all enrolled students completed every assessment --- a pattern that persisted throughout the course (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}).

The mean--median relationship shifted across seminars: in Seminars~1 and~3, the median exceeded the mean (67.2\% vs.\ 65.0\% and 74.5\% vs.\ 70.7\%, respectively), indicating left-skewed distributions where a few low performers pulled the mean downward. In Seminar~2, the reverse pattern held (mean 64.3\% vs.\ median 53.3\%), suggesting right-skew. This instability in distributional shape reflects a heterogeneous cohort with no stable performance profile in the early weeks --- a finding consistent with open-enrollment preparatory courses where participants enter with widely varying prior knowledge~\cite{CohenManionMorrison2018}. The high within-group variability aligns with findings from the systematic review (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}), where individual differences were rarely analysed despite being pervasive~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\textbf{MC1 as first summative checkpoint.} The first modular control (MC1, November~3, N=19, mean 77.2\%, median 88.2\%, SD=28.4~pp) provides the first standardised summative measure. Participation (N=19) exceeded the Phase~1 seminar mean of 14.3, likely because the modular control carried greater perceived weight than weekly seminars. The mean--median gap of 11~percentage points signals a left-skewed distribution: a cluster of strong performers scoring near 90\% alongside a weaker tail pulling the mean to 77.2\%. This bimodal tendency is consistent with the heterogeneity observed in the seminars.

MC1 preceded the gamification intervention by 28~days, establishing a clean pre-intervention summative benchmark. The full modular control results across all seven assessments are presented in Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats} (Section~\ref{sec:formative}).

\textbf{Interpretation.} Three baseline characteristics condition all subsequent analyses in this chapter: (i)~high within-group heterogeneity (SDs of 20--29~pp), consistent with the open-enrollment nature of CDPO courses where no prior-knowledge screening is applied; (ii)~a bimodal tendency (high median, lower mean) suggesting a prepared core of students alongside less-prepared participants; and (iii)~moderate and variable participation (N=8--19 out of 11~enrolled), indicating voluntary engagement patterns even in the earliest phase. These characteristics motivate the individual trajectory approach adopted in Section~\ref{sec:formative}, which tracks within-subject change over time rather than relying on aggregate means that mask the underlying heterogeneity~\cite{ary, CohenManionMorrison2018}.

\subsection{Pre-gamification reference period}

Phase~2 (5~seminars, November~10 -- November~30, 2024) represents the fully operational pre-gamification course. By this point the instructional format, assessment approach, and instructor--student interaction rhythm had been established. As defined in Section~\ref{sec:organisation}, Phase~2 serves as the primary pre-gamification baseline for comparison with the post-gamification Phase~4.

\textbf{Phase~2 aggregate characterisation.} Mean participation rose to N$\approx$15.8 (from 14.3 in Phase~1), reflecting the expanding cohort, while mean performance improved to 71.3\% --- a gain of +4.6~pp over Phase~1, consistent with the expected instruction effect as students became familiar with the assessment format and accumulated content knowledge. Standard deviations remained high (14.5--25.8~pp across Seminars~4--7 and~9), confirming that within-group heterogeneity persisted beyond the initial weeks. Individual seminar values are reported in Table~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}.

\textbf{MC2 as summative reference.} The second modular control (MC2, December~1, N=27, mean 84.6\%, median 88.4\%, SD=23.8~pp) was the highest-participation assessment in the entire course and was administered on the same day gamification was introduced via the HistoryLikeGame platform~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Participation (N=27) exceeded the cumulative enrollment of 17 at that date, suggesting that some respondents accessed the Google Forms link through CDPO administrative channels beyond the core Viber group. The performance improvement from MC1 (77.2\%) to MC2 (84.6\%) --- a gain of +7.4~pp --- demonstrates measurable learning gains during the pre-gamification instructional period. MC2 establishes the performance ceiling against which later modular control scores should be interpreted (Section~\ref{sec:formative}; Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}).

\textbf{Interpretive context.} The upward baseline trajectory --- from Phase~1 to Phase~2 in seminar means (66.7\% $\to$ 71.3\%) and from MC1 to MC2 in summative assessments (77.2\% $\to$ 84.6\%) --- is a critical interpretive context for the remainder of this chapter. Any post-gamification decline in scores must be weighed against: (a)~regression toward the mean from the MC2 peak; (b)~increasing content difficulty as the curriculum progressed to later historical periods (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); and (c)~the confounding effects of wartime infrastructure disruptions on participation and performance (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}). The gamification intervention was introduced at the baseline performance peak, not at a trough --- a circumstance that shapes the expected direction of post-intervention change~\cite{Hamari2014, DichevDicheva2017}.

\subsection{Introductory assessment and baseline synthesis}

An introductory test was administered on October~13, 2024 (the first day of instruction) via Google Forms, intended as a baseline diagnostic assessment of participants' prior knowledge of Ukrainian history~\cite{Hattie2009, Clark2016}. Response data for this test are not available in the dataset, as the test preceded the systematic response collection that began with Seminar~25. Consequently, Phase~1 seminar performance (mean 66.7\%) serves as the de facto baseline proxy. While not a pure pre-instruction diagnostic --- the seminars assessed material from the first weeks of instruction --- it provides the earliest available quantitative evidence. This limitation is not uncommon: the systematic review found that only 31.1\% of gamification studies in history education included pre-intervention baseline measures (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base})~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\section{Formative stage results} \label{sec:formative}

The following 36 seminars and 7 modular controls --- among the most comprehensive formative records in the history-education gamification corpus --- trace how the Phase~1--2 baseline evolved through the gamification period. Seminar-level statistics, individual trajectories, and modular controls are presented in turn.

Three seminars require interpretive caveats before the aggregate patterns are examined. Seminar~13 (N\,=\,2, max\_possible\,=\,131 points) employed an anomalous format with substantially more items than other seminars; its statistics should be treated as indicative rather than directly comparable (see Section~\ref{sec:scoring} for scoring methodology). Seminars~28 and~30 each attracted only a single respondent; these data points represent individual performance rather than group statistics, and standard deviations are undefined. These three seminars are retained in the tables for completeness but should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The post-gamification period contains two structurally distinct sub-periods. Seminars~25--30 (January--March 2025) experienced severely depressed participation (N\,=\,6, 3, 2, 1, 3, 1; mean N\,=\,2.7), coinciding with the peak of winter wartime disruptions documented in Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context} and the delayed arrival of Phase~3 enrollees. Seminars~31--39 (March--May 2025) saw a marked recovery (N\,=\,12, 3, 7, 3, 5, 15, 12; mean N\,=\,8.1), driven by both NMT proximity and the integration of Phase~3 participants into the assessment cycle (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}). The aggregate post-gamification mean of N\,=\,5.6 therefore conflates a deep trough with a recovery phase, and any interpretation of post-gamification trends must account for this structural break.

The full-course data reveal three notable tendencies in participation, scoring, and progression.

The number of respondents varied substantially across the course, from 1 (Seminars 28 and 30) to 27 (Seminar 17). In the pre-gamification period, participation was generally higher (mean N=12.9, range 2--27), reflecting the larger active group in the early course stages. In the post-gamification period, participation varied more widely (mean N=5.6, range 1--15). The partial recovery of participation in the final seminars (N=15 in Seminar 37 and N=12 in Seminar 39) may reflect increased engagement as the NMT examination date approached (May 14, 2025), a pattern consistent with findings in the gamification engagement literature \cite{Hamari2014, KoivistoHamari2019}.

Mean scores ranged from 33.3\% (Seminar 30) to 94.2\% (Seminar 13) across the full course. Because later seminars covered more complex modern and contemporary history, employed comprehensive NMT-format assessments, drew on smaller respondent pools, and were affected by rolling enrollment, any comparison between the pre-gamification period (mean 70.7\%, Seminars 1--24) and the post-gamification period (mean 63.4\%, Seminars 25--39) should be considered alongside these confounding factors rather than attributed to the presence or absence of gamification \cite{ary, Cohen2005, Torgerson202237}.

An important confound is the distinction between topic-specific and comprehensive NMT-format assessments. Topic-specific seminars covering delimited historical periods (e.g., Seminars~31--32: 80.8--83.3\%) yielded substantially higher scores than comprehensive NMT-format assessments spanning multiple periods (e.g., Seminars~36--37: 50.7--59.6\%). This pattern is consistent with the content-specificity of historical knowledge \cite{Wineburg2001, Reisman2012} and the progressive difficulty principle embedded in the assessment design (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rationale}). The format distinction must therefore be considered when interpreting score trajectories.

The trend in mean and median scores across all 36 available seminars is displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:progression}, spanning the complete course from Seminar 1 to Seminar 39. The vertical dashed line marks the introduction of gamification at Seminar 24/25. The pre-gamification seminars show a generally stable performance pattern with some variation by topic, while the post-gamification seminars show wider variation, with a U-shaped pattern in the later seminars (high scores in 31--35, a dip in 36--37, recovery in 39) consistent with the progressive difficulty design principle \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

The SD columns now included in Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre} and~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post} quantify the within-seminar dispersion visible in Figure~\ref{fig:distribution}. Pre-gamification SDs ranged from 4.9~pp (Seminar~13, N\,=\,2) to 37.4~pp (Seminar~23, N\,=\,11); post-gamification SDs ranged from 9.0~pp (Seminar~32, N\,=\,3) to 27.8~pp (Seminar~25, N\,=\,6). The persistence of heterogeneity across both periods --- rather than convergence --- is consistent with the baseline variation documented in Section~\ref{sec:baseline} and suggests that the gamification intervention did not substantially alter the group's dispersion structure.

In aggregate, the eight profiled students fall into four performance clusters: consistent mastery ($>$79\% mean) accounts for 3 of 8 students (37.5\%: Viktoriia~S., Alina~B., Mariia~S.); topic-variable performance (75--80\%) for 2 of 8 (25.0\%: Anastasiia~K., Veronika~H.); persistent struggle ($<$50\%) for 2 of 8 (25.0\%: Olha~K., Violetta~P.); and progressive decline for 1 of 8 (12.5\%: Anastasiia~N.). This unequal distribution mirrors the baseline heterogeneity documented in Section~\ref{sec:baseline} and is consistent with the high within-group variability ($I^2 > 80\%$) reported in gamification meta-analyses \cite{SailerHomner2020, BaiHewHuang2020}. The qualitative profiles underlying these clusters are detailed below.

The trajectory data reveal several distinct performance profiles, consistent with the individual differences observed in gamified learning environments \cite{Qian2016, Lampropoulos2024, Schafer2017}:

Viktoriia S. exemplified consistent mastery, maintaining scores between 77.3\% and 93.5\% across 9 seminars (the maximum number of data points for any student). Her mean of 86.9\% indicates sustained engagement throughout the second half of the course, consistent with the mastery learning paradigm \cite{Hattie2009}. Alina B., while appearing in only 3 seminars, achieved uniformly high scores (82.1\%--100.0\%, mean 90.6\%), and Mariia S. similarly maintained strong performance (75.0\%--87.5\%, mean 79.5\%) across her 3 appearances. Such consistent high performance in gamified environments has been associated with intrinsic motivation and sustained engagement \cite{Sailer2017, Hamari2014}.

A different pattern characterised students whose competence was strong but whose scores varied by topic. Anastasiia K. (4 seminars, mean 79.7\%) and Veronika H. (4 seminars, mean 75.1\%) showed wider score ranges. Veronika H.'s trajectory is particularly instructive: she scored 100\% on Seminar 31, dropped to 45.7\% on the more difficult Seminar 36, recovered to 86.7\% on Seminar 37 (an NMT-format comprehensive test), and finished at 67.8\% on Seminar 39. This pattern suggests that topic-specific difficulty rather than overall competence drives score variation for this group, a finding consistent with the content-specificity of historical knowledge assessments \cite{Reisman2012, MonteSano2012, Ethier202311}.

Can struggling students improve through gamified practice? Olha K. (5 seminars, mean 47.8\%) showed a distinctive pattern: a low initial score (30.8\% on Seminar 31), a strong improvement to 81.4\% (Seminar 33), followed by a decline in later comprehensive assessments (43.5\%--47.8\%). Similarly, Violetta P. (5 seminars, mean 47.0\%) showed high variability (23.3\%--73.9\%), with her best performance on a seminar focused on a specific historical period (Seminar 36: 73.9\%) and her weakest on the comprehensive NMT-format test (Seminar 37: 23.3\%). The gap between topic-specific and comprehensive assessment performance aligns with research on transfer of learning in gamified environments \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012, Shiue2017460}.

Not all trajectories pointed upward. Anastasiia N. appeared in 4 seminars with a concerning downward trend: 53.3\% (Seminar 26) $\rightarrow$ 69.2\% (Seminar 31) $\rightarrow$ 32.6\% (Seminar 33) $\rightarrow$ 29.9\% (Seminar 39). Although her Seminar 31 score represented a temporary improvement, the overall trajectory shows declining performance, possibly related to increasing course difficulty or competing demands. Such declining trajectories may reflect motivational challenges, as the gamification literature notes that initial novelty effects can wane over extended implementations \cite{Hamari2014, KoivistoHamari2019, DichevDicheva2017}.

One student merits particular attention. Danylo T. submitted 7 responses to Seminar 37 (the 2016 NMT comprehensive test), with scores progressively improving from 18.9\% to 94.4\% (17, 24, 24, 38, 44, 59, and 85 out of 90 points). This pattern of repeated attempts with steady improvement illustrates how the immediate feedback mechanism of Google Forms enabled self-regulated learning \cite{Bonwell1991}: the student used each attempt as a formative learning opportunity --- a pattern consistent with the testing effect, whereby retrieval practice with feedback enhances long-term retention more effectively than re-study alone \cite{RoedigerButler2011, Dunlosky2013}. This behaviour exemplifies the mastery learning approach \cite{Hattie2009}, in which iterative practice with task-level feedback (Sections~\ref{sec:scoring} and~\ref{sec:principle-grounding}) drives progressive competence development \cite{HattieTimperley2007}. On the subsequent Seminar 39, this student scored 82.8\%, demonstrating retention of the knowledge gained through iterative practice -- a finding consistent with the effectiveness of immediate feedback in gamified learning contexts \cite{Sailer2017, 17Manzanares2019, Zhonggen2019}.

Of the 17 named participants in the post-gamification period, 8 appeared in 3 or more seminars (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}), while 9 appeared in only 1--2 datasets. The low appearance rate of many students may reflect: (a) irregular attendance at synchronous sessions, (b) inconsistent provision of names in quiz forms (the name field was optional), or (c) late enrollment limiting the number of available assessments. Such variable participation rates are a common challenge in distance education research \cite{UNESCO2023} and in gamification studies conducted in naturalistic settings \cite{Deterding2011, Hung201757}. Of the 23 enrolled students, 6 never appear in any post-gamification assessment; the trajectory analysis thus covers 17 of 23 (73.9\%) of the cohort, with detailed profiling possible for only 8 of 23 (34.8\%). This coverage rate exceeds the 31.1\% baseline-reporting rate found in the systematic review of gamification assessment quality \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} but falls short of ideal standards identified in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}, constraining generalisability and motivating cautious interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Three features of the modular control data warrant discussion. First, participation declined steadily from 27 respondents (MC~2) to 7--9 (MCs~5--7), reflecting both the general attrition common in extended distance courses and the increasing impact of wartime disruptions in the later months \cite{UNESCO2023}. Second, mean scores were highest in MC~2 (84.6\%) and MC~4 (83.6\%), with a notable dip in MC~5 (63.8\%) that coincides with the transition to 20th-century content covering complex political events (World War~II, Soviet period, independence movement); the recovery in MCs~6 and 7 (72.1\% and 74.3\%) suggests adaptation to this content difficulty. Third, the consistently high standard deviations (14.84--28.39) confirm the substantial individual variation observed in the seminar data, consistent with the heterogeneous group composition.

The MC~5 dip merits closer examination because it aligns with concurrent seminar evidence. MC~5 (63.8\%, March 30) coincided with Seminars 36 (59.6\%) and 37 (50.7\%) --- convergent low scores across both formative and summative instruments for the same content period, confirming that the decline reflects content difficulty rather than a format artefact. Conversely, MC~4 (83.6\%, February 23) aligned with Seminars 31 (80.8\%) and 32 (83.3\%), where both instruments produced convergent high scores. The MC~5$\rightarrow$MC~7 recovery of +10.5 percentage points over six weeks (63.8\%$\rightarrow$74.3\%) parallels the seminar recovery from Seminar 37 to 39 (+12.8~pp), reinforcing the progressive difficulty design principle (Section~\ref{sec:principle-grounding}). Nevertheless, MC~7 (74.3\%) did not recover to the MC~2 ceiling of 84.6\% established in the baseline period (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-summary}), suggesting that cumulative content difficulty imposed a lasting performance constraint.

The modular controls were emphasised as ``mandatory for all'' (``ОБОВ'ЯЗКОВО до виконання'' / ``Проходження обов'язкове''), distinguishing them from the optional seminar quizzes. This distinction between mandatory summative and optional formative assessments aligns with assessment design principles in educational research \cite{Hattie2009, Bonwell1991}. The mandatory nature is reflected in quantifiably higher participation: post-gamification modular controls (MCs~3--7) attracted a mean of N\,=\,10.6 respondents, compared with N\,=\,5.6 for post-gamification seminars --- an 89\% difference. The contrast was most pronounced during the Seminar 25--30 participation trough (mean N\,=\,2.7), when MC~3 (N\,=\,15) and MC~4 (N\,=\,13) drew 4.8--5.6 times more respondents than concurrent seminars. This pattern indicates that the mandatory framing effectively counteracted the voluntary attrition observed in formative assessments (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rationale}), consistent with the role of accountability structures in sustaining engagement \cite{Hattie2009}.


Three converging patterns emerge from the formative data. First, the participation structural break --- a severe trough in Seminars 25--30 (mean N\,=\,2.7) followed by recovery in Seminars 31--39 (mean N\,=\,8.1) --- reveals that aggregate post-gamification statistics conflate two distinct sub-periods with different dynamics. Second, individual trajectories range from consistent mastery (37.5\% of profiled students) to progressive decline (12.5\%), mirroring the baseline heterogeneity documented in Section~\ref{sec:baseline}. Third, convergent evidence from modular controls and concurrent seminars confirms that late-period score declines reflect content difficulty rather than assessment format artefacts. These performance patterns must be understood against the backdrop of the wartime conditions that shaped participation throughout the course.

\section{Attendance and wartime context analysis} \label{sec:attendance}

The participation structural break identified in Section~\ref{sec:formative} --- a trough in Seminars 25--30 (mean N\,=\,2.7) followed by partial recovery in Seminars 31--39 (mean N\,=\,8.1) --- raises the question of mechanism: what drove the abrupt decline and subsequent recovery? The 33 absence reports preserved in the course chat log, combined with enrollment records and individual trajectory data, provide qualitative and quantitative insight into the disruption dynamics underlying those aggregate shifts. This section examines three intersecting dimensions: the classification and temporal distribution of reported absences; the intersection of enrollment dynamics, individual absence patterns, and performance trajectories; and the course timeline as an integrative temporal frame. The infrastructure conditions and resilience design principles that shaped these dynamics are addressed in Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context} of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\subsection{Wartime context}

The absence data provide a vivid picture of education under wartime conditions \cite{UNESCO2023, Humeniuk2022113}. Three categories of absences are directly attributable to the conflict:

\textbf{Infrastructure disruptions (33.3\%).} Internet problems (24.2\%) and power outages (9.1\%) together account for one-third of all reported absences. These are characteristic of wartime conditions in Kryvyi Rih, where missile and drone attacks frequently damage energy infrastructure, causing both immediate blackouts and cascading internet disruptions.

\textit{Cross-referencing absences with performance.} Comparing the absence data with the individual trajectory data (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) reveals four distinct profiles.

\textbf{Oleksandra Z.} (7 absences: internet, family, unspecified) achieved MC1\,=\,79.4\% and MC2\,=\,83.7\% and appeared in Seminar~17 (60.5\%) during the pre-gamification phase, but recorded zero post-gamification seminar or assessment appearances. Her escalating absences --- infrastructure-driven in December and family-attributed in February--March --- accompanied a complete withdrawal from post-gamification learning activities.

\textbf{Nataliia B.} (5 absences, predominantly infrastructure) recorded zero seminar appearances across the entire dataset, yet achieved MC1\,=\,100.0\%, MC3\,=\,97.5\%, MC4\,=\,96.3\%, and MC5\,=\,72.3\%, for an MC mean of 91.5\% --- the highest among all frequently absent students. Her MC5 dip to 72.3\% parallels the cohort-wide MC5 mean of 63.8\% (Section~\ref{sec:formative}), confirming that the decline reflects content difficulty rather than disengagement. This profile demonstrates that mandatory summative structures sustain academic performance even under severe infrastructure disruption \cite{Martynets202428}.

\textbf{Yana M.} (5 absences: internet, family, power, unspecified) presents the most analytically informative profile. She appeared in four seminars (Seminar~16: 44.0\%, Seminar~21: 23.5\%, Seminar~24: 64.3\%, Seminar~31: 69.2\%; seminar mean 50.2\%) and five modular controls (MC1: 91.2\%, MC2: 86.0\%, MC3: 82.5\%, MC4: 64.8\%, MC6: 96.7\%; MC mean 84.2\%). The resulting mandatory--voluntary differential of 34.0 percentage points is the largest among all profiled students and provides individual-level confirmation of the cohort-level pattern documented in Section~\ref{sec:formative} \cite{Zamkova2023299}.

\textbf{Violetta P.} (3 absences: internet, late arrival, unspecified) appeared in five post-gamification seminars with a mean score of 47.0\%, demonstrating sustained voluntary engagement despite synchronous interruptions. Her continued seminar participation contrasts with Oleksandra Z.'s post-gamification withdrawal, suggesting that lower cumulative absence frequency and the availability of gamified assessment alternatives together supported continued engagement.

\textit{Engagement typology under wartime disruption.} Four engagement modes can be distinguished among frequent absentees, each shaped differently by wartime conditions. Nataliia B. represents \emph{exclusively summative} engagement: zero seminar appearances yet the highest MC mean (91.5\%) among frequent absentees, demonstrating that mandatory assessment structures sustain academic performance even when synchronous attendance is impossible \cite{Hattie2009}. Yana M. exhibits an \emph{MC-dominant with sporadic seminars} pattern (MC mean 84.2\%, seminar mean 50.2\%), reflecting the cohort-level mandatory--voluntary differential at the individual level. Violetta P. demonstrates \emph{sustained voluntary} engagement across five post-gamification seminars despite synchronous interruptions. Oleksandra Z. exhibits \emph{post-gamification disengagement}, with all activity confined to the pre-gamification period. All three ``present'' modes were enabled by the asynchronous delivery infrastructure described in Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}, which decoupled participation from synchronous attendance.

The concentration of absences among a subset of students, combined with their continued enrollment and quiz participation, suggests that these students maintained engagement with the course through asynchronous materials despite being unable to attend synchronous sessions regularly. This finding aligns with research on the role of digital technologies in maintaining educational continuity during crises \cite{UNESCO2023, Striuk_2022}.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of findings in context of the systematic reviews}

The results presented above --- seminar performance across 36 sessions, modular control trajectories, individual learning profiles, and attendance patterns under wartime conditions --- can be organised along three interpretive threads, each connected to a distinct component of the theoretical and evidentiary infrastructure established in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}. The first thread concerns \emph{engagement and its motivational mechanisms}: the 70.3\% engagement-positive rate from the gamification review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} and the 100\% engagement-positive rate from the digital history review \cite{Korniienko2025cte} benchmark this study's participation patterns, while \citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}'s self-determination theory~\cite{RyanDeci2000} and \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}'s flow theory~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} furnish the motivational interpretation of the mandatory--voluntary differential and the score variance distribution. The second thread concerns \emph{learning outcomes and within-group heterogeneity}: the 51.4\%/37.8\%/10.8\% outcome distribution and the \(d\,=\,0.28\)--\(0.61\) effect-size range from the gamification review (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}) frame the factual/interpretive score differential and the trajectory cluster analysis. The third thread concerns \emph{methodological positioning}: the MRQS analysis \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} --- mean quality 4.31/10, 83.8\% high risk of bias, definitional clarity decoupled from rigour --- defines the quality landscape within which this study's design choices and acknowledged limitations are situated. The pre-gamification versus post-gamification comparison, the design principles implementation, and the iterative mastery mechanism are treated in Section~3.4.2; wartime resilience implications are treated in Section~3.4.3.

\textit{Engagement outcomes and motivational mechanisms.} The most consistently supported finding in the gamification literature is a positive effect on engagement: 70.3\% of the 74 reviewed studies reported this outcome \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, replicating the meta-analytic consensus established by \citeauthor{Hamari2014}~\cite{Hamari2014} and \citeauthor{Sailer2017}'s experimental need-satisfaction model~\cite{Sailer2017}. The digital history review \citeauthor{Korniienko2025cte}~\cite{Korniienko2025cte} found an even stronger signal, with 100\% of the 6 reviewed studies reporting positive engagement effects, suggesting that gamification in a history-specific disciplinary context may be particularly well-matched to sustained motivation. The present study is broadly consistent with this pattern: 17 of 23 enrolled students (73.9\%) appeared in at least one post-gamification assessment, a rate that is notable given the wartime infrastructure disruptions documented in Section~\ref{sec:attendance}. The mandatory--voluntary participation differential provides a more analytically precise engagement indicator: post-gamification modular controls attracted a mean of N\,=\,10.6 respondents versus N\,=\,5.6 for seminars, an 89\% differential that intensified during the Seminars~25--30 trough (seminar N\,=\,2.7 versus MC3--4 N\,=\,13--15, a 5.6-fold ratio), confirming that assessment stakes rather than platform availability drove participation under conditions of environmental stress.

\citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}'s self-determination theory~\cite{RyanDeci2000} provides the most coherent motivational framework for this differential. The mandatory MC framing (explicitly labelled ``ОБОВ'ЯЗКОВО до виконання'' in the chat) operationalises \emph{controlled motivation}: participation driven by external obligation. Voluntary seminar engagement operationalises \emph{autonomous motivation}: participation driven by intrinsic interest or self-regulated learning goals. The four engagement modes identified in Section~\ref{sec:attendance} map onto this spectrum: Nataliia~B.'s exclusively summative profile and Yana~M.'s MC-dominant pattern reflect the accountability function of controlled motivation structures, while Violetta~P.'s sustained voluntary seminar engagement across five post-gamification sessions represents autonomous motivation persisting under wartime disruption. Oleksandra~Z.'s post-gamification disengagement, by contrast, illustrates a failure mode where neither motivational orientation was sufficient to sustain engagement after accumulated disruption. \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}'s flow theory~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} offers a complementary lens for the wide score variance (33.3\%--94.2\% across seminars): the progressive difficulty structure was designed to maintain challenge--skill balance, but the substantial heterogeneity of preparation levels (within-group SD 20--29\,pp) meant that the same assessment represented an optimal challenge for some students and either excessive difficulty or insufficient challenge for others.

\textit{Learning outcomes and the factual--interpretive differential.} The systematic review \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} found that 51.4\% of reviewed studies reported positive effects on learning outcomes, 37.8\% found no significant difference, and 10.8\% reported negative or mixed results. Meta-analytically estimated effect sizes ranged from \(d\,=\,0.28\) to \(d\,=\,0.61\), with stronger effects consistently observed for factual recall tasks than for interpretive or synthetic understanding (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}). A complementary meta-analysis by \citeauthor{BaiHewHuang2020}~\cite{BaiHewHuang2020} likewise found that gamification benefits were more pronounced for recall-level outcomes than for higher-order cognitive tasks. The present study replicates this factual/interpretive differential within a single course: period-specific seminars assessing discrete historical content (Seminars~31--32: means 80.8\%--83.3\%) systematically outperformed comprehensive NMT-format assessments requiring synthesis across multiple periods (Seminars~36--37: means 50.7\%--59.6\%). The post-gamification mean score (63.4\%) is lower than the pre-gamification mean (70.7\%), but this comparison is substantially confounded by content difficulty escalation and assessment format change (Section~\ref{sec:formative}); the comprehensive-format score pattern places this study in alignment with the 37.8\% of reviewed studies that found no significant advantage on broader learning outcomes.

A persistent feature of the data that the reviewed literature rarely examines is within-group heterogeneity \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. In the present study, within-group standard deviations remained large throughout both periods: 20--29\,pp across pre-gamification and post-gamification seminars alike. This heterogeneity did not decrease after gamification was introduced --- the distribution of outcomes did not become more uniform. The \citeauthor{SailerHomner2020}~\cite{SailerHomner2020} meta-analysis on the gamification of learning confirms that effect sizes are substantially moderated by individual learner characteristics, including prior motivation and task-specific self-efficacy --- factors that gamification design alone cannot neutralise. The four post-gamification trajectory clusters (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) capture this distributional reality: consistent mastery (37.5\%), topic-variable performance (25\%), persistent struggle (25\%), and progressive decline (12.5\%) coexisted throughout the course. This distribution underscores a conclusion consistent across both the present data and the meta-analytic literature: gamification demonstrably supports continued engagement and factual-level learning but does not uniformly elevate all learners --- and aggregate mean scores systematically obscure this distributional reality \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}.

\textit{Longitudinal depth and what extended observation makes visible.} A structural characteristic that distinguishes this study from the reviewed literature is its duration. The systematic review \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} identified that only 4 of the 74 reviewed studies employed implementations lasting more than one week; the majority involved interventions of 20 minutes or less. The seven-month implementation period (October 2024 -- May 2025) creates analytical affordances that brief interventions cannot provide. Three phenomena in the present dataset depend on extended temporal observation and would be invisible in a standard single-session or pre--post design. First, the participation structural break --- a severe trough in Seminars~25--30 (mean N\,=\,2.7) followed by recovery in Seminars~31--39 (mean N\,=\,8.1, Section~\ref{sec:formative}) --- is a dynamic pattern requiring at least three months of data to detect. Second, the four post-gamification trajectory clusters (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) --- consistent mastery, topic-variable, persistent struggle, and progressive decline --- require multi-point observation to distinguish from one another; a two-time-point design collapses all four profiles into a single, misleading pre--post contrast. Third, the modular control progression across seven distinct summative assessments (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) reveals a U-shaped performance arc --- peak at MC2 (84.6\%), dip at MC5 (63.8\%), partial recovery at MC7 (74.3\%) --- that a single post-assessment design would capture only as a terminal data point. The iterative mastery mechanism enabled by the immediate-feedback design principle, which manifests as progressive within-student score improvement across repeated attempts, similarly requires an extended course to confirm as durable rather than transient. The 7-month duration is therefore both an ecological feature of the study context and a methodological contribution: it demonstrates what longitudinal commitment makes analytically visible in gamification research.

\textit{Methodological positioning.} The MRQS assessment \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}) established a quantitative baseline of methodological quality across the 74 reviewed studies: a mean score of 4.31/10 (SD\,=\,1.93), with 83.8\% of studies carrying a high overall risk of bias. Specific MRQS criteria most frequently absent include explicit effect-size reporting (9.5\% compliance), explicit gamification definition (14.9\%), validated measurement instruments (21.6\%), and sample representativeness (0\% compliance). These gaps echo broader concerns about research quality in educational technology \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012} and gamification research specifically \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}. A particularly consequential structural finding was that definitional clarity and methodological rigour are \emph{decoupled}: studies that clearly defined gamification did not, as a rule, employ stronger designs \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

The present study was designed to demonstrate that both can be achieved simultaneously. Gamification is operationally defined as the integration of game design elements (creative quiz naming, interactive web-based exercises, immediate feedback, progressive difficulty) into the non-game NMT preparation context, following the canonical definition by \citeauthor{Deterding2011}~\cite{Deterding2011}. Methodological transparency is achieved through full preservation of all data sources (chat log, response files, platform source code, analysis pipeline); explicit acknowledgement of design limitations (single-group, no random assignment, N\,=\,23, rolling enrollment, confounded pre--post comparison); application of descriptive analysis without unsupported inferential claims; and reporting of within-group variability alongside means. On the MRQS instrument, this study satisfies the following criteria that were most frequently absent in the reviewed corpus: explicit gamification definition, theoretical framework, sample size reported, limitations discussed, and data transparency. While the absence of a control group and the below-threshold sample size constrain causal interpretation, the overall risk-of-bias profile places this study among the top 16.2\% of the reviewed literature --- positioning that reflects not the absence of limitations but the transparent documentation of them.

\subsection{Pre-gamification vs post-gamification comparison}

The aggregate pre-post comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:pre-post}) shows a mean score 7.3\,pp higher in the pre-gamification period (70.7\% vs.\ 63.4\%) and substantially higher average participation (N\,=\,12.9 vs.\ N\,=\,5.6). These aggregate figures are not reliable indicators of gamification effectiveness: three structurally distinct sources of variance operate jointly on the post-gamification period, and each can be quantified from the data in Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre} and~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post}. The figure should be read alongside the disaggregation below.

\centering

\textit{Confound~1: Assessment format shift.} All 24 pre-gamification seminars (Seminars~1--24) used period-specific formats covering a single delimited historical unit. The post-gamification period introduced comprehensive NMT-format assessments spanning multiple periods simultaneously (Seminars~36, 37, 39), which are structurally more demanding: they require cross-period synthesis and recall rather than within-unit retrieval \cite{Wineburg2001}. When post-gamification scores are disaggregated by format, the contrast is unambiguous: the five period-specific post-gamification seminars (Seminars~25, 31--33, 35) yield a mean of 78.8\% --- \emph{8.1\,pp above} the pre-gamification mean of 70.7\% --- while the three comprehensive NMT-format seminars (Seminars~36, 37, 39) yield a mean of 57.9\%. The aggregate post-gamification mean of 63.4\% is therefore a weighted average of these two structurally incommensurable assessment types, not a measure of gamification's effect on learning \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}.

\textit{Confound~2: Content difficulty escalation.} The post-gamification period covers 20th-century and contemporary Ukrainian history --- the most demanding portion of the NMT curriculum, characterised by greater event density and interpretive complexity than the ancient-to-early-modern content assessed pre-gamification. The modular control sequence provides within-course difficulty calibration on a consistent format: MC1 (November 2024, medieval and Cossack content, N\,=\,19) achieved a group mean of 77.2\%, while MC5 (March 2025, 20th-century content, N\,=\,9) dropped to 63.8\% --- a 13.4\,pp decline on assessments with \emph{identical format} and the same gamification infrastructure fully in place. This within-MC gradient confirms that content complexity is the primary driver of the downward pressure on late-course scores.

\textit{Confound~3: Participation composition and a controlled comparator.} Post-gamification voluntary seminar participation (mean N\,=\,5.6) reflects a wartime-disrupted, self-selected subset, while pre-gamification participation (mean N\,=\,12.9) reflects a more stable, obligation-framed early cohort (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}). The modular controls maintained consistent accountability framing throughout the course and sustained substantially higher participation (post-gamification MC mean N\,=\,10.6). Using the MC sequence as a controlled comparator, the pre-post gap narrows markedly: MC1 (pre-gamification, 77.2\%) versus MC2--MC7 (post-gamification, mean 75.6\%) yields a difference of only 1.6\,pp --- versus the 7.3\,pp gap in the confounded seminar data. When assessment format and participation mode are held approximately constant, the apparent performance decline largely disappears. The single-group design without random assignment still precludes causal attribution; these findings are descriptive and interpretive rather than causal \cite{creswell, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

The modular control participation data provide the clearest controlled engagement signal in the dataset. MC2 --- launched on December~1, 2024, the same day HistoryLikeGame was introduced --- attracted N\,=\,27 respondents at a group mean of 84.6\%: the highest participation and the highest score of any assessment across the entire seven-month course. This confluence of peak participation and peak performance at the precise moment of gamification introduction is the most direct positive signal in the dataset, consistent with the engagement-positive benchmark from the systematic review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The MC2 datapoint also grounds a critical observation: the post-gamification period did not begin with reduced performance --- it began with the course's best result. The full motivational interpretation of engagement patterns, including the mandatory--voluntary differential, the four engagement modes, and the SDT framework, is developed in Section~3.4.1; the present section focuses on what MC participation implies for reading the pre-post comparison.

The six design principles identified in the systematic review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} were implemented in this study, drawing on evidence from both the gamification literature \cite{Sailer2017, Qian2016} and digital history pedagogy \cite{Korniienko2025cte}. The implementation of all six principles simultaneously distinguishes this study from the majority of gamification implementations reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review} \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, where studies typically employed only 1--3 principles. For example, \cite{1Barbatsis201159} focused primarily on narrative integration, while \citeauthor{16Chen201447}~\cite{16Chen201447} emphasised social elements, and \cite{55Squire2004} combined historical authenticity with narrative integration. The comprehensive approach in the present study reflects the implementation guidelines developed in Section~\ref{sec:digcomp} \cite{Titova2025cte} and aligned with DigComp~2.0 \cite{Vuorikari2016}.

Beyond documenting that all six principles were implemented, the empirical record contains observable traces of each principle's activation. \textit{Historical authenticity and progressive difficulty} are jointly confirmed by the content-specificity of score patterns: period-specific post-gamification seminars outperformed the pre-gamification mean, while the MC trajectory's U-shaped arc (high at MC1--MC2, trough at MC5, partial recovery at MC6--MC7) maps directly onto the curriculum difficulty gradient embedded in 40 lectures. \textit{Immediate feedback} is evidenced at both group and individual levels: at the group level, MC2's peak performance coincides with the first occasion when HistoryLikeGame's colour-coded immediate-feedback mechanism was simultaneously available; at the individual level, the iterative mastery case (discussed below) documents the mechanism with seven-attempt resolution. \textit{Social elements} are evidenced by the accountability-driven MC participation during the Seminars~25--30 trough: when voluntary seminar attendance collapsed to a mean N\,=\,2.7, the group-framed mandatory controls (MC3--MC4) attracted N\,=\,13--15 --- a 5.6-fold ratio demonstrating the social obligation function operating under infrastructure stress \cite{Sailer2017}. \textit{Narrative integration} is evidenced qualitatively by the Viber chat record: students' acknowledgements of quiz naming themes (Greek mythology, gemstones, trees, minerals) confirm sustained affective engagement with the platform's identity layer across the post-gamification period. \textit{Multiple modalities} are evidenced by the structural heterogeneity of the 62-module catalogue (Table~\ref{tab:game-modules}) --- quizzes, drag-drop, Three.js, falling-answer, and canvas modules represent distinct interaction affordances --- consistent with \citeauthor{Qian2016}'s~\cite{Qian2016} finding that modality diversity in game-based learning correlates with broader student engagement profiles.

The modular control trajectory (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) provides the most interpretable longitudinal performance signal in the dataset because MC assessments maintain consistent format and scoring rubric across all seven administrations. The trajectory describes a U-shaped arc: MC1 (77.2\%) $\to$ MC2 peak (84.6\%) $\to$ MC5 trough (63.8\%) $\to$ partial recovery at MC6--MC7 (72.1\%--74.3\%). Three features of this arc carry interpretive weight. First, the MC1-to-MC2 transition represents the only controlled pre-to-post comparison available: consistent format, comparable N (19 vs.\ 27), and a boundary straddling the gamification introduction date; it shows a positive shift of +7.4\,pp, the \emph{opposite direction} from the confounded seminar comparison. Second, the MC5 trough (63.8\%) aligns precisely with the most demanding content unit --- 20th-century Ukrainian history (World Wars, Soviet period, independence process) --- and the partial recovery in MC6--MC7 is consistent with a difficulty--consolidation dynamic rather than sustained gamification failure. Third, the post-gamification MC average (MC2--MC7: 75.6\%) differs from MC1 (77.2\%) by only 1.6\,pp, reinforcing the disaggregation conclusion: when format is controlled, there is no material performance decline across the course. This pattern of differential effectiveness by assessment type and content difficulty aligns with findings from \cite{8Yu2014} and \cite{6Czerwinski2018} in history-specific gamification, and with the systematic review's conclusion that effect sizes were stronger for factual recall tasks than for interpretive performance \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}).

The case of Danylo~T. (Section~\ref{sec:formative}) provides the richest individual-level documentation of how the immediate-feedback principle generates learning progression within an extended course. Across seven attempts at Seminar~37 (the 2016 NMT comprehensive test), scores progressed from 18.9\% to 94.4\%, with each submission producing item-level corrective feedback and an immediate percentage display. Two theoretically grounded mechanisms explain this trajectory. First, the \emph{testing effect} \cite{RoedigerButler2011, Dunlosky2013}: retrieval practice with immediate corrective feedback substantially outperforms re-study on delayed retention tests, and each of Danylo~T.'s re-attempts constituted a targeted retrieval episode followed by immediate error correction --- the operationalisation of this mechanism within the gamified assessment design. Second, the \emph{formative feedback loop} described by \citeauthor{HattieTimperley2007}~\cite{HattieTimperley2007}: effective feedback reduces the gap between current performance and a learning goal through task-level information about what was incorrect, then motivates a reattempt; the seven-attempt sequence instantiates this cycle iterated to mastery threshold, realising the mastery learning model described by \citeauthor{Hattie2009}~\cite{Hattie2009}. The durability of this learning is confirmed by Seminar~39 (82.8\%), a different NMT-format assessment administered after the seven-attempt cycle: Danylo~T.'s retention score is 43.9\,pp above his first Seminar~37 attempt and 17.9\,pp above the Seminar~39 group mean (63.5\%), consistent with the long-term retention advantage predicted by retrieval-practice theory \cite{RoedigerButler2011}. The mechanism is not frequently discussed in the gamification literature \cite{DichevDicheva2017}: similar repeated-attempt designs appear in \cite{5kazanidis2018alpha} and \cite{50Lye2013}, but neither provides multi-session retention evidence. The present study's seven-month duration enables confirmation of the mechanism as durable rather than transient --- an analytical affordance that brief interventions cannot provide.

\textit{Synthesis.} The pre-gamification versus post-gamification comparison, properly disaggregated, supports a more substantiated conclusion than the aggregate mean contrast. The 7.3\,pp aggregate score decline is primarily explained by assessment format shift and content difficulty escalation: period-specific post-gamification seminars outperformed the pre-gamification mean by 8.1\,pp, and the controlled MC comparator shows a gap of only 1.6\,pp versus the 7.3\,pp apparent seminar decline. MC2's peak performance (84.6\%, N\,=\,27) at the moment of HistoryLikeGame's introduction is the study's most direct positive signal. The six design principles leave observable traces across participation, score, and individual behaviour data: the MC trajectory maps onto the progressive difficulty structure; immediate feedback enables iterative mastery at the individual level and correlates with MC2's group-level peak; social accountability sustains MC participation when voluntary seminar attendance collapses; narrative integration generates documentable affective engagement in the chat record. The iterative mastery mechanism is confirmed as durable through a delayed retention result 43.9\,pp above the initial attempt. These findings align with the 51.4\% of reviewed studies reporting positive learning outcomes and the 70.3\% reporting positive engagement \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, while placing the comprehensive-format score pattern within the 37.8\% reporting no significant advantage on broader outcome measures. No causal attribution is warranted given the single-group, no-control design; the evidence is descriptive, internally consistent, and theoretically interpretable within the framework established in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}.

\subsection{Wartime education implications}

To the authors' knowledge, this study represents the first documented implementation of gamified digital history education conducted under active armed conflict conditions, contributing to a context that empirical research has rarely examined \cite{Burde2017, Kirk2007}. The ``Education in Emergencies'' research tradition \cite{Burde2017} has identified three domains requiring empirical documentation in conflict-affected educational settings: infrastructure resilience mechanisms, motivational dynamics under sustained disruption, and learner trajectory divergence. This section addresses all three from a gamification-specific perspective, connecting the empirical findings of Section~\ref{sec:attendance} with the Ukrainian wartime education literature \cite{Martynets202428, Ovcharuk2023, Zamkova2023299}. The wartime context functions in the present analysis not as a limitation to be acknowledged and set aside but as an analytically informative moderating variable: by observing how the five structural layers of the course responded differently to the same infrastructure disruptions, the data reveal which educational mechanisms are genuinely wartime-resilient and which are wartime-fragile.

\textbf{Infrastructure disruptions: quantified and compared.} The course attendance log documents three categories of infrastructure failure. \emph{Student-level failures}: internet problems (8 reports, 24.2\%) and power outages (3 reports, 9.1\%) account for 11 of 33 absence notifications (33.3\%, Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}). This rate closely parallels findings from \cite{Semeniv2025}, who documented internet connectivity barriers in 37.5\% and electricity outages in 33.3\% of Ukrainian higher education participants during the same conflict period --- a cross-institutional confirmation that infrastructure disruption constitutes a \emph{systemic} rather than idiosyncratic barrier in Ukrainian wartime education \cite{Martynets202428, Vovchasta2024}. \emph{Instructor-level failures}: the instructor experienced complete internet failure on December~8, 2024 (``internet collapsed completely''), requiring session reduction; total internet outage on December~26, 2024 forced the entire lecture to asynchronous recorded delivery. Instructor-level connectivity failures document that wartime infrastructure instability is a bilateral constraint affecting the entire educational interaction, not a student-specific vulnerability. \emph{Temporal clustering}: infrastructure absences were concentrated in December 2024 (4 student reports + 2 instructor failures) and April 2025 (4 infrastructure reports from 3 students in the final seminar session), corresponding to documented periods of intensified Russian attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure \cite{Humeniuk2022113}. Disruption clustering is not random noise but a consequence of external military targeting: this is the primary reason absence data cannot be treated as a standard methodological covariate.

A fourth wartime effect is rarely discussed in the distance learning literature: \emph{enrollment suspension}. New student enrollment ceased entirely for 71 days between November~13, 2024 and January~22, 2025, coinciding precisely with the December 2024 infrastructure crisis peak and the onset of the participation trough (Seminars~25--30, mean N\,=\,2.7). This enrollment gap is invisible in cross-sectional data but causally significant: the absence of new enrollments during the highest-disruption period meant that the participation trough received no compensating fresh-cohort effect. The five-layer resilience architecture (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}; Figure~\ref{fig:resilience-architecture}) was validated under these conditions through the principle of redundancy \cite{Tamim2011}: when the synchronous layer failed (instructor or student connectivity loss), the communication, content, assessment, and gamification layers remained accessible. Critically, this architecture was not designed as a wartime strategy but emerged from pedagogical diversity --- an empirically important precedent for emergency remote teaching design \cite{Hlianenko2024, Zamkova2023299}.

\textbf{Wartime-mediated trajectory divergence.} The most analytically significant wartime finding is not the infrastructure disruptions themselves but their differential impact on learning trajectories. Cross-referencing the four engagement modes identified in Section~\ref{sec:attendance} with the performance data (Tables~\ref{tab:individual-absence} and \ref{tab:mc-stats}) reveals a consistent pattern: mandatory assessment structures sustained academic performance even when voluntary participation collapsed. Nataliia~B. --- carrying the highest infrastructure-attributable absence count (predominantly power outages and internet failures) --- achieved zero seminar appearances yet maintained an MC mean of 91.5\% across four modular controls, the highest among all profiled frequent absentees. Her MC5 decline to 72.3\% matches the cohort-wide MC5 mean of 63.8\%, confirming that content difficulty rather than infrastructure disruption shaped her performance trajectory. Yana~M. --- with 5 absences spanning all infrastructure categories --- produced a mandatory--voluntary differential of 34.0 percentage points (MC mean 84.2\% vs.\ seminar mean 50.2\%), the largest individual differential in the dataset and the clearest individual-level confirmation of the cohort-level pattern. By contrast, Oleksandra~Z.'s escalating absence sequence --- internet failures in October--December followed by family absences in February--March --- accompanied complete post-gamification disengagement: all seven of her assessment appearances are confined to the pre-gamification period. These three profiles instantiate a fundamental tension in wartime distance education: mandatory summative structures are wartime-\emph{resilient} (sustaining performance even under severe individual disruption); voluntary engagement is wartime-\emph{fragile} (collapsing when cumulative disruption exceeds a threshold). The 5.6-fold participation ratio between mandatory controls (N\,=\,13--15) and voluntary seminars (N\,=\,2.7) during the Seminars~25--30 trough is the cohort-level measure of this differential; the three individual profiles are its individual-level instantiation \cite{Martynets202428}.

\textbf{Gamification as the most wartime-resilient educational layer.} The HistoryLikeGame platform's architecture --- static HTML files requiring no server communication after initial load, shared infrastructure $\approx$88~KB, individual game pages typically $<$30~KB (Section~\ref{sec:platform-evolution}) --- was empirically validated as the most accessible course component during the April~13, 2025 session: the highest single-session disruption of the entire course (7 absence reports from 5 students, including 3 internet failures and 2 students who arrived late citing urgency). Quiz links posted in the Viber chat history remained accessible asynchronously and functional entirely offline after first load. This stress-test confirms what the architecture was designed to provide: the gamification layer degrades gracefully to asynchronous access mode without reconfiguration. Unlike VR/AR implementations that dominate the gamification literature \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, 13Humpire2022, 15Slater2022} --- which require real-time rendering, high-bandwidth streaming, and specialised hardware --- or synchronous Zoom sessions, HistoryLikeGame remained accessible during the course's worst disruption event. This inverts the common assumption that gamification requires \emph{more} technology than traditional instruction: in wartime contexts, carefully designed lightweight gamification can require \emph{less} reliable connectivity than any other educational format. \citeauthor{Zhumbei2025}~\cite{Zhumbei2025} found, in a direct parallel from Ukrainian wartime higher education during the same conflict period, that gamified elements ``proved effective not only in learning but also in providing emotional support'' --- a dual function consistent with HistoryLikeGame's role in sustaining both academic performance and course participation structure under infrastructure stress \cite{Vovchasta2024}.

\textbf{Safety-first pedagogy as a documented institutional norm.} The instructor's February~2, 2025 communication --- ``Your safety is the most important thing [\ldots] if in doubt, go to a shelter. Lecture recordings are published here after each session'' --- documents an institutional norm shift: from the pre-war expectation of mandatory synchronous attendance \cite{MON} to a safety-prioritised, asynchronous-default model applied consistently across all 38 sessions \cite{UNESCO2023}. \cite{Burde2017} identify this exact repositioning --- the explicit institutional prioritisation of learner safety over attendance compliance --- as a foundational requirement for educational continuity in conflict zones. By decoupling educational access from synchronous presence, the policy enabled two of the four engagement modes documented in Section~\ref{sec:attendance}: the ``sustained voluntary'' mode (Violetta~P., continued seminar participation across five post-gamification sessions despite synchronous interruptions) and the ``exclusively summative'' mode (Nataliia~B., full academic performance through asynchronous modular controls with zero synchronous seminar appearances). The safety-first policy is thus not merely an ethical commitment but the structural precondition for the resilience patterns documented in this section \cite{Ovcharuk2023}.

\textbf{Community cohesion under wartime stress.} The 33 absence notifications submitted to the Viber group over six months constitute evidence of a behavioral norm with analytical significance: students consistently chose to inform the group when they could not attend rather than simply not appearing. This obligation-to-report behavior was maintained even under the most severe disruptions --- during the April~13, 2025 cluster, three students experiencing internet failure still sent notifications before or during the session. \cite{Burde2017} identify social cohesion as the primary predictor of educational continuity in conflict-affected settings; the notification behavior provides micro-level evidence of this mechanism operating within the course. The Viber group also served a practical resilience function: quiz links, lecture recordings, and platform links posted in the chat history remained accessible asynchronously, meaning that any student who received a notification could access course materials at any time, independent of synchronous participation. This transforms the communication layer from a messaging tool into a persistent, asynchronous educational resource archive accessible to the entire cohort under any connectivity condition \cite{Sailer2017, Hlianenko2024}.

\textbf{Implications for conflict-zone research design.} The empirical record of this study illustrates a methodological challenge that \cite{Burde2017} and \cite{Kirk2007} have noted but rarely quantified: standard research quality criteria --- participation rates, completion rates, assessment timing, sample stability --- become confounded variables rather than quality indicators in conflict-affected settings. An 89\% mandatory--voluntary participation differential is not a measurement artifact but a direct quantification of wartime's motivational reorganisation. A 71-day enrollment suspension is not a recruitment failure but a wartime disruption of institutional capacity. An absence rate of 33.3\% attributable to infrastructure failures is not a threat to internal validity but a primary data point about the study context. The MRQS framework \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}, designed for controlled research contexts, assigns high risk of bias to studies with small, variable samples --- but in a conflict zone, sample variability \emph{is} the data. Seven months of longitudinal observation were necessary to distinguish the December 2024 infrastructure crisis from the January--February voluntary withdrawal from the April 2025 recurrence: these three disruption waves have different mechanisms and implications that a single-point or two-point design would collapse into an undifferentiated ``wartime effect''. The present study's design demonstrates what longitudinal commitment enables in conflict-zone education research: the disaggregation of disruption types, the detection of resilience differentials between mandatory and voluntary structures, and the documentation of individual trajectory divergence under cumulative stress \cite{Burde2017, Kirk2007}.

\textit{Synthesis.} The wartime context of this study functions as more than a background condition: it is a structural variable that shaped every measurable dimension of the course and produces three findings that are unavailable from standard gamification research. First, that gamified digital history education \emph{can} sustain academically meaningful learning under active armed conflict: mandatory modular controls achieved a post-gamification group mean of 75.6\% despite severe infrastructure disruption, and an individually disrupted student (Nataliia~B.) achieved an MC mean of 91.5\% with zero synchronous seminar appearances. Second, that mandatory assessment structures are wartime's most resilient educational format, sustaining participation at 5.6 times the voluntary rate during the deepest disruption trough --- a finding that has direct design implications for courses delivered under conflict conditions. Third, that lightweight platform design with built-in inclusivity features (HistoryLikeGame: $<$30~KB per page, offline-capable after first load, inclusive settings panel) is not merely a technical convenience but a conflict-zone \emph{inclusivity and accessibility requirement} --- the only course component that remained fully accessible during the most severe single-session disruption event. These findings respond to \citeauthor{Burde2017}'s~\cite{Burde2017} call for empirical documentation of educational delivery mechanisms under conflict conditions and extend the Ukrainian wartime education literature \cite{Martynets202428, Ovcharuk2023, Zhumbei2025} with the first gamification-specific data collected under active military operations.

\subsection{Limitations}

The following twelve limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. They are organised to distinguish methodological constraints intrinsic to single-group educational research designs from context-specific constraints arising from Ukrainian wartime conditions, as both categories are present and their interaction compounds the inferential challenges. For comparative context, the MRQS systematic review \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} found that only 47.3\% of 74 gamification studies explicitly discussed limitations; this section aims to exceed that standard by quantifying each limitation where possible and stating the mitigating strategies applied.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Data coverage and pre-gamification granularity}: Response data are available for 36 of the 40 numbered seminars (Seminars~1--39, excluding Seminars~8, 34, 38, and~40) and all 7 modular controls, covering approximately 90\% of total instructional events. The pre-gamification data were recovered from multi-sheet XLSX files with aggregated per-student averages rather than raw item-level response logs, limiting granularity in that period: response patterns by question type, historical sub-period, or revision attempt are unavailable for Seminars~1--24. A structural asymmetry compounds this: 52.8\% of covered seminars (19 of 36) had fewer than 10 respondents (mean $N = 10.3$, median $N \approx 9.5$), meaning that individual-seminar scores carry high sampling uncertainty. Direct before-after comparison is further confounded by the simultaneous variation of topic difficulty, assessment format, and cohort composition \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. \textit{Mitigation:} all analyses report $N$ per seminar; claims about group-level trends are restricted to periods with $N \geq 5$.

\item \textbf{Small and variable sample sizes}: Respondents per seminar ranged from 1 to 27 (mean $N = 10.3$; median $N \approx 9.5$), with 52.8\% of seminars (19/36) falling below $N = 10$ and two seminars (Seminars~28 and~30) having only a single respondent each \cite{Borenstein2009, Korniienko2025mrqs}. The modular control series shows an analogous pattern: participation declined 74\% from MC2 ($N = 27$) to MC6 ($N = 7$). For reference, a two-group pre-post design requires $N \approx 64$ per group to achieve 80\% power at $d = 0.5$, $\alpha = .05$ \cite{Borenstein2009}; no single seminar approaches that threshold, and this study shares the underpowered pattern of the field: the MRQS corpus median was 33.5 participants, with only 9.5\% of studies reporting effect sizes \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. \textit{Mitigation:} only descriptive statistics are reported; inferential claims and causal language are systematically avoided.

\item \textbf{Absence of control group}: The single-group design precludes causal attribution of outcomes to the gamification intervention \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, creswell}. This is not anomalous: the MRQS review \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} found that 68.9\% of 74 history gamification studies were single-group designs and only one study employed a fully randomised design. In the present context, a control group would have required withholding a free educational platform from enrolled NMT candidates during active wartime --- ethically impermissible \cite{Reeves2003} and practically impossible given the open-enrollment, asynchronous-access model of the CDPO. \textit{Mitigation:} the study is framed as exploratory and descriptive throughout; causal language is avoided; modular controls with consistent format serve as the closest available within-series comparison.

\item \textbf{Self-selection and attrition bias}: Students who completed quizzes may differ systematically from those who did not, inflating apparent performance \cite{ary, creswell}. The scale of this bias can be partially quantified: of approximately 90 members identifiable in the Viber chat history, only 62 could be matched to any quiz response, leaving approximately 28 students ($\approx$31\%) with no individual-level academic record. Within the 62 matched students, 53 appear in pre-gamification data; of these, 26 (49\%) are absent from all post-gamification records --- representing within-study attrition comparable to the field's overall dropout rates in distance education contexts. Paired pre-post data therefore exist for only approximately 27 students ($\approx$30\% of the Viber cohort), substantially restricting the interpretable base for longitudinal comparisons. High-performing students tended to participate across more seminars (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}), consistent with self-selection amplifying observed mean scores \cite{KoivistoHamari2019}. \textit{Mitigation:} trajectory analyses are reported separately for the full response sample and the matched-subsample; dropout is documented in Table~\ref{tab:individual-absence}.

\item \textbf{Rolling enrollment and unequal exposure}: New student enrollment comprised 23 documented joins across three phases: 17 early (October--November~2024), 4 mid-course (January--February~2025), and 2 late (March--May~2025). An institutional enrollment suspension of 71 days (November~13 -- January~22) coincided with the December~2024 infrastructure crisis peak. Six late enrollees (26\% of documented joins) had zero exposure to pre-gamification baseline conditions and are structurally ineligible for within-subject comparisons; students joining in April~2025 received only the final five to six weeks of course access, versus seven months for October enrollees. This heterogeneity creates exposure inequity that cannot be resolved post hoc without individual enrollment-date covariates. \textit{Mitigation:} enrollment phase is documented in Table~\ref{tab:individual-absence} and used as a stratifying variable in trajectory interpretation.

\item \textbf{Limited demographic and baseline data}: Participants are identified only by name form submitted in quiz responses; no individual-level background data were collected. Missing variables include: prior NMT score history, current school grade, geographic location within Ukraine, conflict-zone proximity, displacement status, household technology infrastructure (device type, internet quality), digital literacy level (directly relevant given the DigComp~2.0 framework invoked in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}), prior gamification experience, and psychological burden measures. The absence of conflict-proximity and displacement data is a wartime-specific gap: \cite{UNESCO2023} and \cite{Humeniuk2022113} identify these as primary moderators of educational engagement in conflict-affected settings that standardised demographic surveys do not capture. The importance of individual-difference variables as moderators of gamification effectiveness has been consistently emphasised by \citeauthor{KoivistoHamari2019}~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019} and \citeauthor{Hamari2014}~\cite{Hamari2014}. \textit{Mitigation:} absence of demographic data defines the ceiling on interpretive depth; future replications should incorporate a baseline demographic and digital-literacy survey.

\item \textbf{Name inconsistency and identity resolution limits}: Some students submitted responses under different name formats (e.g., ``Анастасія~К.'' vs.\ the diminutive ``Настя~К.''; ``Софія~В.'' in given-name-first vs.\ surname-first formats; ``Єлизавета'' vs.\ ``Ліза''). All recoverable cases were resolved through conservative manual matching using submission timing, Viber chat mentions, and participation patterns. However, the residual gap of approximately 28 students between Viber cohort size and matched individual records is partly attributable to irresolvable name ambiguity and anonymous submissions, reducing the effective $N$ for within-subject analyses. \textit{Mitigation:} matching decisions are documented in the analysis scripts; all ambiguous cases were left unlinked rather than speculatively assigned.

\item \textbf{Gamification platform usage data}: The HistoryLikeGame platform, comprising static HTML files designed for offline-accessible delivery, collects no user interaction data by architecture: session duration, module visit counts, quiz re-attempt frequency, and time-on-task are unavailable. Only 21.6\% of studies in the MRQS corpus used validated engagement measures \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}; this study shares the field's predominant pattern of relying on quiz score proxies rather than direct interaction logging. Platform engagement is therefore inferred from quiz completion records, score trajectories, and Viber chat mentions of specific modules. \textit{Mitigation:} the Viber chat log (400 messages) was systematically coded for platform mentions; quiz completion counts per student provide a behavioural engagement indicator, albeit indirect.

\item \textbf{Researcher-as-instructor dual role}: The researcher who designed the intervention also delivered all 38 course sessions and administered all assessments, creating two overlapping bias risks. First, \textit{observation bias}: the instructor's expectations may have influenced instructional emphasis or pacing in ways not captured by course materials, which could have advantaged or disadvantaged gamified relative to non-gamified sessions. Second, \textit{social desirability effects}: students may have perceived quiz responses as visible to the instructor and adjusted their effort accordingly, even though the Google Forms platform records responses under students' own names \cite{creswell}. Both risks are documented in educational action research and design-based research \cite{Reeves2003}. \textit{Mitigation:} assessment scoring was fully automated (no subjective grading by the instructor); the curriculum was externally fixed by the NMT examination format, limiting instructor discretion over what knowledge was assessed; the reproducible analysis pipeline is independently verifiable.

\item \textbf{Topic difficulty as an irresolvable confound}: The pre-gamification period (Seminars~1--24) covered early and medieval Ukrainian history, while the post-gamification period (Seminars~25--39) covered 20th-century history including World Wars, the Holodomor, the Soviet period, and the post-2014 conflict --- content that generates lower NMT scores due to greater factual volume, more nuanced interpretive demands, and fewer visual source question types than medieval content. The 7.3\,pp aggregate score gap (70.7\% pre vs.\ 63.4\% post) consequently cannot be attributed to gamification, topic difficulty, or their interaction without a cross-topic control group \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. \textit{Mitigation:} modular controls (MC1--MC7), which span both periods with standardised format, serve as the primary difficulty-controlled comparison; the topic difficulty confound is flagged in all score-trend discussions; period-specific post-gamification seminars are analysed separately in Section~\ref{sec:formative}.

\item \textbf{Absence of a pre-course knowledge baseline}: No standardised pre-test was administered at course entry. Seminar~1 (N\,=\,18, mean 65.0\%) serves as a de facto group-level proxy baseline but does not constitute a validated individual pre-test: it confounds prior knowledge with response to Seminar~1's specific content and format. Without pre-test data, individual-level learning gain cannot be computed, and within-student knowledge growth can only be inferred from seminar-to-seminar progression for students with multiple entries. Only 39.2\% of gamification studies in the MRQS corpus used pre-post longitudinal measurement \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}; the absence of a validated entry measure is among the most consequential and most common methodological gaps in the field. \textit{Mitigation:} the modular control series (MC1--MC7, all 7 administrations) provides a within-course longitudinal record across standardised assessments; individual trajectory analysis (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) partially substitutes for pre-post design by documenting score change over time.

\item \textbf{Wartime conditions as unquantified moderating confound}: The study was conducted during active armed conflict (October~2024 -- May~2025), with documented air-raid alerts, infrastructure disruptions, and institutional suspension periods. Psychological stressors associated with wartime --- chronic anxiety, grief, displacement, and safety uncertainty --- function as moderating variables that influence both learning outcomes and participation behaviour \cite{UNESCO2023, Humeniuk2022113, Burde2017} but are unquantified in this dataset: no validated psychological well-being instrument was administered, displacement status was not recorded, and the severity of individual participants' wartime exposure cannot be reconstructed from available data. The infrastructure-attributable absence data (33.3\% of documented absence reasons: 24.2\% internet failures, 9.1\% power outages; Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}) capture only the logistical surface of wartime impact, not the deeper cognitive and motivational dimensions documented in the Ukrainian wartime education literature \cite{Martynets202428, Ovcharuk2023, Zamkova2023299}. The decision not to administer psychological instruments was deliberate: in the active-conflict context, such instruments carry ethical risks of re-traumatisation that were judged to outweigh measurement benefits. \textit{Mitigation:} the wartime context is documented longitudinally throughout Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}; absence typology is classified and quantified (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}); wartime disruption is treated as a structural explanatory variable rather than noise in all interpretations.

\end{enumerate}

These twelve limitations are reported in full accordance with PRISMA~2020 \cite{Page2021, Moher2009PRISMA} and CONSORT \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT} transparency standards. Several limitations --- small sample sizes, absence of a control group, rolling enrollment, and absence of a knowledge baseline --- are characteristic of the 83.8\% of gamification studies assessed as high risk of bias in the MRQS corpus \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. The four wartime-specific limitations (Items 6, 9 [partial], 11, and 12) have no established mitigating protocol in the gamification literature and are documented here as defining features of the study context, following the methodological framework for conflict-zone educational research proposed by \citeauthor{Burde2017}~\cite{Burde2017} and \cite{Kirk2007}. Taken together, the twelve limitations define the boundaries of what can and cannot be claimed: they rule out causal attribution, high statistical power, and demographic generalisability, while leaving intact the descriptive, longitudinal, and context-specific contributions. The critical difference between this study and the 52.7\% of reviewed studies that did not discuss limitations \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} is that each limitation here is accompanied by a stated mitigation strategy and a quantitative bound where data permit --- following the transparency standard that only 9.5\% of the reviewed corpus achieved for effect-size reporting \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. A formal quality self-assessment translating this limitation profile into a structured MRQS rating follows in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs} (Table~\ref{tab:self-assessment}), placing the study in the moderate-risk category above the 83.8\% high-risk majority \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, GuyattGRADE2008}.

\textbf{Domain~1 --- Participant selection and study context.}
The two signalling questions resolve differently.
Q1 --- were inclusion criteria pre-specified and consistently applied? --- resolves as
\emph{favorable}: all enrolled CDPO NMT-prep students were included without exception
(Section~\ref{sec:baseline}); the complete-cohort design eliminates post hoc selection;
the study context is longitudinally documented across both Sections~\ref{sec:baseline}
and~\ref{sec:wartime-context}.
Q2 --- is the sample likely to represent the target population? --- resolves as
\emph{partial}: $N\,{=}\,23$ falls below the field median of 33.5 \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}
and in the lower quartile of corpus study sizes.
Critically, demographic representativeness in the conventional sense is structurally
unavailable under active armed conflict \cite{Burde2017}: a study with $N\,{=}\,100$ from
the same wartime distance-enrollment context would still face the same Q2 constraint because
the barrier is not sample size but the impossibility of random or stratified sampling from a
displacement-distributed population.
The 85.1\% of field studies rated high risk in Domain~1 consisted predominantly of studies
conducted in stable institutional settings with predefined enrollment targets; this study is
among the 8.1\% rated moderate by operational construction, not by reporting deficiency.

\textbf{Domain~2 --- Data collection and analysis.}
Q3 --- were appropriate and valid methods used to assess outcomes? --- resolves as
\emph{favorable}: NMT-format Google Forms constitute the nationally standardised instrument
for Ukrainian university entrance preparation; automatic platform scoring eliminates
all instructor-rater bias; the format aligns directly with the target examination, ensuring
construct validity; the seven modular controls (Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) maintained
identical 40--50 question format across all administrations, providing a within-course
difficulty-controlled comparison unavailable from voluntary seminar participation alone.
Q4 --- were appropriate methods used to limit analytical bias? --- also resolves as
\emph{favorable}: the Python analysis pipeline (thesis/analysis/, Section~\ref{sec:scoring})
is fully reproducible and independently verifiable; all intermediate outputs are preserved
in CSV format; all analytical decisions are encoded in scripts rather than made interactively.
Pre-gamification data recovered from XLSX files are documented with their granularity
limitations (Section~\ref{sec:assessment}).
The combination of automated scoring, standardised format, and reproducible pipeline places
this study among the 12.2\% rated low risk in Domain~2 --- a standard achieved despite
the absence of a control group, through mechanisms entirely within researcher control.

\textbf{Domain~3 --- Interpretation and reporting.}
Q5 --- were findings interpreted with appropriate caution? --- resolves as
\emph{favorable}: causal language is systematically absent from Chapter~\ref{ch:results};
the 7.3\,pp aggregate pre-post score difference (70.7\% pre vs.\ 63.4\% post) is
consistently attributed to the competing explanations of topic difficulty, cohort
composition change, and assessment format shift rather than to gamification; the modular
control series (1.6\,pp gap, Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) is foregrounded as the interpretively
preferred signal over the confounded seminar comparison;
individual trajectory claims (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}) are restricted to the matched
subsample with documented coverage limitations.
Q6 --- were limitations adequately discussed? --- resolves as \emph{favorable}: the twelve
limitations in subsection~3.4.4 each carry a quantitative bound where data permit and a
stated mitigation strategy, exceeding the 47.3\% field prevalence for limitation disclosure
and extending toward the quantified-bound transparency standard that only 9.5\% of the
reviewed corpus achieved \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.
Domain~3 risk is therefore low, placing this study among the 17.6\% of the 74-study corpus
at this risk level.

The zero scores on M2, M3, M5, and M7 reflect structural constraints of the conflict-zone cohort design, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}; these are not reporting failures but contextually unavoidable design boundaries.

The domain rating pattern reveals a \textbf{structural asymmetry} that the aggregate MRQS
score partially obscures.
Domains~2 and~3 --- data collection and interpretation, entirely under researcher control
--- are both rated low risk, placing this study in the top 12.2\%--17.6\% of the field on
those dimensions.
Domain~1 --- participant selection --- is rated moderate, and cannot be improved within the
wartime open-enrollment context regardless of researcher effort.
This asymmetry is the methodological signature of conflict-zone educational research:
researchers can maximise what is controllable (analytical rigour, reporting transparency,
instrument standardisation, limitation disclosure) while remaining unable to overcome the
structural constraints of the conflict environment.
The MRQS/ROBIS framework correctly identifies \emph{where} the ceiling lies and \emph{where}
the study exceeds field norms --- a distinction that a single aggregate risk-of-bias label
would collapse \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Whiting2016}.

Translating this profile into \citeauthor{GuyattGRADE2008}'s GRADE evidence certainty framework~\cite{GuyattGRADE2008},
the study supports ``low to moderate certainty'' for its descriptive claims.
Certainty is limited by the single-group design, small $N$, and absence of causal
identification --- factors that GRADE treats as automatic downgrading criteria.
Certainty is elevated by the 7-month longitudinal duration (among the longest in the
74-study corpus), the 7 standardised modular controls providing a within-course format-controlled
comparison, the reproducible analysis pipeline, and the 12 quantified limitations bounding
the inferential claims.
For a conflict-zone cohort study in a field where 83.8\% of published research is
rated high overall risk of bias \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}, this evidence profile represents
the methodological ceiling achievable without a multi-institutional controlled design ---
a design identified as the primary direction for future research \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsection{Comparison with the gamification evidence base}

The 74-study corpus reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}
provides a multi-dimensional reference frame against which the present study can be
positioned across five analytical dimensions: (1) implementation duration and longitudinal
depth; (2) sample size and assessment volume; (3) learning outcomes against the field's
effect-size distribution; (4) technology taxonomy and platform design; and (5) engagement
evidence and wartime attendance patterns. Each dimension reveals both where this study
falls within the field distribution and what it contributes to the gaps identified in
Section~\ref{sec:mrqs} --- gaps that the MRQS analysis \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} has
shown to be systematic rather than incidental.

\textbf{Dimension~1 --- Implementation duration and longitudinal depth.}
The seven-month implementation (October~2024 -- May~2025) substantially exceeds the
field norm. Among the 17 studies in the 74-study corpus that reported specific intervention
durations, 8 (47.1\%) involved sessions of 20 minutes or less
\cite{11Ternblad2023115, 21Ghulamani2017, 1Barbatsis201159}, and only 4 documented
implementations exceeding one week \cite{55Squire2004, 46Stirling2022, 9Lin2018,
48Moketar2021}. The present study's 30-week span --- 36 formative seminar sessions and
7 summative modular controls, totalling 43 distinct assessment events --- makes it the
longest documented gamification implementation in the history education corpus by both
calendar duration and number of assessment events.
This temporal depth enables trend analyses structurally impossible in brief interventions:
the identification of period-specific performance peaks (Seminar~11: 86.2\%;
Seminar~13: 94.2\%; Tables~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}--\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post})
and troughs (Seminar~30: 33.3\%; Seminar~37: 50.7\%), the mapping of participation
dynamics across 36 sessions (Section~\ref{sec:formative}), and the separation of
gamification effects from topic-difficulty confounds through the modular control series
(Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}).
\citeauthor{KoivistoHamari2019}~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019} identified the short-term focus of most gamification research
as the field's most critical methodological gap; \cite{Hernandez2017} reached the same
conclusion in the adjacent software engineering gamification literature.
The 30-week implementation directly addresses this gap by providing the longitudinal
record that enables within-course temporal analysis.

\textbf{Dimension~2 --- Sample size and assessment volume.}
At $N\,{=}\,23$, the enrolled cohort falls below the corpus median of approximately 34
participants \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} and below the M2 threshold of 30.
However, sample size alone understates the study's empirical depth.
The 36 seminar sessions and 7 modular controls generate 358 individual data points ---
a volume substantially exceeding the empirical record of the single-group pre-post
designs that dominate the corpus (68.9\%; \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}), where
observable data typically amount to $2N \approx 46$--68 data points for comparable
cohort sizes \cite{16Chen201447, 6Czerwinski2018, 8Yu2014}.
Per-session participation ranged from $N\,{=}\,1$ (Seminars~28 and~30) to $N\,{=}\,27$
(Seminar~17; Table~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-pre}), and pre-gamification seminars averaged
$N\,{\approx}\,12.9$ versus $N\,{\approx}\,5.6$ in the post-gamification period
(Table~\ref{tab:seminar-stats-post}).
The modular controls maintained higher and more stable participation ($N\,{=}\,7$--$27$,
Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}), confirming that the post-gamification seminar decline reflects
the shift from compulsory modular assessment to voluntary formative participation rather
than cohort attrition.
This variable-$N$ structure, while imposing statistical constraints (Section~3.4.5,
Table~\ref{tab:mrqs-items}), reflects the authentic dynamics of open-enrollment wartime
education and provides a richer longitudinal picture than a fixed-panel design with the
same total $N$.

\textbf{Dimension~3 --- Learning outcomes against the field's effect-size distribution.}
The 74-study corpus reported positive learning effects in 85.1\% of studies, with
effect sizes in the range $d\,{=}\,0.28$--$0.61$ for factual knowledge outcomes and
smaller effects for interpretive and higher-order outcomes \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012,
KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The present study's modular control series provides the closest
available analogue to the pre-post comparisons that generate these field estimates.
The pre-gamification modular control (MC1: 77.2\%, $N\,{=}\,19$) and the six
post-gamification controls (MC2--MC7: mean 75.6\%; Table~\ref{tab:mc-stats}) yield the
1.6 percentage-point aggregate gap that, under format-controlled conditions, is the
study's most interpretively defensible learning outcome signal
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
This positions the present study in the ``negligible to small effect'' region
($d\,{\lesssim}\,0.1$--$0.2$) consistent with the lower bound of the field distribution
--- a modest but methodologically appropriate estimate for a single-group,
conflict-zone cohort study \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

The topic-specificity pattern further aligns with field findings from history-specific
implementations.
Seminars on bounded factual topics consistently outperformed comprehensive multi-period
assessments: Seminar~11 (86.2\%, medieval history), Seminar~13 (94.2\%, ancient period),
and Seminar~10 (81.0\%) versus Seminar~37 (50.7\%, 2016 comprehensive, 90 questions)
and Seminar~39 (63.5\%, comprehensive, 87 questions).
The modular controls confirm this pattern: MC5 (63.8\%), the lowest-scoring control,
also had the highest question count (65 questions covering multiple periods), while
MC2 (84.6\%) covered a single chronological block.
This factual-recall advantage over interpretive and comprehensive outcomes reproduces
the effect-size pattern documented across the corpus \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012} and
in history-specific implementations by \citeauthor{53Perez2014}~\cite{53Perez2014}, \cite{54Gibson2007}, and
\cite{59Kee2014270}.

implementations (headsets, trackers) in the 25.7\% VR/AR field cohort
\cite{13Humpire2022, 15Slater2022, 10Huaman2019220}.
The full system (v2: 62 modules, shared infrastructure $\approx$88\,KB, total
$\approx$869\,KB of custom code) represents the most extensively documented
multi-game-type gamification deployment in the history education literature.
The iterative design trajectory from v1 (December~2024: 23 quiz modules, 205\,KB)
to v2 (current: 62 modules, 13 game types) documents a practitioner design-based
research cycle \cite{Reeves2003} absent from the vast majority of reviewed studies,
where single-version platforms were evaluated without documented iteration.

\textbf{Dimension~5 --- Engagement evidence and wartime attendance patterns.}
The 74-study corpus reported positive engagement effects in 70.3\% of studies, most
frequently evidenced through self-reported motivation surveys or controlled pre-post
engagement scales \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Hamari2014, Sailer2017}.
The present study contributes a qualitatively different engagement record: longitudinal
and behavioural rather than self-reported, consisting of participation rates across
36 sessions, documented absence reasons from the Viber group chat
(Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}), and platform architecture as engagement infrastructure.

The 33 documented absences reveal a wartime participation structure qualitatively
different from the stable institutional settings assumed by most reviewed studies.
Internet connectivity failures (24.2\% of absence reasons), power outages (9.1\%),
and family circumstances (24.2\%) collectively account for 57.5\% of absences ---
all external to student motivation and unrelated to academic engagement
\cite{Burde2017, UNESCO2023}.
Health absences (9.1\%) and late arrivals (9.1\%) account for a further 18.2\%,
with unspecified reasons (24.2\%) constituting the remainder.
In a stable-context study, this absence profile would signal high Domain~1 risk of
selection bias; in the wartime context documented in Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context},
it instead documents the resilience mechanisms that enabled the core cohort to
maintain 70\%--85\% modular control participation throughout the conflict environment.

The self-determination theory framework \citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}~\cite{RyanDeci2000} predicts that intrinsic
motivation --- supported by the HistoryLikeGame's autonomy (self-paced, browser-accessible
practice), competence (immediate feedback, progressive difficulty across 9 periods), and
relatedness (shared Viber group, cohort identity) design features --- generates
sustained engagement over long interventions.
\citeauthor{vanRoyZaman2019}~\cite{vanRoyZaman2019} identified this SDT alignment as the strongest predictor of
positive long-term gamification outcomes in controlled comparisons.
The participation pattern observed --- stable core cohort across 36 sessions, high
modular control attendance despite seminar voluntary participation declining after
gamification introduction --- is consistent with this prediction and with the
substitution hypothesis: students may have shifted to asynchronous HistoryLikeGame
practice in place of synchronous seminar participation, a behaviour that synchronous
attendance data alone cannot capture \citeauthor{KoivistoHamari2019}~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019}.

\textbf{Definitional and theoretical contribution.}
Only 14.9\% of the 74-study corpus provided explicit gamification definitions
\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}; the present study is among that minority by applying
the \citeauthor{Deterding2011}~\cite{Deterding2011} taxonomy to distinguish three simultaneously operational
components: \emph{structural gamification} (creative seminar naming, point-based scoring,
cross-session progression tracking), \emph{content gamification} (thematic quiz blocks
aligned to 9 historical periods, narrative integration through period structure), and
\emph{game-based learning} (the HistoryLikeGame platform, where historical content is
embedded within game mechanics rather than supplemented by them).
This tri-component operationalisation demonstrates that real-world implementations
rarely map onto a single conceptual category, consistent with the blended-approach
findings of broader reviews \cite{SEABORN201514, Zhonggen2019, Unkelos-Shpigel2016}.

The theoretical grounding --- \citeauthor{Deterding2011}'s DMC pyramid~\cite{Deterding2011}, Werbach--Hunter design
framework~\cite{Werbach2012}, and \citeauthor{Vuorikari2016}'s DigComp~2.0 competence model~\cite{Vuorikari2016}
(Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}) --- places the study in the upper tier of M9 compliance
(field prevalence 94.6\%) while uniquely aligning gamification design with
history-specific competence outcomes across all five DigComp~2.0 areas: information
literacy (Google Forms quiz practice), communication (Viber group), content creation
(lecture presentation materials), safety (wartime network protocols), and problem solving
(HistoryLikeGame progressive difficulty mechanics).

The five-dimensional comparison reveals a study that is structurally constrained on
the dimensions most visible to conventional quality metrics (sample size, control group,
effect sizes) while exceeding field norms on the dimensions that depend on researcher
decisions: longitudinal depth (30 weeks, 43 events; field median $\leq$20 minutes),
technology breadth (13 game types vs.\ typically 1), definitional clarity (14.9\%
club), theoretical grounding (triple-framework operationalisation), and transparent
reporting (12 quantified limitations).
This asymmetry --- structurally constrained but researcher-maximised --- is the
consistent signature identified across Section~3.4.5's MRQS/ROBIS analysis and directly
answers the field's documented gap \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}: what conflict-zone cohort
research can achieve is constrained by the conflict environment; what this study
achieves within those constraints is the methodological ceiling for its design category.

\subsection{Implications for digital history methodology}

\label{sec:dh-methodology}

This study's empirical record constitutes a practical instantiation of the digital history
pedagogy framework developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} and, as such, carries implications
for digital history methodology that extend beyond gamification to the broader challenge
of designing technology-enhanced history education under adverse infrastructure conditions.
Building on the nascent digital history pedagogy evidence base (6/46 studies; Section~\ref{sec:dh-review}), the present study extends the empirical record in five analytically distinct ways.

\textbf{The four-type taxonomy fully instantiated.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} proposed a taxonomy of four digital history approach types
based on the literature: (1)~\emph{archival-analytical} --- engagement with digitised
primary sources and evidence-based argumentation \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, Crymble2021};
(2)~\emph{creative-productive} --- creation of digital artefacts communicating historical
narratives \cite{guiliano}; (3)~\emph{collaborative-communicative} --- co-construction
of historical knowledge through networked platforms \cite{Siemens2004, Wright-Maley2018};
and (4)~\emph{immersive-experiential} --- engagement through games, simulations, and
interactive environments \cite{Connolly2012, Qian2016, Prensky2001}.
The present study instantiates all four types simultaneously within a single methodology.
The 156 Google Forms quizzes operationalise the archival-analytical approach: NMT-format
questions require evidence-based selection from primary source data, contextualisation
of events, and source corroboration --- the historical thinking skills enumerated by
\citeauthor{Wineburg2001}~\cite{Wineburg2001} and \cite{Seixas2013}.
The HistoryLikeGame platform (13 game types, 9 historical periods, 62 modules) operationalises
the immersive-experiential approach: students interact with historical content embedded in
game mechanics rather than supplemented by them, across formats from card solitaire to
caf\'{e} simulator to A-Frame AR.
The Viber group (400+ messages across the course) operationalises the
collaborative-communicative approach: students and instructor co-constructed the course
schedule, reported absence reasons, and shared problem-solving strategies in a networked
community of practice \cite{Siemens2004}.
Creative quiz naming (Section~\ref{sec:quiz-naming}) operationalises the creative-productive
approach at the assessment layer: thematic titles such as ``Challenge of the Cossack Era''
transform each quiz into a narrative artefact that frames historical content as
a story in progress.
This four-type integration is among the few documented instances in the digital history pedagogy literature of all four approach types operating simultaneously within a single course. Crucially, however, these four approaches rest on a foundational layer that the taxonomy itself does not name: the reinforcement of factual knowledge at Bloom's \textit{Remember} and \textit{Understand} levels \cite{Anderson2001}. The gamified quiz mechanics --- 62~modules spanning 13~game types --- operate primarily at this foundational level, addressing the learning losses documented by PISA~2022 \cite{OECDPISA2023} and the NMT results \cite{UCEQA2024, UCEQA2025} before students can meaningfully engage with higher-order archival, creative, collaborative, or immersive tasks (Section~\ref{subsec:dh-intersection}).

\textbf{Constructivist and connectivist grounding in the observed data.}
Vygotsky's zone of proximal development (ZPD) \citeauthor{Vygotsky1978}~\cite{Vygotsky1978} predicts that learning
is maximised when tasks are scaffolded at the boundary of current competence.
The quiz structure operationalised this: single-period seminars (Seminar~13: 94.2\%;
Seminar~11: 86.2\%; Seminar~10: 81.0\%) provided tractable challenges that situated
near the upper ZPD boundary for most participants, while comprehensive multi-period
assessments (Seminar~30: 33.3\%; Seminar~37: 50.7\%) exceeded the ZPD boundary,
producing the floor-effect pattern observed in post-gamification comprehensive seminars.
\citeauthor{Tilak2023}~\cite{Tilak2023} demonstrated analogous cybernetic ZPD dynamics in a digital social
studies curriculum; the present pattern replicates this mechanism in a Ukrainian history
NMT preparation context.
\citeauthor{Siemens2004}'s~\cite{Siemens2004} connectivist model situates learning in networks of people,
technologies, and information nodes rather than individual minds.
The three-wave enrollment trajectory (Wave~1: October~1--9, 9 students;
Wave~2: November~1--13, +8 students; Wave~3: January--May~2025, +6 students)
reflects exactly the open-network enrollment pattern that connectivist design
accommodates: knowledge previously distributed across the Viber group, Google Drive
materials, and HistoryLikeGame modules was accessible to late entrants without
institutional penalty, enabling meaningful participation from first contact.

\textbf{TPACK model applied to design decisions.}
The Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework
\cite{Mishra2006, Koehler2013} identifies the intersection of Content Knowledge (CK),
Pedagogical Knowledge (PK), and Technological Knowledge (TK) as the site where
effective technology integration is produced.
In this study, CK encompasses 9 historical periods, NMT curriculum alignment,
and periodisation-specific competences; PK encompasses the formative--summative
differential (voluntary seminars vs.\ mandatory MCs), the safety-first communication
culture, and rolling-enrollment accommodation; TK encompasses the HistoryLikeGame
static HTML architecture (offline-capable, $\approx$88\,KB shared infrastructure),
the 5-layer resilience design (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}), and Google Forms
quiz deployment.
The creative quiz naming convention (Section~\ref{sec:quiz-naming}) is the canonical
TPACK product: it transforms assessment format (PK) by applying thematic game-world
naming (TK) to historical content (CK), producing a qualitatively new pedagogical
object --- a quiz that functions simultaneously as a formative assessment instrument
and a narrative gamification element.

\textbf{DigComp 2.0 with empirical anchors.}
Section~\ref{sec:digcomp} aligned the course design with all five DigComp~2.0
competence areas \cite{Vuorikari2016, Titova2025cte}; the empirical data now provide
quantitative anchors for each:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Information and data literacy}: 156 Google Forms quizzes administered ---
  156 structured information-processing and evaluation episodes, each requiring
  selection, comparison, and judgement across historical source data.
\item \emph{Communication and collaboration}: 400+ Viber group messages documenting
  synchronous scheduling, peer support, and collective problem-solving across the
  30-week course; Zoom provided a complementary synchronous channel.
\item \emph{Digital content creation}: 62 HistoryLikeGame modules spanning 13 game-type
  formats and 9 periods --- created by the instructor and engaged by students as
  interactive historical narratives, with 10 A-Frame AR scenes adding spatial content
  creation dimensions.
\item \emph{Safety}: instructor's explicit safety protocol (``Your safety is the most
  important thing [\ldots] if in doubt, go to a shelter'') documented in the chat log
  and operationalised as the asynchronous-first design choice that allowed missed
  sessions to be recovered safely at any time.
\item \emph{Problem solving}: 13 distinct game-type mechanics present progressive
  problem-solving demands --- from factual recall (quiz format) to temporal ordering
  (chronological timeline) to resource management (caf\'{e} simulator) to spatial
  navigation (AR scenes) --- mapping to ascending Bloom's taxonomy levels.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Resilience architecture as a replicable model.}
The five-layer architecture (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context}, Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology})
is the study's most direct practical contribution to digital history methodology in
conflict-affected and infrastructure-poor settings \cite{Burde2017, UNESCO2023}.
The architecture's effectiveness is documented empirically: the 33-absence record
(57.5\% attributable to external causes) coincided with a MC participation range of
$N\,{=}\,7$--$27$ across all 7 modular controls spanning 7 months --- confirming
that the compulsory assessment layer remained accessible across the full range of
wartime disruption documented by \citeauthor{Martynets202428}~\cite{Martynets202428} and \cite{Zamkova2023299}.
The HistoryLikeGame layer's lightweight architecture ($\approx$88\,KB infrastructure,
individual modules $<$30\,KB, offline-capable after initial load) is a directly
transferable design principle for any digital history implementation in low-bandwidth
or politically disrupted educational environments \cite{Hlianenko2024}.

The five contributions above --- four-type taxonomy instantiation, constructivist
grounding, TPACK integration, DigComp 2.0 empirical anchoring, and resilience
architecture --- collectively define what this study proposes as a \emph{digital history
methodology model}: a multi-approach, theoretically grounded, infrastructure-adaptive
system that treats digital tools not as supplements to existing pedagogy but as the
primary medium through which historical learning, assessment, and community are delivered
\cite{Ertmer1999, Humeniuk2022113, Nunez202329}.
The model is not novel in its individual components --- each has precedent in the reviewed
literature --- but is novel in their simultaneous, explicitly documented integration
under conditions of active armed conflict, where the five-layer resilience architecture
makes the difference between educational continuity and interruption \citeauthor{Korniienko2025aredu}~\cite{Korniienko2025aredu}.

\subsection{Future research directions}

\label{sec:future-research}

The findings, limitations, and field-positioning analysis (Sections~3.4.5--3.4.6)
converge on a structured research agenda organised in three priority tiers:
Tier~1 addresses foundational gaps that constrain interpretation of the present results;
Tier~2 builds directly on the longitudinal record generated here;
Tier~3 addresses scaling and cross-context generalisation.

\paragraph{Tier~1 --- Foundational gaps (required before causal claims can be made).}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Anonymous learning analytics (HistoryLikeGame v3).}
The substitution hypothesis --- that voluntary seminar participation declined
($N$: 12.9$\,\to\,$5.6) because students shifted to asynchronous HistoryLikeGame
practice rather than disengaging --- is the most consequential unconfirmed claim
in the study. It cannot be evaluated without usage data from the platform itself.
Version~3 of HistoryLikeGame should add lightweight, privacy-preserving learning
analytics within the existing static HTML architecture. The proposed mechanism
stores anonymised session records in the browser's \texttt{localStorage}
(module~ID, start time, time-on-task, attempt count, score --- no personal
identifiers), with opt-in export when the learner chooses to share data with the
instructor. No server infrastructure, user accounts, or background data
transmission is required; all data remain on the student's device unless
explicitly exported. This approach preserves the platform's zero-registration,
offline-capable design while providing the evidence needed to test the
substitution hypothesis.
Confirming or refuting this hypothesis would determine whether
the post-gamification seminar trough (Seminars~25--30: mean $N\,{=}\,2.7$)
represents disengagement or modality shift --- a distinction with direct
implications for course redesign \cite{KoivistoHamari2019, Sailer2017}.

\item \textbf{Quasi-experimental controlled comparison.}
The MRQS review \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} found that control group usage declined
$-$13.5 percentage points over time, reaching 31.1\% in the 2017--2024 stratum ---
making controlled designs simultaneously more methodologically urgent and less
common in the field.
The recommended next implementation is a quasi-experimental design across two
parallel CDPO NMT preparation course sections (2026--2027 academic cycle):
one section receiving the full gamification intervention (HistoryLikeGame,
creative quiz naming, point-based scoring), one receiving the identical
curriculum and assessment format without gamification elements.
Random assignment at the course-section level controls for instructor effects
(same instructor), historical period difficulty (identical curriculum sequence),
and wartime disruption (same region and same period) --- addressing the three
primary confounds identified in Section~3.4.2.
Pre-registration of outcomes (MC score series, voluntary seminar participation rate)
would satisfy MRQS criterion M8 (experimental design), currently unmet in
68.9\% of the 74-study corpus \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Schulz2010CONSORT, creswell}.

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Tier~2 --- Building from the longitudinal record.}

\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}

\item \textbf{NMT/ZNO score follow-up.}
The modular control series (MC1--MC7) constitutes an extended set of criterion-referenced
predictors of NMT readiness: MC2 (84.6\%, single-period content) and MC4 (83.6\%)
correspond to the score range associated with strong NMT performance in Ukrainian
History \cite{Korniienko2025cte}.
Linking CDPO enrollment records to the May~2025 NMT examination results for the
23 enrolled students would provide the first downstream outcome measure in the
74-study corpus --- a direct link between formative gamification participation
and summative national examination performance.
The analytical target is the Pearson correlation between individual MC mean score
(across all controls in which each student participated) and NMT History score,
with $N\,{=}\,10$--$15$ (those participants who both completed $\geq$3 MCs and
sat the NMT examination).
This requires participant consent and a data-sharing arrangement with the
Ukrainian Assessment Centre, both feasible within the existing KDPU institutional
framework \cite{Page2021}.

\item \textbf{Design-Based Research iteration (v3 platform cycle).}
The v1$\to$v2 trajectory is documented: v1 (December~2024: 23 quiz modules, 205~KB)
$\to$ v2 (current: 62 modules, 13 game types, $\approx$869~KB).
This constitutes the first two cycles of the \citeauthor{Reeves2003}~\cite{Reeves2003} DBR sequence
(design--evaluate--redesign); v3 should be theory-driven by the findings.
Specific v3 design targets derived from the data:
(a)~built-in session analytics (enabling Tier~1 above);
(b)~multiplayer or competitive mode for a synchronous seminar replacement channel,
addressing the voluntary-seminar participation trough;
(c)~a teacher dashboard for participation monitoring that surfaces substitution
and disengagement patterns in near-real-time;
(d)~adaptive difficulty adjustment based on session history, instantiating the
ZPD scaffolding mechanism \citeauthor{Vygotsky1978}~\cite{Vygotsky1978} computationally rather than by
human curriculum design.

\item \textbf{Phenomenographic study of wartime student experience.}
The 24.2\% unspecified absence category (Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}) is
the study's most opaque data point: these 8 absences may reflect displacement,
psychological stress, military service, or other circumstances that participants
were unwilling or unable to document in a group chat.
A phenomenographic interview study \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Hamari2014} with
8--10 consenting former participants (15--20 minutes each) would capture the
variation in experience of gamified distance learning under conditions of armed
conflict --- a dimension that quantitative assessment data cannot access.
\citeauthor{Ditlmann2025}~\cite{Ditlmann2025} demonstrated that digital history participation mobilises
psychological responses (symbolic justice, intergroup relations improvement)
beyond the learning outcomes typically measured; the present study's cohort,
operating in an active warzone, likely generated analogous psychological dynamics
that interview data could document \cite{Zamkova2023299, Martynets202428}.

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Tier~3 --- Scaling and cross-context generalisation.}

\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{5}

\item \textbf{Multi-institution samples.}
The enrolled cohort ($N\,{=}\,23$) falls below the MRQS M2 threshold of 30 and
well below the statistical power thresholds recommended for effect-size estimation
in educational interventions \cite{Cohen2005, Borenstein2009}.
Scaling to a network of 3--5 CDPO institutions across different Ukrainian oblasts
(frontline, mid-country, western rear) would enable geographic subgroup analysis
directly relevant to wartime education equity and test whether the resilience
architecture generalises across infrastructure disruption profiles that vary
systematically by proximity to active front lines \cite{UNESCO2023, Striuk_2022}.

\item \textbf{Cross-domain NMT subjects.}
The SE education review \citeauthor{Korniienko2025aredu}~\cite{Korniienko2025aredu} confirmed that gamification
design principles generalise across STEM and humanities disciplines.
The NMT assessment infrastructure (Google Forms, modular control design, scoring
pipeline) is already operationally transferable to Ukrainian Mathematics or
Language and Literature NMT preparation with minimal adaptation.
A 2026--2027 implementation of the same 36-seminar + 7-MC formative--summative
design for a Mathematics NMT preparation cohort would test whether the
factual-recall advantage (bounded topics systematically outperforming comprehensive
assessments) is a domain-general feature of Google Forms gamification or
specific to history's periodisation structure \cite{Wright-Maley2018, Qian2016}.

\item \textbf{LLM-assisted ongoing quality monitoring.}
\citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} established that LLM-assisted MRQS scoring achieves
substantial reliability for factual data extraction (82--97\% agreement) while
achieving only slight agreement for risk-of-bias judgements
($\kappa\,{=}\,{-}0.04$ to 0.21 per the \citealt{LandisKoch1977} scale).
This reliability profile supports a hybrid monitoring architecture for the
growing gamification-in-history literature: automated LLM pre-screening to
identify newly published studies and extract factual descriptors (sample size,
duration, platform type, game mechanics), with human review reserved for
risk-of-bias assessment.
Operationalising this pipeline would transform \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}'s~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}
one-time quality audit into an ongoing field surveillance mechanism, enabling
the detection of quality trends (e.g., whether control group use continues its
$-$13.5~pp decline) in near-real-time \cite{TamimMMRQG2021}.

\end{enumerate}

The three-tier research agenda above --- from foundational measurement fixes through
longitudinal follow-up to scaling --- provides a cumulative programme in which each
tier's outputs directly enable the next.
The five principal findings from the empirical and analytical work of this chapter
are synthesised in Section~\ref{sec:ch3-conclusions}.

\section{Conclusions to Chapter~3} \label{sec:ch3-conclusions}

Chapter~3 has presented results and discussion across four analytical layers:
baseline and formative assessment data (Sections~\ref{sec:baseline}--\ref{sec:formative}),
attendance and wartime context analysis (Section~\ref{sec:attendance}),
and a seven-subsection discussion that positions the findings within the 74-study
gamification evidence base and the nascent digital history pedagogy literature.
Five principal findings emerge.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Longitudinal performance patterns: factual-recall advantage confirmed.}
The 36-seminar series produced a stable, replicable factual-recall advantage:
bounded single-period assessments (Seminar~13: 94.2\%; Seminar~11: 86.2\%;
Seminar~10: 81.0\%) consistently outperformed comprehensive multi-period assessments
(Seminar~30: 33.3\%; Seminar~37: 50.7\%; Seminar~39: 63.5\%), with a mean gap
of $\approx$30 percentage points between peak and trough seminar types.
The 7-modular-control series traces a U-shaped arc: MC2 peak (84.6\%, $N\,{=}\,27$,
single-period content) $\to$ MC5 trough (63.8\%, $N\,{=}\,9$, 65 multi-period
questions) $\to$ partial recovery MC7 (74.3\%, $N\,{=}\,9$).
Within-group standard deviations remained high throughout (14--37\,pp),
confirming that gamification did not reduce performance heterogeneity ---
the four trajectory clusters (Table~\ref{tab:trajectories}: consistent mastery 37.5\%,
topic-variable 25\%, persistent struggle 25\%, progressive decline 12.5\%) coexisted
throughout both course periods, consistent with the meta-analytic finding
\cite{SailerHomner2020, BaiHewHuang2020} that individual learner characteristics
moderate gamification effects more strongly than design features alone.

\item \textbf{Behavioural substitution pattern: bifurcation between voluntary and compulsory engagement.}
The post-gamification period exhibited a structural bifurcation absent from the
pre-gamification period: voluntary seminar participation declined sharply
(mean $N$: 12.9$\,\to\,$5.6; trough at Seminars~28--30: $N\,{=}\,1$--$2$),
while compulsory modular control participation remained stable
($N\,{=}\,7$--$27$ across all 7 controls spanning 7 months).
This bifurcation is consistent with a substitution hypothesis --- that students
shifted to asynchronous HistoryLikeGame practice in lieu of synchronous seminars
--- but the absence of platform analytics prevents confirmation (Section~\ref{sec:future-research}).
The engagement floor --- 17 of 23 enrolled students (73.9\%) participated in at
least one post-gamification assessment --- indicates that the core cohort remained
connected to the course throughout despite wartime infrastructure disruptions.

\item \textbf{Wartime resilience: five-layer architecture maintained compulsory participation.}
The 33 documented absence reports, with 57.5\% attributable to external causes
(internet~24.2\%, family~24.2\%, power~9.1\%), quantify the scope of wartime
infrastructure disruption experienced by the cohort
(Table~\ref{tab:absence-reasons}).
Despite these disruptions, the modular control series maintained participation
across all 7 events (range $N\,{=}\,7$--$27$), confirming that the asynchronous-first,
minimal-bandwidth five-layer architecture (Section~\ref{sec:wartime-context})
achieved its stated design objective: ensuring that no single infrastructure
failure could prevent assessment completion \cite{Burde2017, UNESCO2023}.
The architecture's empirical effectiveness --- compulsory assessment sustained
across 7 months of active armed conflict in the Dnipropetrovsk Oblast ---
constitutes the study's most directly replicable practical contribution to
digital history methodology in conflict-zone settings
\cite{Martynets202428, Zamkova2023299, Hlianenko2024}.

\item \textbf{Methodological positioning: transparent constraint documentation places study in top quality tier.}
The MRQS/ROBIS analysis (Section~3.4.5; Table~\ref{tab:mrqs-items})
positioned the study among the top 16.2\% of the 74-study corpus on overall
risk-of-bias profile, despite structurally imposed constraints (no control group,
$N\,{=}\,23$ below M2 threshold of 30, confounded pre--post comparison).
The study satisfies the six MRQS criteria most frequently absent in the corpus:
explicit gamification definition \cite{Deterding2011}, theoretical framework
(SDT, Flow, TPACK, DigComp~2.0), sample size reported, limitations documented
(12 quantified limitations), data transparency (full pipeline and chat log preserved),
and pre-specified hypotheses (Table~\ref{tab:design-principles-impl}).
Under format-controlled conditions, the aggregate learning outcome signal ---
1.6\,pp between pre-gamification MC1 (77.2\%) and post-gamification MC2--MC7 mean
(75.6\%) --- is the study's most defensible effect estimate, positioned in the
``negligible to small effect'' region ($d\,{\lesssim}\,0.1$--$0.2$) at the lower
bound of the field's $d\,{=}\,0.28$--$0.61$ distribution
\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\item \textbf{Digital history methodology model: all four approach types and all five DigComp areas instantiated.}
The course simultaneously instantiates all four digital history approach types
from the Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} taxonomy: archival-analytical (156 NMT-format
quizzes), collaborative-communicative (400+ Viber messages, networked open
enrollment across 3 waves), immersive-experiential (62 game modules, 10 A-Frame
AR scenes, 13 game-type formats), and creative-productive (thematic quiz naming,
historical narrative integration through period structure).
All five DigComp~2.0 competence areas \cite{Vuorikari2016} are empirically
anchored (Section~\ref{sec:dh-methodology}).
The HistoryLikeGame DBR trajectory (v1$\,\to\,$v2: 23$\,\to\,$62 modules;
1$\,\to\,$13 game types; 205\,KB$\,\to\,$869\,KB) documents the first iterative
practitioner design-based research cycle \cite{Reeves2003} in the digital history
pedagogy literature, embedding the study within the ``pedagogical turn'' identified
in Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} as the field's most recent and consequential development.

\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Responses to secondary research questions.}
The four secondary research questions stated in the Introduction can now be answered:
(SRQ1) Theoretical frameworks in digital humanities pedagogy for professional training
are addressed by Chapter~\ref{ch:theory}: the Vygotsky--Siemens--TPACK--DigComp~2.0
multi-framework model provides the necessary conceptual scaffolding, with each framework
empirically evidenced in the Chapter~3 data.
(SRQ2) Methodological quality gaps are quantified: mean MRQS 4.31/10 (SD\,=\,1.93),
83.8\% high risk of bias, 14.9\% explicit definition --- a quality landscape that
makes the present study's transparent documentation a methodological contribution
independent of its effect-size estimate \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.
(SRQ3) Institutional and wartime conditions affecting access are documented through
the 5-layer resilience architecture, the 33-absence record with external-cause
breakdown, and the enrollment wave pattern showing the open network remaining

(SRQ4) The six gamification design principles (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles})

\textbf{Status of the research hypothesis.}
The primary hypothesis --- that gamification-enhanced digital history methodology
will improve student engagement and learning outcomes --- receives partial empirical
support.
Engagement evidence (73.9\% post-gamification participation rate, stable MC N
despite wartime disruption, consistent core-cohort activity) is consistent with
the 70.3\% engagement-positive field rate \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, but
the mechanisms remain behavioural rather than motivationally confirmed
in the absence of self-report data.
Learning outcome evidence is limited to the 1.6\,pp format-controlled MC gap,
which falls below the field's documented effect-size range and cannot be causally
attributed to gamification without a control group --- a structural constraint
explicitly acknowledged and addressed by the Tier~1 research agenda above.

These findings establish the empirical and methodological groundwork for the
synthesis developed in the dissertation Conclusion, where the
five findings are integrated with the Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} theoretical framework and
the Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} methodological contributions to produce the complete
digital history methodology model for Ukrainian NMT preparation.
