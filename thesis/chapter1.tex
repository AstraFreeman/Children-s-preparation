\chpt{Theoretical foundations of training future specialists using digital history} \label{ch:theory}

\section{Digital history in pedagogical research} \label{sec:dh-review}

Digital history is a modern interdisciplinary approach to the study of history using digital means \cite{ROMEIN, guiliano, Milligan}, involving the creation of digital archives, databases, and multimedia resources to make historical data more accessible and interactive \citep{CohenRosenzweig2006}. The concept emerged in the mid-1990s alongside the World Wide Web \cite{SeefeldtClark2009, NCPH2016}, with Roy Rosenzweig and Daniel Cohen at CHNM (George Mason University) articulating its theoretical foundations \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006}. Since then, digital history has expanded to encompass text mining, GIS, digital storytelling, virtual exhibits, and computational historical analysis \cite{Crymble2021, Graham20151, Bordalejo2025, Lucchesi2019}. Despite this breadth, digital history has not yet received adequate theoretical grounding as a \textit{pedagogical} discipline: most accounts treat it either as a research methodology \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021} or as a subset of digital humanities \cite{Berry2012, Moretti2013}, without articulating the pedagogical frameworks required for effective teaching. This section addresses that gap.

\subsection{Digital history: concept and pedagogical frameworks} \label{subsec:dh-concept}

\textbf{Intellectual genealogy.} \label{subsec:dh-genealogy}
The application of computational methods to historical inquiry spans four eras: \textit{cliometrics} (1960s--1980s), when quantitative methods were first applied to historical questions \cite{Fogel1974}; the \textit{CHNM founding era} (1990s--2000s), when \citeauthor{CohenRosenzweig2006}~\cite{CohenRosenzweig2006} provided the first systematic theoretical framework; the \textit{digital humanities expansion} (2000s--2010s), when computation was recognised as fundamentally altering humanistic knowledge production \cite{Berry2012, Moretti2013, Drucker2011}; and the \textit{pedagogical turn} (2010s--present), when digital history shifted from primarily a research methodology to a subject of teaching and curricular design \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021, guiliano, ROMEIN, Milligan}. Figure~\ref{fig:dh-timeline} (Appendix~\ref{app:F}) visualises this evolution. A complementary subject-of-inquiry periodization identifies three phases: a \textit{statistical period} (1970s--2000s) focused on aggregate patterns \cite{Fogel1974}; a \textit{humanistic period} (2000s--2010s) focused on individual lives and interpretive pluralism \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, Graham20151}; and a \textit{postmodern period} (2010s--present) expanding into pedagogy, public history, and game-based learning \cite{Crymble2021, guiliano, ROMEIN}. This dissertation is situated within the postmodern period and the pedagogical turn.

\textbf{Digital history at the intersection of disciplines.} \label{subsec:dh-intersection}
Digital history pedagogy exists at the convergence of three scholarly domains: History (historical thinking skills~-- sourcing, contextualisation, corroboration, close reading~-- and disciplinary knowledge \cite{Wineburg2001, Seixas2013}), Digital Humanities (computational methods, critical data literacy \cite{Berry2012, Drucker2011, Bordalejo2025}), and Pedagogy (learning theories, assessment frameworks \cite{Vygotsky1978, Siemens2004, Hattie2009}). Their pairwise intersections produce established fields: digital history as research practice (History $\cap$ DH \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, ROMEIN}), history education (History $\cap$ Pedagogy \cite{Wineburg2001, Seixas2013}), and \textbf{digital pedagogy}~-- the study of how digital tools reshape teaching and learning, including gamification as one of its most actively researched instantiations (DH $\cap$ Pedagogy \cite{Vuorikari2016, Voogt20161, Deterding2011}). The triple intersection~-- \textbf{digital history pedagogy}~-- is the subject of this dissertation (Figure~\ref{fig:dh-venn}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}).

Drawing on this model, a taxonomy of four digital history approach types is proposed:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Archival-analytical}: students work with digitised primary sources, databases, and computational text analysis \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159, Crymble2021};
\item \textit{Creative-productive}: students create digital artefacts (videos, exhibitions, websites) that communicate historical narratives \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011, guiliano};
\item \textit{Collaborative-communicative}: students use wikis, blogs, and discussion platforms to co-construct historical knowledge \cite{Soh2013, McLean20144, Graham20151};
\item \textit{Immersive-experiential}: students engage with history through games, simulations, and VR/AR \cite{Connolly2012, Qian2016, Prensky2001}.
\end{enumerate}
The taxonomy presupposes baseline factual knowledge~-- Bloom's \textit{Remember} and \textit{Understand} levels \cite{Anderson2001}. Recent evidence challenges this assumption: NMT history scores of 143.2/200 (2024) and 141.6/200 (2025) \cite{UCEQA2024, UCEQA2025}, rising ЗНО failure rates ($\sim$13\% in 2016--2017 to 18\% in 2021), and a PISA~2022 reading decline of 38~points \cite{OECDPISA2023} indicate that many students lack this foundation \cite{BychkoTereshchenko2023, UNESCO2023}. Gamification addresses precisely this gap: quiz-based mechanics operate at Bloom's lower levels, reinforcing factual knowledge upon which higher-order historical thinking depends (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}). The taxonomy thus represents a \textit{second stage} that first secures factual foundations through gamified practice, then builds toward the competences the four types describe. Of these, gamification within the immersive-experiential category has attracted the most research attention (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}).

\textbf{Pedagogical frameworks.} \label{subsec:dh-frameworks}
Four theoretical perspectives underpin the methodology. \textit{Constructivism} \cite{Vygotsky1978}: digital tools serve as Vygotskian scaffolding enabling students to engage with historical sources beyond their unaided capacity; the gamified quiz system functions as a mediating tool supporting progression through increasingly complex tasks. \textit{TPACK} \cite{Mishra2006, Koehler2013}: effective technology integration requires the intersection of content, pedagogical, and technological knowledge~-- a framework the systematic review below confirms as a key barrier when absent. \textit{DigComp~2.0} \cite{Vuorikari2016}: provides a structured competence taxonomy for specifying learning outcomes and aligning assessment with digital competence development (Section~\ref{sec:digcomp}). \textit{Connectivism} \cite{Siemens2004}: learning is distributed across networks, as exemplified by the course chat log analysed in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. These frameworks operate within the Ukrainian context, where the Bologna process drives competence-based reform \cite{UNESCO2023} while the full-scale invasion since February 2022 creates unprecedented challenges for distance learning~-- making this study among the first to examine digital history pedagogy under active wartime conditions.

\subsection{Systematic review of digital history pedagogy} \label{subsec:dh-systematic}

A systematic review following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines \citep{Page2021} was conducted to answer: \textit{How effective is teaching digital history to students compared to non-digital methods?} \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The Scopus database was searched using \texttt{TITLE-ABS-KEY("digital history" AND student*)}, limited to English-language publications (2011--2025). Studies were included if they involved undergraduate or master's-level students in history courses, implemented a digital technology intervention, and employed empirical methods. The initial search yielded 46 records; after title-abstract screening (28 excluded) and full-text assessment of 15 studies (9 excluded; 2 unretrievable), 6 studies met all criteria. Claude~4 Opus and Claude~4 Sonnet served as auxiliary screening tools, with all final decisions made by human reviewers \cite{MalekiKarami2024, Taneri2025}. Data were extracted by three reviewers simultaneously (two LLMs and one human researcher), with discrepancies resolved by the human reviewer \cite{Rubinstein2025}. The studies represent diverse geographical contexts (USA: 3, Australia, New Zealand, Canada: 1 each), with sample sizes from 27 to $\sim$200 participants. Only one study \cite{Soh2013} included a control group. Interventions spanned digital storytelling \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011}, digital exhibition creation \cite{Davis2017}, personal digital histories \cite{Lee2014159}, wiki/blog-based projects \cite{McLean20144}, and collaborative wiki writing \cite{Soh2013}.

\textbf{Synthesis of findings.} All six studies reported positive engagement effects. Historical knowledge acquisition was assessed in four studies, with three reporting positive effects; \citeauthor{Soh2013}~\cite{Soh2013} provided the strongest evidence (quasi-experimental, 340\% increase in peer interactions, 40\% more time on digital assignments). Three mechanisms drove positive outcomes \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}: \textit{multimodal representation} \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011}; the \textit{authentic audience effect}, where public visibility motivated higher-quality work \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159}; and \textit{technological affordances} enabling approaches impractical in analog environments \cite{Soh2013, McLean20144}. Implementation challenges included technical difficulties consuming instructional time \cite{McLean20144}, insufficient instructor TPACK \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159}, and inadequate traditional assessment methods \cite{Coleborne2011}.

\textbf{Implications.} The gap between digital history practice and pedagogical research is explained by two structural factors: the field's orientation as a research methodology rather than a teaching approach \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021, CohenRosenzweig2006}, and the interdisciplinary TPACK demands on instructors who must integrate historical, computational, and pedagogical knowledge simultaneously \cite{Mishra2006, Bordalejo2025, Lucchesi2019}. Additional barriers include the absence of standardised outcome measures \cite{Hattie2009, MonteSano2012} and of a unified competence framework bridging digital and historical reasoning \cite{Wineburg2001, Vuorikari2016}~-- challenges this dissertation addresses. The scarcity of pedagogical research motivates the examination of gamification in history education in the following section: 74 studies compared to 6 \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, DichevDicheva2017}.

\section{Gamification in history education: definitions, effectiveness, and design principles} \label{sec:gamification-review}

\subsection{Background and rationale}

Given that the systematic review of digital history pedagogy identified only 6 empirical studies (Section~\ref{sec:dh-review}), this section examines gamification in history education~-- the most extensively researched subset~-- to build the evidence base that digital history pedagogy currently lacks. Gamification~-- ``the use of game design elements in non-game contexts'' \cite{Deterding2011}~-- exists within a continuum spanning structural gamification (points, badges, leaderboards added to unchanged content), content gamification (narrative and quest elements), game-based learning \cite{Connolly2012, Prensky2001}, and serious games \cite{SEABORN201514} (Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-spectrum}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}). This dissertation proposes an additional spectral position~-- \textbf{reward-bridged learning} (RBL)~-- where an entertainment game serves as the primary context and educational micro-tasks are integrated as optional pathways to in-game rewards \cite{RBL2025}. Unlike tangential learning \cite{frith2021} (implicit, self-directed) and stealth learning \cite{ShuteFormative2008} (invisible), RBL is explicit, voluntary, and reward-driven, reversing \citeauthor{Deterding2011}'s assumed directionality of game--education integration \cite{12Gilbert2019108}.

\textbf{Theoretical foundations.} \label{sec:theory}
Four perspectives explain gamification's mechanisms (Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-theory}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}). \textit{Self-Determination Theory} \cite{RyanDeci2000}: gamification elements map onto three innate needs~-- autonomy (choice mechanics), competence (progression feedback), and relatedness (social comparison)~-- and designs supporting all three promote sustained motivation, while extrinsic-only approaches may undermine it \cite{Sailer2017, Gupta2022341}. \textit{Flow Theory} \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990}: the balance between challenge and skill, supported by clear goals and immediate feedback, sustains engagement with cognitively demanding historical content \cite{Ho2024139}. The \textit{MDA Framework} \cite{Hunicke2004}: instructors design mechanics (points, quests, narratives), but learning emerges from dynamics~-- the unpredictable interactions between learners and game elements \cite{Bozic2024538}. \textit{Behaviourist reinforcement}: immediate rewards and variable reinforcement schedules establish engagement, but risk the overjustification effect when extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation \cite{Nunez-Naranjo2024106, Prieto-Andreu2024244}.

The author's systematic review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} identified 74 studies~-- a twelve-fold increase over the digital history review~-- providing the empirical foundation for the analysis below.

\subsection{Methodology}

The systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines \citep{Page2021}, addressing four questions: definitions and conceptualisations, effectiveness evidence, design principles and technologies, and challenges and limitations. Three databases were searched (Scopus \cite{scopus}, Web of Science \cite{wos}, Dimensions \cite{dimensions}):

\begin{quote}
\texttt{TITLE (history AND (game* OR gamification) AND (education OR teaching OR learning OR student* OR instruction))}
\end{quote}

Limits were applied for time frame (2000--2024), language (English or Ukrainian), and publication type (peer-reviewed articles, conference papers, book chapters).

\textbf{Inclusion criteria:} (1) students in primary, secondary, or higher education learning history as a discipline; (2) gamification techniques, elements, or strategies applied in history education; (3) empirical studies using quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods; (4) formal or informal educational settings; (5) published between 2000 and 2024; (6) indexed in Scopus, Web of Science, or Dimensions.

\textbf{Exclusion criteria:} (1) studies not focused on history as a discipline (e.g., history of mathematics, history of science); (2) secondary research (systematic reviews, meta-analyses); (3) studies with no gamification intervention; (4) non-empirical publications (theoretical essays, editorials, position papers).

\textbf{Screening process.} The initial search yielded 4,011 records across the three databases \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. After duplicate removal and title-abstract screening, 80 potentially relevant studies were retained for full-text assessment. During full-text analysis, 6 studies were excluded against the exclusion criteria: 3 were not about history as a discipline (violating exclusion criterion~1), 1 was a systematic review rather than a primary study (criterion~2), 1 addressed the history of mathematics rather than history education (criterion~1), and 1 contained no gamification intervention (criterion~3). The final corpus comprised 74 studies published between 2004 and 2024 across 27 countries. Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-prisma} presents the complete selection process.

\subsection{Definitional analysis} \label{sec:definitional-analysis}

Of the 74 included studies, only 11 (14.9\%) provided explicit definitions; the remaining 85.1\% used ``gamification'' without operational specification, and several conflated it with game-based learning or serious games \cite{36Scholz2021, 42Huffer201581}. Definitional approaches differed by disciplinary orientation: educational technology studies defined gamification narrowly around mechanics \cite{Deterding2011, SEABORN201514}, history education studies conceptualised it broadly as experiential learning \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 55Squire2004}, and computer science studies emphasised system design \cite{Souza2018201}. This fragmentation directly impedes synthesis \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Buckley2018, Calderon2016}: studies providing explicit definitions demonstrate higher reporting quality (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}), while the declining definitional rate over time (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}) suggests growth without conceptual consolidation. \textbf{Conceptual framings and approaches.} \label{sec:conceptual-approaches}
The 11 explicit definitions cluster into three framings \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Titova2025cte}: \textit{game mechanics overlay} (game elements layered onto unchanged activities \cite{Deterding2011}); \textit{serious games} (purpose-built games as the pedagogical tool \cite{42Huffer201581, 41Petersen2023301}); and \textit{educational integration} (game/pedagogy boundary blurred \cite{30Zin2008, 65Razak201139}). Figure~\ref{fig:definitional-framings} visualises this continuum. These framings map onto four implementation approaches identified in the corpus: structural gamification (points/badges added to existing content \cite{Humeniuk2022113}), content gamification (narratives, quests \cite{43ali2021indonesian}), full gamification (comprehensive MDA integration \cite{36Scholz2021, Sailer2017}), and game-based learning (complete games as pedagogical tools \cite{Qian2016, Prensky2001}).

Beyond definitions, five pedagogical lenses recurred across the corpus \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}: immersive experiential learning, student-centred engagement, historical empathy and perspective-taking, problem-solving and inquiry, and visualisation. Notably, four of these five are generic educational constructs; only historical empathy engages the discipline's epistemological traditions \cite{Wineburg2001}. \subsection{Characteristics of the evidence base} \label{sec:evidence-base}

The 74 included studies display a characteristic geographic, temporal, and methodological profile that shapes the interpretation of effectiveness findings.

\textbf{Geographic distribution.}
Research was conducted across 27 countries but was highly concentrated: Taiwan (n=13, 17.6\%), the United States (n=11, 14.9\%), Malaysia (n=9, 12.2\%), and Indonesia (n=8, 10.8\%) together accounted for 55.4\% of the total (Table~\ref{tab:country-distribution}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}). By macro-region, East and Southeast Asia contributed 43.2\%, Western Europe 33.8\%, North America 23.0\%, and the rest of the world combined $<$10\%~-- proportions mirroring broader EdTech research patterns \cite{Bond2020}. The Global South (Latin America, Africa, Middle East, South Asia) was almost entirely absent. Notably, no studies from Ukraine or other post-Soviet states were identified \cite{UNESCO2023}; the present study addresses this geographic gap \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\textbf{Temporal distribution.} \label{par:temporal-distribution}
Studies spanned 2004--2024 (each half contributing 37 studies), with peak years in 2011, 2014, 2018, and 2023 (7--8 studies each). Bibliometric keyword analysis reveals three temporal stages: (1)~\textit{early exploration} (2004--2012), focused on foundational questions about computer games for history learning \cite{55Squire2004, 54Gibson2007, 49Huizenga2007127, 30Zin2008}; (2)~\textit{narrative and engagement} (2013--2017), shifting toward game narratives for historical empathy \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 59Kee2014270, 4Cruz2017}; and (3)~\textit{immersive technology} (2018--2024), adopting VR/AR and gamification \cite{10Huaman2019220, 6Czerwinski2018, 32Cheng20241718, Alexov2023}. This periodisation receives external validation from \cite{Shen2025}, whose 29-year bibliometric analysis of 249 publications identified a similar trajectory.

\textbf{Publication types and evidence maturity.}
Conference papers dominated (n=40, 54.1\%), followed by journal articles (n=24, 32.4\%), book chapters (n=9, 12.2\%), and one preprint (1.4\%). The journal proportion rose from 28\% in 2004--2012 to 46\% in 2018--2024, indicating gradual maturation (Figure~\ref{fig:publication-type-evolution}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}). The persistent conference dominance contrasts with established fields such as medical education, where journal articles dominate \cite{vanGaalen2021}, and reflects the relative youth of this research area. Combined with the definitional instability documented in Section~\ref{sec:definitional-analysis} and the near-absence of RCTs (below), this indicates a \textit{pre-consolidation phase} of evidence development \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\textbf{Education levels.}
Secondary education was the most studied context (n=35, 47.3\%), followed by higher education (n=21, 28.4\%) and primary education (n=13, 17.6\%) \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 11Ternblad2023115, 15Slater2022}. Gamification findings from secondary settings cannot be straightforwardly generalised to higher education due to differences in motivation profiles and assessment traditions \cite{Ahlfeld2021493}. The present dissertation's NMT preparatory course occupies a unique position at the secondary-to-tertiary transition~-- a population underrepresented in both subcorpora. A cross-tabulation of education level against game type (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-cross-dimensions}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}) reveals that secondary studies disproportionately employ board games and commercial titles, higher education studies concentrate on VR/AR and custom digital games, and primary studies favour web-based and mobile formats~-- suggesting that positive engagement findings may partly reflect format-context matching rather than a universal gamification effect.

\textbf{Game types and technological formats.}
The interventions employed diverse formats (Table~\ref{tab:game-types}): VR/AR games led (n=19, 25.7\%) \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 10Huaman2019220, 6Czerwinski2018}, followed by board/card games (n=11, 14.9\%), custom digital games (n=10, 13.5\%), web-based games (n=10, 13.5\%), and commercial off-the-shelf titles (n=8, 10.8\%) such as \textit{Civilization} \cite{55Squire2004} and \textit{Assassin's Creed} \cite{12Gilbert2019108}. Digital formats accounted for approximately 84\% of the corpus, creating a bias toward technology-dependent interventions. The present dissertation's web-based/mobile hybrid format, requiring no specialised hardware, addresses this accessibility gap \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\textbf{Research designs.} \label{sec:research-designs}

\textbf{Theoretical frameworks and disciplinary grounding.}
The theoretical frameworks cited across the corpus fall into three tiers (Figure~\ref{fig:theoretical-framework-tiers}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}): Tier~1~-- generic educational theories (constructivism, experiential learning, collaborative learning, situated learning: ${\approx}$34 citations); Tier~2~-- technology and game-specific frameworks (GBL, flow theory, motivation theories, TAM, SDT: ${\approx}$50 citations); and Tier~3~-- history-discipline-specific frameworks (historical thinking, historical empathy: only 7 citations, 9.5\%). The \textbf{34:50:7 ratio} reveals that gamification in history education is overwhelmingly theorised from \textit{outside} the history discipline \cite{Hew2019, Sailer2017, Hamari2014}. The consequences are threefold: outcome measures default to generic engagement metrics rather than historical thinking competences (only 18.9\% measured higher-order reasoning, Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}); game design prioritises generic mechanics over history-specific affordances; and the evidence base cannot answer whether gamification develops the competences distinguishing history from other subjects. The 7 studies engaging historical thinking frameworks \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 56Akkerman2009449, 55Squire2004, 78Rouse2022357} report qualitatively different findings~-- assessing empathy, source evaluation, and perspective-taking~-- suggesting that theoretical grounding actively shapes what research can discover. The experimental design in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} addresses this Tier~3 deficit by grounding gamification in historical thinking competences \cite{Wineburg2001, MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}.

\textbf{Cross-domain comparison.}
A parallel systematic review of gamification in software engineering education \citep{Korniienko2025aredu} (29 studies) found consistent cross-domain patterns: predominantly positive engagement effects with mixed learning outcomes ($d = 0.23$), small samples, lack of control groups, and weak definitional practices (14.9\% explicit definitions in history vs. ${\approx}$30\% in software engineering). In health professions, \cite{vanGaalen2021} found no negative outcomes (vs. 10.8\% in history) and better outcome reporting. \citeauthor{Dehghanzadeh2024}~\cite{Dehghanzadeh2024} found similar limitations across 54 K-12 gamification studies, suggesting these are structural features of the gamification evidence base rather than discipline-specific shortcomings \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019, Korniienko2025aredu}.

\textbf{Evidence base maturity profile.} \label{sec:evidence-maturity}
Synthesising the preceding indicators into a diagnostic maturity profile (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-maturity-profile}), the corpus exhibits uniformly low scores: four countries account for 55\% of studies (geographic diversity: low); conference papers constitute 54.1\% (journal dominance: low); only 24.3\% support causal inference (experimental designs: low); only 33.9\% exceed $n = 30$ (adequate samples: low); and only 9.5\% of theoretical citations engage the history discipline (disciplinary theory: very low). These indicators are not independent: geographic concentration correlates with VR/AR dominance, conference papers associate with lower rigour (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}), and small underpowered samples cannot produce causal evidence in the GRADE sense \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}. The field produces encouraging signals but not causal evidence \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}.

\input{figures/evidence_maturity_profile}

Three priorities emerge: (1)~geographic diversification, particularly in post-Soviet, African, and Latin American contexts; (2)~methodological strengthening through RCTs, validated instruments, and effect size reporting; and (3)~disciplinary integration through historical thinking frameworks \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The present dissertation addresses all three. These characteristics motivate the methodological quality assessment in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs} and the experimental methodology in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Effectiveness findings} \label{sec:effectiveness}

\textbf{Outcome types and the ease-of-measurement hierarchy.}
The review synthesised effectiveness evidence across multiple outcome categories \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The most frequently measured outcome was student engagement (52 studies, 70.3\%), followed by knowledge acquisition (45 studies, 60.8\%), attitudes and satisfaction (39 studies, 52.7\%), and motivation (26 studies, 35.1\%). Historical thinking skills were assessed in only 14 studies (18.9\%) \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 56Akkerman2009449, 55Squire2004, 78Rouse2022357, 59Kee2014270}; flow and immersion in 15 \cite{16Chen201447, 19Chou2019, 70Hou201519, 28Chao201734}. This distribution reflects a structural \textit{ease-of-measurement hierarchy} (Figure~\ref{fig:outcome-taxonomy}): outcome frequency inversely correlates with measurement difficulty. Engagement is operationalised through platform analytics and time-on-task; historical thinking requires discipline-specific instruments and expertise in historical epistemology \cite{MonteSano2012, Seixas2013}. The field thus measures what is methodologically convenient rather than what matters most~-- only 18.9\% of studies assess discipline-specific outcomes, compared to 91\% in health professions gamification \cite{vanGaalen2021}. This gap is linked to the 9.5\% disciplinary theory engagement rate (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}).

\input{figures/outcome_taxonomy}

\textbf{Overall effects and the engagement-learning decoupling.}
Of 74 studies, 12 (16.2\%) reported exclusively positive results, 37 (50.0\%) mixed, 18 (24.3\%) null or negative, and 7 (9.5\%) did not measure outcomes quantitatively \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, DichevDicheva2017, Clark2016}. Cross-tabulating by domain reveals the section's central finding~-- the \textit{engagement-learning decoupling}: engagement outcomes were positive in approximately 85\% of studies, attitudes in 84\%, but learning outcomes in only 51.4\%. This $\sim$19~pp gap demonstrates that gamification reliably increases participation but does not automatically transfer to learning gains. Meta-analytic evidence confirms the pattern: \citeauthor{SailerHomner2020}~\cite{SailerHomner2020} found significant effects on cognitive ($g = 0.49$) and motivational ($g = 0.36$) outcomes but with substantial heterogeneity ($I^2 > 80\%$); \cite{BaiHewHuang2020} confirmed positive but heterogeneous effects across 30 studies. Most ``mixed'' studies reported positive engagement alongside null learning~-- engagement is a necessary but insufficient condition for learning without explicit pedagogical scaffolding. The 10.8\% negative rate exceeds the 0\% in health professions \cite{vanGaalen2021}, and the history-specific negative effects~-- misconceptions, ideological bias, fact-fiction confusion~-- have no parallel in other disciplines (Section~\ref{sec:harm-typology}).

\textbf{Engagement, motivation, and affective outcomes.}
The majority of studies ($n = 52$, 70.3\%) reported positive engagement effects, including increased voluntary participation, longer time-on-task, and higher self-reported motivation. Key findings include: correlation between perceived reality and engagement ($r = .70$, $p < .001$) in VR \cite{1Barbatsis201159}; mean engagement scores above 5/7 across VR studies \cite{10Huaman2019220, 31DeAmicis2009451}; satisfaction averaging 4.51/5 \cite{64Hsu201324}; and a significant gender difference ($F(1,64) = 4.408$, $p = .040$) with boys showing higher engagement \cite{49Huizenga2007127}. However, sustainability beyond novelty periods was rarely examined \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Hamari2014}, approximately 10\% of participants responded negatively \cite{47Clara2010156}, and competitive formats generated stress \cite{11Ternblad2023115}.

Affective outcomes (attitude, enjoyment, self-efficacy) were positive in 83.8\% of reporting studies ($n = 31/37$), strongest in narrative-rich environments \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 74Fendt2019, 29Stiso20201165}. A large-scale study ($N = 2{,}417$) identified five engagement profiles through latent class analysis, with 15--20\% showing neutral or negative attitudinal shifts \cite{15Slater2022}. SEM analysis confirmed that perceived usefulness and epistemological sophistication predict positive attitudes \cite{45Yu2015727}, suggesting a potential Matthew effect: students who already think critically benefit more from gamification.

\textbf{Learning outcomes and effect sizes.}
Academic achievement results were more mixed: 38 studies (51.4\%) reported positive learning effects, 28 (37.8\%) no significant difference, and 8 (10.8\%) negative or mixed effects \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, 47Clara2010156, Oceja2022419}. Table~\ref{tab:significant-learning} presents all 16 studies reporting inferential statistics; only 7 included control groups. Effect sizes ranged from $d = 0.28$ to $d = 0.61$, with stronger effects for factual recall than interpretive understanding.

Against Kraft's \cite{Kraft2020} benchmarks for scalable interventions ($d > 0.20$ = ``large''), these effects are substantial; against Hattie's \cite{Hattie2009} hinge point ($d = 0.40$), they cluster near or above the threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:effect-size-forest}). Gamification appears more effective for surface-level knowledge than for deep historical thinking \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}.

\textbf{Flow, historical thinking, and other disciplinary outcomes.}
Eight studies measured flow states using validated instruments \cite{16Chen201447, 19Chou2019, 28Chao201734, 44Shiue2016, 45Yu2015727, 70Hou201519, 64Hsu201324, 67Lin201296}. Concentration and immersion scored highest across studies, while loss of self-consciousness consistently scored lowest ($M = 3.29$--$3.68$) \cite{63Liu2021, 64Hsu201324, 16Chen201447}~-- suggesting ``partial flow'' \cite{16Chen201447} compatible with the reflective processing that historical thinking requires. Only one study \cite{45Yu2015727} found a significant flow-learning correlation; flow therefore functions as an engagement amplifier requiring pedagogical scaffolding to translate into understanding.

Of 18 studies examining historical thinking, only 14 (18.9\%) used validated instruments \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Seixas2013}. Assessed competences cluster around perspective-taking (7 studies) and source analysis (5), while causal reasoning (2) is nearly absent. Game type determines which competences develop: narrative role-playing produces empathy gains \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 78Rouse2022357, 56Akkerman2009449}; document-based games improve source analysis \cite{59Kee2014270, 60Hoy2018}; strategy games develop systemic thinking \cite{55Squire2004, 40Kuran2018}. This pattern maps onto the causal pathways: narrative games (Path~B) develop empathy, document games (Path~D) develop source analysis, competitive quiz games (Path~C) develop neither. The 18.9\% assessment rate reflects the theoretical framework deficit (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}): theory drives measurement.

\textbf{Harm typology and the four causal pathways.} \label{sec:harm-typology}
The synthesis reveals four distinct causal pathways mediating gamification effects, each with characteristic outcomes and risks (Figure~\ref{fig:causal-pathways}).

\input{figures/causal_pathways}

\textit{Path~A (Flow):} Game complexity generates flow states sustaining attention; overload degrades to no learning \cite{16Chen201447, 63Liu2021, 64Hsu201324, 70Hou201519, 48Moketar2021, 45Yu2015727}. \textit{Path~B (Narrative):} Story and role-play create emotional connections promoting historical empathy; uncritical immersion produces fact-fiction confusion \cite{71lynch2015pedagogical, 61Karsenti202027, 12Gilbert2019108}. \textit{Path~C (Competition):} Points and leaderboards increase task completion but typically produce surface-level learning; competition creates gender-differentiated stress ($F(1,64) = 4.408$, $p = .040$; \citealt{8Yu2014}) and explains the engagement-without-learning pattern. \textit{Path~D (Source interaction):} Document-based games develop source evaluation and historical thinking but risk cognitive overload \cite{52Lin2011265, 54Gibson2007, 41Petersen2023301}; this pathway produces the deepest learning but appears in only 18.9\% of studies.

The framework explains the engagement-learning decoupling: Paths~A, B, and D produce deep learning when scaffolded, but Path~C~-- the most commonly implemented~-- generates engagement without learning transfer. Of the 74 studies, 60 (81.1\%) reported at least one negative effect \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, systematised in Figure~\ref{fig:harm-typology} as: \textit{cognitive harms} (13 studies: overload, distraction, misconceptions \cite{52Lin2011265, 54Gibson2007, 71lynch2015pedagogical, 61Karsenti202027}); \textit{affective harms} (8 studies: stress, emotional distress, frustration \cite{34Serrano2023, 78Rouse2022357, 55Squire2004}); \textit{equity harms} (6 studies: gender disparities, reading-level effects \cite{8Yu2014, 15Slater2022}); and \textit{epistemic harms} (5 studies: ideological bias, historical inaccuracies \cite{46Stirling2022, 61Karsenti202027}). The epistemic harms are unique to history education~-- arising because history games make claims about real events \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Connolly2012}~-- elevating teacher mediation from a desirable addition to a necessary condition.

\input{figures/harm_landscape}

The synthesis yields a design principle: effectiveness depends on \textit{alignment} between game mechanics, learning outcomes, and assessment. Path~C paired with historical thinking goals represents poor alignment; Path~D paired with source analysis represents strong alignment. This principle informs the experimental methodology in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, where gamification elements activate Paths~B and D while mitigating Path~C risks.

\subsection{Implementation challenges} \label{sec:implementation-challenges}

The review identified five categories of implementation barriers (Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-frequency}): student-related challenges (44 studies, 59.5\%), pedagogical and curriculum integration difficulties (38, 51.4\%), technical and infrastructure constraints (30, 40.5\%), resource limitations (13, 17.6\%), and time constraints (8, 10.8\%). Following \citeauthor{Ertmer1999}'s~\cite{Ertmer1999} taxonomy, these can be organised into first-order barriers (external: technology, resources, time) and second-order barriers (internal: teacher beliefs, pedagogical knowledge, willingness to change). The corpus overwhelmingly documents first-order barriers while under-reporting second-order ones, yet the latter determine whether first-order resources are used effectively \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-stakeholder-matrix} (Appendix~\ref{app:F}) maps these barrier categories against the stakeholder levels at which they operate, revealing a response mismatch: the most frequently reported barriers require systemic responses, while the most frequently addressed barriers are tractable at the individual level.

\input{figures/barrier_frequency}

\textbf{Technical barriers and resource constraints.} The 30 studies reporting technical barriers cluster into three tiers: hardware (AR/VR device costs and recognition failures) \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 49Huizenga2007127, 6Czerwinski2018, 73Wolfartsberger2011160}, software (steep learning curves for Unity/Unreal; browser compatibility issues) \cite{63Liu2021, 14Forsyth2023}, and connectivity (limited internet constraining real-time features) \cite{77Ramansyah2021, 22Nugroho2020149}. A technology-access gradient emerges: VR/AR implementations (25.7\% of corpus) demand the highest infrastructure, while web-based and mobile formats (13.5\%) demand the lowest \cite{58Shih2015173, 48Moketar2021}. The evidence base is geographically biased toward well-resourced contexts (Taiwan, USA, Malaysia, and Indonesia account for 55.4\% of studies), systematically under-representing barriers in under-resourced settings. The present study's wartime Ukrainian context (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}) represents an extreme case, where power outages and internet disruptions caused 33.3\% of documented absences \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Resource constraints generate a \textit{scalability tension}: custom-designed, multi-modal implementations produce the strongest pedagogy but are the most resource-intensive, hardest to scale, and least amenable to rigorous research with adequate sample sizes \cite{50Lye2013, 9Lin2018, Gasca-Hurtado2016}. The present study's hub-and-spoke static HTML platform (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) attempts to resolve this through design that minimises both development and deployment costs.

\textbf{Pedagogical barriers.} Effective gamification requires the intersection of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge formalised as TPACK \cite{Mishra2006, Koehler2013}. Yet only 4 of 74 studies addressed teacher training needs, while 38 (51.4\%) reported curriculum integration difficulties~-- suggesting the teacher is the under-studied bottleneck. When teachers lack TPACK, gamification degrades from pedagogical strategy to technological novelty \cite{Mercader2020}. Studies with substantial instructor training or co-design reported better outcomes \cite{61Karsenti202027, 36Scholz2021, Buisman2014}; conversely, insufficient preparation produced implementation failures \cite{60Hoy2018, 4Cruz2017}. Student heterogeneity compounds the challenge: the largest barrier category (44 studies, 59.5\%) documents variation in prior gaming experience \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 6Czerwinski2018}, motivational response (approximately 10\% of students respond negatively to gamification \cite{47Clara2010156}), and demographic moderators including gender ($F(1,64) = 4.408$, $p = .040$ for motivation; \citealt{8Yu2014}), reading level \cite{15Slater2022}, and cultural background \cite{66Rahmawati2023163}. Different students activate different causal pathways (Section~\ref{sec:harm-typology}), producing divergent outcomes from identical game designs.

\textbf{Institutional barriers and synthesis.} Most implementations adopted a \textit{bolt-on} approach~-- adding game elements to existing curricula~-- generating friction as game activities compete for time, disrupt assessment rhythms, and require scheduling flexibility that rigid timetables rarely permit \cite{61Karsenti202027, 57Huntley2011567, 49Huizenga2007127}. An assessment mismatch compounds this: gamification develops competences (collaboration, empathy, perspective-taking) that traditional instruments cannot measure \cite{3Watson2011466, 4Cruz2017}. Long-term sustainability remains unstudied; most implementations are single-site pilots with no replication or follow-up \cite{47Clara2010156, 2Mahamarowi2023133}. As Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-interaction-model} shows, these barriers form a reinforcing cycle: technical constraints increase resource costs; resource constraints eliminate training budgets; untrained teachers cannot adapt curricula; rigid curricula cannot accommodate diverse student profiles \cite{Ertmer1999, Mercader2020}. This systems-level view identifies an \textit{implementation threshold} below which barrier interactions amplify each other, reframing implementation from a checklist to a systems problem requiring coordinated investment. The present study addresses specific barriers: lightweight technology (static HTML, Viber) mitigates first-order technical barriers; instructor-designed content resolves the build-versus-buy trade-off; and voluntary participation within a non-formal course removes some institutional constraints. The wartime Ukrainian context of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} transforms implementation barriers into dynamic, fluctuating variables~-- an empirical situation without precedent in the existing literature.

\subsection{Game design elements and technological platforms} \label{sec:game-elements}

\textbf{DMC framework and the disciplinary element profile.} \label{sec:dmc-mapping} The 74 studies employ 12 categories of game design elements (Table~\ref{tab:game-elements}), dominated by immersion/VR/AR (55 studies, 74.3\%), narrative/storytelling (44, 59.5\%), and role-playing (44, 59.5\%). Mapping these onto \citeauthor{Werbach2012}'s \cite{Werbach2012} three-level DMC framework~-- Dynamics (big-picture patterns: narrative arcs, progression), Mechanics (processes: challenges, feedback loops), and Components (specific instantiations: points, badges, leaderboards)~-- yields a striking ``top-heavy'' profile (Figure~\ref{fig:dmc-pyramid}). Dynamics-level elements dominate with 117 cumulative study-element instances, mechanics-level with 138, and components-level with only 48 \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. This structural inversion is unique to history education: in software engineering education, points dominate (58.6\%) while narrative elements rarely exceed 15\% \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}; general gamification meta-reviews consistently identify points and rewards as dominant, approaching 80\% \cite{Hamari2014, SEABORN201514, Souza2018201}. The inversion constitutes a \textit{disciplinary signature} explained by \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} Theory of Gamified Learning: game elements \textit{moderate} the relationship between instruction and outcomes, and the moderating elements must engage discipline-specific cognitive processes~-- in history, empathy, multiperspectivity, and temporal reasoning, which are inherently narrative and experiential \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Hong2024}. The profile explains both the field's pedagogical richness and its high implementation barriers, since dynamics-level elements require more design expertise than concrete components \cite{Werbach2012, Toda2019}. \textbf{Synthesis: from elements to learning outcomes.} Game design in history education requires assembling coherent configurations aligned with disciplinary epistemology rather than selecting individual elements from a menu. \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory provides the unifying interpretation: game elements function as moderators of the relationship between instructional quality and learning outcomes. A poorly designed history lesson with points remains a poor lesson; a well-designed source analysis activity with narrative scaffolding can produce genuine historical thinking \cite{Werbach2012, Toda2019, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Narrative and immersive elements moderate \textit{the right cognitive processes} for history, whereas points moderate effort and persistence~-- useful but insufficient for disciplinary understanding \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}. Collaborative learning was the most frequent instructional strategy (46 studies, 62.2\%), followed by narrative-driven instruction (40, 54.1\%) and assessment-integrated approaches (31, 41.9\%) \cite{3Watson2011466, 12Gilbert2019108, 19Chou2019}; constructivist approaches were \textit{under-operationalised} (only 15 studies, 20.3\%, despite the prevalence of constructivist-enabling elements) \cite{Constructivism}. The experimental methodology in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} deliberately selects the Competitive Quizzer archetype (quiz-based, point-driven, Viber-delivered) as a test of whether lightweight gamification can produce meaningful effects in resource-constrained conditions~-- trading dynamics-level richness for components-level scalability.

\subsection{Design principles} \label{sec:design-principles}

The systematic review corpus \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} yields an inventory of implementation features associated with positive outcomes, synthesised below into a principled design framework: the principles' selection rationale (Section~\ref{sec:principle-selection}), theoretical grounding (Section~\ref{sec:principle-grounding}), inter-principle interactions and archetype alignment (Section~\ref{sec:principle-interactions}), and implications for the experimental design (Section~\ref{sec:principle-implications}).

\textbf{From evidence to principles: selection rationale.} \label{sec:principle-selection}

The systematic review identified 12 candidate design principles from studies reporting positive outcomes: (1)~historical authenticity, (2)~narrative integration, (3)~progressive difficulty, (4)~immediate feedback, (5)~social elements, (6)~multiple modalities, (7)~interactivity, (8)~agency/choice, (9)~clear objectives, (10)~meaningful rewards, (11)~accessibility, and (12)~replayability \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, LaineLindberg2020}. While all 12 appear in at least one effective implementation, their frequency and generalisability vary considerably.

Three criteria guided the distillation from 12 to 6 retained principles. First, \textit{positive-outcome frequency}: the principle must be implemented in more than 40\% of studies reporting positive effects on engagement or learning. Second, \textit{cross-archetype applicability}: the principle must appear in at least three of the five design archetypes identified in Section~\ref{sec:archetypes}. Third, \textit{operationalisability}: the principle must be implementable in resource-constrained settings without specialist development tools \cite{Hong2024, Toda2019}.

The six excluded principles fail one or more criteria. Interactivity is subsumed by multiple modalities and social elements; agency/choice is subsumed by narrative integration (where quests provide structured autonomy); clear objectives constitute an implementation condition rather than a design principle per se \cite{LaineLindberg2020}. Meaningful rewards are subsumed by historical authenticity~-- in history education, ``meaningful'' necessarily means ``historically authentic'' \cite{Nicholson2015}. Accessibility is an implementation condition addressed in Section~\ref{sec:guidelines}. Replayability appears in only 8.1\% of studies, rendering it too infrequent to qualify as a general principle \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

Existing reviews present design principles without explaining why certain principles are prioritised over others or how they relate to specific disciplinary contexts.

\textbf{The six principles with theoretical grounding.} \label{sec:principle-grounding}

Each retained principle is presented below with its empirical evidence, theoretical mechanism(s) drawn from the frameworks in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, history-specific manifestation, and violation evidence. Figure~\ref{fig:principle-theoretical-grounding} provides a matrix view of the theoretical grounding.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Historical authenticity.} The most effective implementations maintained historical accuracy while incorporating game elements \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 1Barbatsis201159, 51Ardito2007}. Superficial gamification that trivialised historical content was associated with negative outcomes in 10.8\% of studies \cite{47Clara2010156, Oceja2022419}. Authenticity encompasses factual accuracy, appropriate representation of historical complexity, ambiguity, and multiple perspectives \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}. Studies using historically accurate game environments (archaeological reconstructions, period-appropriate visual design) reported stronger learning outcomes than those using generic game formats with historical content superimposed \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 31DeAmicis2009451, 62Milosz201896}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Historical authenticity is the only principle that lacks grounding in any general motivational or design theory (Figure~\ref{fig:principle-theoretical-grounding}, top row). It derives instead from the epistemology of the discipline itself: historical thinking requires engagement with authentic sources and contexts \cite{Wineburg2001, MonteSano2012}. Within \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} framework, authenticity operates as a content validity condition~-- it ensures that game elements moderate historically valid learning rather than trivial recall. This disciplinary specificity distinguishes gamification in history education from domain-general approaches.

\textit{Violation evidence.} When authenticity is absent, gamification risks producing what \cite{Clark2016} terms ``chocolate-covered broccoli''~-- superficially engaging activities that fail to develop historical understanding. The 10.8\% negative-outcome rate in the corpus is concentrated in implementations lacking historical authenticity \cite{47Clara2010156, Oceja2022419}.

\item \textbf{Narrative integration.} Using historical narratives as the framework for gamified activities enhanced both engagement and learning \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 74Fendt2019, 29Stiso20201165}. Quest-based structures aligned naturally with historical investigation, casting students as detectives uncovering evidence \cite{59Kee2014270, 42Huffer201581}. Narrative-rich environments showed the strongest effects on affective outcomes ($d = 0.61$ for attitude toward history) \cite{15Slater2022, 55Squire2004, SailerHomner2020}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Narrative integration serves SDT's autonomy need: quest structures provide meaningful choice within historically constrained parameters \cite{RyanDeci2000, vanRoyZaman2019}. Within \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory, narrative is the primary mechanism through which history education's dynamics-dominant profile (Section~\ref{sec:dmc-mapping}) achieves its effect~-- narrative moderates the relationship between instructional content and affective outcomes by providing emotional and cognitive engagement with historical situations \cite{Nicholson2015}. This makes narrative integration history's primary modulator in the Landers framework.

\item \textbf{Progressive difficulty.} Scaffolded challenges that increased in complexity over time supported sustained engagement and skill development \cite{19Chou2019, 9Lin2018, 64Hsu201324}. Effective scaffolding moved from recognition tasks (matching, multiple choice) to analysis tasks (source evaluation, perspective-taking) to synthesis tasks (constructing historical narratives, arguing causation), mirroring established taxonomies of historical thinking skills \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Calandra2005323}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Progressive difficulty lies at the intersection of Flow theory and scaffolded learning. \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}'s \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} challenge--skill balance requires that difficulty increase as competence grows; simultaneously, the principle enacts Vygotskian scaffolding within the zone of proximal development \cite{Keller2010}. It also serves SDT's competence need by ensuring that mastery experiences accumulate progressively \cite{RyanDeci2000, Sailer2017}. In behaviourist terms, progressive difficulty implements shaping~-- successive approximation toward complex historical reasoning.

\item \textbf{Immediate feedback.} Providing instant feedback on quiz responses and game actions facilitated self-regulated learning \cite{11Ternblad2023115, 15Slater2022, 24Ghannem2019}. Immediate feedback enabled iterative learning cycles where students corrected misconceptions in real time \cite{19Chou2019, 34Serrano2023}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Immediate feedback is the most theoretically overdetermined principle, serving multiple frameworks simultaneously. In behaviourist terms, it provides positive reinforcement that shapes response patterns. In Flow theory, it maintains flow state by keeping learners informed of their performance relative to the challenge \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990}. It signals competence within SDT \cite{Sailer2017}. However, its history-specific application reveals a tension: feedback is highly effective for factual recall but less clearly effective for complex interpretive questions where multiple valid answers exist \cite{Clark2016}. This asymmetry has implications for implementation design (Section~\ref{sec:principle-implications}).

\item \textbf{Social elements.} Collaborative and competitive elements (team challenges, leaderboards, peer review) enhanced motivation, but their effects were moderated by implementation design \cite{3Watson2011466, 49Huizenga2007127, Sailer2017}. Competition needed careful balancing to avoid discouraging lower-performing students \cite{11Ternblad2023115, 47Clara2010156}. Collaborative structures showed more consistently positive effects than purely competitive elements \cite{46Stirling2022, 39Tegos2014838}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Social elements map directly onto SDT's relatedness need \cite{RyanDeci2000, vanRoyZaman2019}. \citeauthor{Sailer2017}~\cite{Sailer2017} demonstrated experimentally that social game features (leaderboards, teams) increased relatedness need satisfaction. In history education specifically, collaborative deliberation mimics the social practices of historical discourse~-- historians construct interpretations through scholarly debate, peer review, and evidential argumentation \cite{Wineburg2001}. Behaviourist social reinforcement (peer approval, competitive standing) provides a secondary mechanism \cite{BaiHewHuang2020}.

\item \textbf{Multiple modalities.} Combining text, images, maps, interactive elements, audio, and video addressed different learning preferences and supported deeper understanding \cite{10Huaman2019220, 62Milosz201896, 27Sumi2022}. Primary source images, historical maps, and audiovisual materials within gamified environments enhanced engagement across diverse learner profiles \cite{59Kee2014270, 74Fendt2019}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Multiple modalities draw on cognitive load theory (dual coding reduces extraneous load) and Universal Design for Learning (multiple means of representation) \cite{Keller2010, LaineLindberg2020}. Within \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} framework, multimodal design improves the instructional quality that game elements moderate. History is uniquely suited to multimodal gamification because primary sources are inherently multimodal~-- texts, images, maps, artefacts, oral histories~-- making multiple modalities a natural fit rather than an artificial addition \cite{Reisman2012, Hunicke2004}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Principle interactions and archetype alignment.} \label{sec:principle-interactions}

The six principles are not independent~-- they form complementary pairs and exhibit tensions that shape implementation decisions.

\textit{Synergies.} Three principle pairs produce mutually reinforcing effects. Narrative integration combined with historical authenticity creates \textit{authentic narrative}~-- the strongest combination in the corpus, associated with the largest reported effect size ($d = 0.61$) on affective outcomes \cite{SailerHomner2020, 15Slater2022}. Progressive difficulty combined with immediate feedback produces an \textit{adaptive assessment loop} where scaffolded challenges and real-time correction maintain learners in the flow channel \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990}. Social elements combined with multiple modalities create \textit{rich collaborative environments} where diverse resources support group investigation \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 46Stirling2022}.

\textit{Tensions.} Two tensions constrain implementation. First, immediate feedback can conflict with historical authenticity: instant correct/incorrect judgements are appropriate for factual recall but oversimplify interpretive questions where historical evidence supports multiple valid conclusions \cite{Clark2016, Wineburg2001}. Second, competitive social elements (leaderboards, rankings) can undermine progressive difficulty by discouraging weaker students from attempting scaffolded challenges, particularly in heterogeneous groups \cite{11Ternblad2023115, vanRoyZaman2019}. Managing these tensions requires design decisions that the archetype framework helps to structure.

\textit{Archetype alignment.} Figure~\ref{fig:principle-archetype-mapping} maps the five design archetypes from Section~\ref{sec:archetypes} onto the six principles, revealing systematic patterns. The Immersive Explorer and Narrative Investigator archetypes emphasise the history-specific principles (authenticity, narrative) but under-implement scalable mechanics (feedback, progression). The Competitive Quizzer inverts this pattern: strong on scalable, components-level principles but weak on the dynamics-level principles that define history education's disciplinary signature. The Collaborative Builder privileges social elements over all other principles. Only the Hybrid Integrator addresses all six principles, explaining both its superior effectiveness in the literature and its resource intensity \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025aredu, Sailer2017}.

\input{figures/principle_archetype_mapping}

This alignment reveals a fundamental trade-off in gamification design for history education: the principles most strongly associated with disciplinary learning outcomes (authenticity, narrative) are the most resource-intensive to implement, while the most scalable principles (feedback, progression, social elements) are the least discipline-specific. No archetype except the Hybrid Integrator resolves this tension, and the Hybrid Integrator's resource requirements place it beyond reach for most educational contexts \cite{Werbach2012, Landers2014}.

\textbf{Implications for experimental design.} \label{sec:principle-implications}

The six principles collectively define the design space for gamification in history education; the archetype alignment reveals which principles are feasible under given constraints. The present study (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) deliberately selects the Competitive Quizzer archetype~-- quiz-based, point-driven, delivered via Viber~-- as a test of whether lightweight gamification can produce meaningful learning effects in resource-constrained conditions.

This selection implements four of the six principles fully: progressive difficulty (scaffolded question sequences across 23 game modules), immediate feedback (automated scoring with instant results), social elements (leaderboards and competitive standings), and multiple modalities (text, images, historical maps within Viber). Two principles are implemented partially: historical authenticity is maintained through NMT-aligned content validated against curriculum standards, and narrative integration is limited to chronological course structure rather than quest-based frameworks.

This is a \textit{deliberate trade-off}: the Competitive Quizzer sacrifices dynamics-level richness for components-level scalability (connecting back to the DMC analysis in Section~\ref{sec:dmc-mapping}). The experimental design thus tests a theoretically grounded boundary condition: can lightweight gamification (components-level, four full and two partial principles) produce meaningful effects on engagement and learning outcomes? \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Werbach2012}

\citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory generates a specific prediction: if game elements moderate rather than replace instructional quality, then high-quality instruction combined with lightweight gamification should outperform poor instruction with rich gamification. The present study's context~-- a structured NMT preparatory course with experienced instruction~-- provides favourable conditions for testing this prediction. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} operationalises these design choices, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} assesses each principle's implementation and its relationship to observed outcomes.

The gamification review thus reveals a field that is growing rapidly in volume but remains methodologically immature~-- characterised by the engagement-learning decoupling, pervasive definitional ambiguity, and a disciplinary signature (dynamics-dominant element profile) that distinguishes history from other gamification domains. The six design principles and five archetype configurations identified above define the design space within which the present study operates, with the Competitive Quizzer archetype deliberately selected to test whether lightweight gamification can produce meaningful effects under resource-constrained conditions. Before this test can proceed, however, the trustworthiness of the evidence base itself must be established~-- the task of the quality assessment that follows.


\section{Methodological quality of gamification research in history education} \label{sec:mrqs}

The MRQS assessment directly informs the experimental design of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} and the self-assessment framework applied in the discussion of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. If the gamification evidence base (74~studies) is to compensate for the scarcity of digital history research (6~studies), its methodological quality must first be established.

\subsection{Rationale for quality assessment}

\textbf{Quality--effect relationship.} The inverse relationship between study quality and effect sizes is well documented: \citeauthor{Hattie2009}~\cite{Hattie2009} identified quality as a significant moderator, \citeauthor{CheungSlavin2012}~\cite{CheungSlavin2012} confirmed lower effects with higher quality across 84~interventions, and \citeauthor{TamimMMRQG2021}~\cite{TamimMMRQG2021} demonstrated negative quality-effect correlations across 52~meta-analyses. The effect sizes in Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness} were computed without quality weighting, making true effects plausibly smaller \cite{Kraft2020}.

\textbf{Existing tools and their limitations.} No major quality instrument fits gamification research in history education (Figure~\ref{fig:quality-tool-comparison}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}): MERSQI~\cite{ReedBeckman2007, CookReed2015} assumes experimental designs, MMAT~\cite{Hong2018} lacks gamification items, Cochrane RoB~2.0~\cite{HigginsThomas2019} targets near-absent RCTs, and ROBIS~\cite{Whiting2016} assesses reviews not primary studies \cite{Zeng2015}. Existing gamification reviews consistently omit structured quality assessment \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019, SailerHomner2020, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\textbf{Design requirements.} Five requirements emerge: (1)~mixed-methods applicability; (2)~domain-specific gamification items; (3)~binary scoring for reliability \cite{CookReed2015}; (4)~complementarity with risk-of-bias tools \cite{Whiting2016}; (5)~codability from structured extraction data. The dual-instrument approach~-- MRQS for reporting quality, adapted ROBIS for risk of bias~-- addresses the known distinction between reporting quality and internal validity \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, Zeng2015}.

\subsection{The MRQS framework} \label{sec:mrqs-framework}

The Methodological Reporting Quality Score (MRQS) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} is a ten-item binary instrument (range 0--10) assessing reporting completeness in gamification research, drawing on CONSORT~2010 \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}, the Cochrane Handbook \cite{HigginsThomas2019}, GRADE \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}, and the Campbell Collaboration \cite{Campbell2020}. Following \citeauthor{MaWang2020}~\cite{MaWang2020}, it targets reporting quality exclusively, complemented by an adapted ROBIS for risk of bias.

\textbf{Three-tier architecture.} The 10 items are grouped into three functional tiers (Figure~\ref{fig:mrqs-item-architecture}): \textit{Tier~A~-- Empirical design} (M2, M3, M5, M6, M7: sample $n \geq 30$, control group, effect sizes, pre-post design, inferential statistics); \textit{Tier~B~-- Reporting transparency} (M1, M4, M10: sample size reported, validated instruments, limitations discussed); \textit{Tier~C~-- Domain-specific} (M8, M9: explicit gamification definition, theoretical framework). A key design feature is the \emph{design-dependent ceiling}: qualitative studies can score at most 5/10. Each tier serves a distinct function: Tier~A enables meta-analytic inclusion, Tier~B enables replication, Tier~C enables cross-study conceptual comparison \cite{Cohen1960, Schulz2010CONSORT, Borenstein2009, Deterding2011, Buckley2018}.

\input{figures/mrqs_item_architecture}

\textbf{Dual-instrument approach and coding.} \label{sec:dual-instrument} All items use binary (0/1) scoring, enabling automated assessment from structured JSON extraction records \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, MarquesCruz2025}. The MRQS is paired with an adapted ROBIS \cite{Whiting2016} modified by Iryna~S.~Mintii \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, resting on a complementarity principle \cite{PerryWhitmarsh2021}: MRQS asks ``Is the information present?'' while adapted ROBIS asks ``How credible is the finding?'' The adapted ROBIS comprises three domains with two signalling questions each: D1 (participant selection: Q1~criteria, Q2~representativeness), D2 (data collection: Q3~instruments, Q4~analysis), D3 (interpretation: Q5~consistency, Q6~limitations). MRQS scores are \emph{reporting completeness indices}, not quality rankings; the instrument has not been externally validated beyond the 74-study corpus. The complementarity of the two instruments is visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:mrqs-robis-complementarity} (Appendix~\ref{app:F}).

\subsection{Quality assessment results} \label{sec:qa-results}

\textbf{Overall MRQS distribution.} \label{sec:mrqs-distribution} Across the 74 included studies, the mean MRQS was 4.31/10 (SD = 1.93, Mdn = 4.0, IQR = 3--6, range = 0--9); no study achieved the maximum \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. The distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:mrqs-distribution}) was approximately unimodal: 15~studies (20.3\%) scored 0--2, 38 (51.4\%) scored 3--5, and 21 (28.4\%) scored 6--9. The modal cluster at 4--5 (35.2\%) represents the practical ceiling for non-experimental designs, reflecting the design-dependent scoring constraint: qualitative studies can score at most 5/10 as Tier~A items are structurally inapplicable.

\textbf{Risk of bias and signalling questions.} \label{sec:rob-results} The overall assessment revealed 83.8\% high risk of bias (62~studies), with a clear domain gradient (Figure~\ref{fig:rob-domains}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}): D1 participant selection 85.1\% high $>$ D2 data collection 63.5\% $>$ D3 interpretation 55.4\% \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Whiting2016}. The most striking signalling-question finding (Figure~\ref{fig:signalling-questions}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}) is Q2's universal failure: 0\% of 74~studies received a favourable rating on sample representativeness~-- no study even \emph{discussed} how its sample relates to a broader population, despite \citeauthor{Wild2022}~\cite{Wild2022} showing that convenience samples differ from national populations at $d = 0.56$ across 32~countries \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}. Conference papers showed 90\% high overall risk versus 71\% for journal articles; cross-domain comparison with \citeauthor{Igelstrom2021}~\cite{Igelstrom2021} (54\% serious/critical risk across 124~medical reviews) confirms the corpus's lesser methodological maturity. Notably, the sole low-risk-of-bias study \cite{15Slater2022} was qualitative~-- paradoxically satisfying ROBIS criteria more readily than quantitative studies, whose design features expose more bias domains to scrutiny.

\textbf{Domain co-occurrence.} \label{sec:domain-cooccurrence} The H-H-H profile (high risk in all three domains) characterised 43.2\% of studies (32/74), while only 24.3\% achieved at least one low-risk domain \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Whiting2016}. However, Domain~3 reveals an important asymmetry: 20.6\% of D1-high studies nonetheless achieved low or moderate D3 ratings, indicating that sound interpretation is achievable even within structural sampling constraints. Definitional clarity was orthogonal to risk of bias (81.8\% high vs.\ 84.1\% for studies without definitions), a finding elaborated in Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}.

\subsection{LLM validation of quality assessment} \label{sec:llm-validation}

\textbf{Context.} \label{sec:llm-context} The two parent reviews employed LLMs across all three major systematic review stages \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Blaizot2022}: screening (Claude~3.5~Sonnet for 423~abstracts), data extraction (GPT-4o, 21~items per study), and risk-of-bias assessment (GPT-4o, 6~signalling questions across 3~ROBIS domains). LLM outputs were treated as auxiliary drafts subject to mandatory human verification \cite{Khraisha2023}.

Cohen's $\kappa$ ranged from $-$0.04 (Q3) to 0.21 (Q6)~-- ``poor'' to at best ``fair'' \cite{LandisKoch1977, Cohen1960}. The dominant pattern was a leniency bias: GPT-4o systematically assigned more favourable ratings than human verification (e.g., 33~Q2 ``unclear'' verified as ``no''; 21~Q4 ``yes'' changed to ``no''), independently confirmed by \citeauthor{DiPumpo2025}~\cite{DiPumpo2025} who found custom GPTs assigned ``high risk'' at half the human rate \cite{MalekiKarami2024, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\textbf{Multi-model extraction.} \label{sec:multi-model} Three LLMs (Gemini~3~Pro, ChatGPT-4, Grok~4.1-thinking) extracting data from 80~studies achieved 82--97\% pairwise agreement on objective fields (publication year, type), with agreement highest for unambiguous metadata and lower for classificatory judgments \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\textbf{The task complexity gradient.} \label{sec:task-gradient} These results occupy a predictable position on a gradient (Figure~\ref{fig:llm-reliability-gradient}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}): \emph{Tier~1}~-- factual extraction achieves 82--100\% agreement \cite{Kim2025bmj, Purewal2025}; \emph{Tier~2}~-- structured RoB on standardised designs achieves $\kappa = 0.24$--$0.80+$ \cite{Lai2024jama, Taneri2025, Xia2025}; \emph{Tier~3}~-- adapted RoB on heterogeneous educational studies yields $\kappa \approx 0$ (present study). The gradient is driven by tool standardisation, study design homogeneity, and question objectivity. Disagreement decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:disagreement-taxonomy}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}) reveals 22.4\% response-format mismatches and $\sim$78\% substantive leniency-direction shifts \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\textbf{Deployment framework.} \label{sec:llm-deployment} The gradient yields tiered recommendations \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Khraisha2023}: Tier~1 tasks (metadata) support unsupervised LLM use with spot-checks; Tier~2 tasks (structured RoB) warrant LLM first-pass with mandatory human verification; Tier~3 tasks (adapted RoB on heterogeneous corpora) require complete human re-assessment, as near-chance $\kappa$ means LLM output provides no informational value and may anchor reviewers toward leniency. An automation paradox emerges: the tasks where LLMs are most needed~-- evaluative judgment on large heterogeneous corpora~-- are precisely those where they are least reliable \cite{Wang2025aera, Blaizot2022, Rubinstein2025}.

\subsection{Definitional practices and quality} \label{sec:def-quality}

\textbf{The definitional-quality nexus.} \label{sec:def-quality-nexus} The association between explicit definitions and MRQS scores (5.36 vs.\ 4.13, $p = .065$) coupled with the non-association with risk-of-bias ratings (81.8\% vs.\ 84.1\% high) reveals a two-dimension model of methodological quality \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Definitional studies scored higher on reporter-level Tier~C items (M1, M8, M9, M10) but showed no advantage on design-level Tier~A items (M3, M6, M7). The psychometric independence of M8 ($r = 0.05$) confirms that definitional clarity is a \emph{reporter-level trait}, not a study-level methodological feature (Figure~\ref{fig:definitional-quality-interaction}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}). Interventions targeting definitional practices would improve reporting transparency but would not, by themselves, improve study design quality \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Clark2016}.

\textbf{Cross-domain benchmarking and declining clarity.} \label{sec:cross-domain-def} The 14.9\% explicit definition rate is lower than rates in broader gamification reviews (Table~\ref{tab:cross-domain-definitions}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}), where $\sim$75\% \emph{cite} \citeauthor{Deterding2011}~\cite{Deterding2011} but far fewer \emph{operationalise} it \cite{Hamari2014, KoivistoHamari2019, SEABORN201514}. The gap reflects disciplinary composition (historians viewing the concept as self-evident), boundary porosity (gamification/GBL/serious games overlap in history), and conference-paper dominance with weaker definitional conventions \cite{Buckley2018, HuotariHamari2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. Counterintuitively, definitional clarity \emph{declined} from 18.9\% (2004--2017) to 10.8\% (2018--2024), driven by terminological normalisation (the term functioning as an unexamined boundary object), compositional dilution (newer entrants lacking definitional conventions), and definitional exhaustion~-- researchers treating the debate as settled despite persistent disagreement \cite{Landers2019, Cronbach1955}.

\textbf{The chain of validity failures.} \label{sec:validity-chain} Definitional ambiguity triggers a five-stage cascade (Figure~\ref{fig:construct-validity-cascade}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}): Stage~1~-- definitional ambiguity (85.1\% of studies lack explicit definitions, conflating structural gamification, content gamification, GBL, and serious games \cite{Deterding2011, Becker2021}); Stage~2~-- construct heterogeneity (the corpus mixes at least four distinct constructs under one label \cite{Buckley2018, DichevDicheva2017}); Stage~3~-- measurement incommensurability (only 21.6\% use validated instruments, 9.5\% report effect sizes \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Borenstein2009}); Stage~4~-- synthesis failure (meta-analytic pooling is structurally precluded); Stage~5~-- knowledge gap persistence (after 74~studies, the field cannot confidently answer ``Does gamification improve history learning?'' \cite{Landers2019, Cronbach1955}). Breaking the chain at Stage~1~-- requiring explicit definitions~-- would propagate improvements through all subsequent stages \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\textbf{Construct consolidation principles.} \label{sec:construct-consolidation} Three principles emerge: (1)~\emph{definitional minimalism}~-- studies need only specify which conceptual approach their intervention instantiates and provide a working definition sufficient for replication, analogous to CONSORT's minimum reporting philosophy \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, Landers2019}; (2)~\emph{construct-measurement alignment}~-- outcome measures must match the specific construct (engagement scales for structural gamification, perspective-taking instruments for GBL) \cite{Borenstein2009, 36Scholz2021}; (3)~\emph{synthesis-ready reporting}~-- studies reporting their conceptual approach, validated measures, and standardised effect sizes would enable construct-specific meta-analyses \cite{HuotariHamari2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. The reporting checklist in Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist} operationalises these principles.

\subsection{Detailed bias analysis by category} \label{sec:detailed-bias}

\textbf{Validity threat mapping.} \label{sec:validity-threats} All four validity types are simultaneously compromised in the corpus \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Cronbach1955}: \emph{statistical conclusion validity} (small samples, near-universal failure to report effect sizes); \emph{internal validity} (absent control groups, uncontrolled confounders, novelty effect); \emph{construct validity} (unvalidated instruments, definitional ambiguity per Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}); and \emph{external validity} (Q2 universal failure at 0\% favourable, geographic concentration per Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}). No inferential pathway from the corpus is secure. At the corpus median of $n = 34$, a two-group study can detect only $d \approx 0.98$~-- five times larger than \citeauthor{Kraft2020}'s~\cite{Kraft2020} ``large'' benchmark ($d = 0.20$), severely underpowering all studies (Table~\ref{tab:power-analysis}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}) \cite{Cohen1960, Borenstein2009}. Implementation fidelity threats (technical failures, pedagogical misuse) produce attenuation bias opposite to inflation bias from absent controls, making the net direction of bias indeterminate \cite{Ioannidis2005, 58Shih2015173, 61Karsenti202027}.

\textbf{Bias compounding.} \label{sec:bias-compounding} The H-H-H triple threat~-- 43.2\% of studies rated high risk across all three ROBIS domains simultaneously, versus 29.9\% expected under independence ($0.851 \times 0.635 \times 0.554$)~-- confirms systematic co-occurrence \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Whiting2016}. The interaction pathways (Figure~\ref{fig:bias-interaction-network}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}) show multiplicative amplification: small samples inflate effects that absent controls cannot correct; unvalidated instruments conflate treatment effects with novelty; definitional ambiguity ensures the wrong construct is measured \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Borenstein2009}. Positive findings from underpowered studies encourage further small studies using similar unvalidated instruments, perpetuating the same weaknesses~-- consistent with Stage~5 of the chain of validity failures (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}).

\textbf{Evidence certainty.} \label{sec:evidence-certainty} Following SWiM Item~9 \cite{Campbell2020}, a modified GRADE assessment \cite{GuyattGRADE2008, GRADE} yields Very Low certainty across all four outcome categories (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-certainty-grade}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}): engagement/motivation (consistent but biased, imprecise, and confounded by novelty); knowledge acquisition (inconsistent across designs); historical thinking (near-absent direct evidence); and long-term retention (only 2~studies). ``Very Low'' means the evidence base cannot reliably distinguish between true positive, null, or negative effects~-- the field produces \emph{signals} but not \emph{evidence} in the GRADE sense \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}. Remedial reporting steps are operationalised in Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}.

\subsection{Implications for the field} \label{sec:implications-field}

\textbf{Interpretive caution.} \label{sec:interpretive-caution} The Very Low GRADE certainty demands that reported effect sizes be interpreted as upper bounds rather than point estimates. The typical study cannot detect effects below $d \approx 0.98$, approximately five times larger than \citeauthor{Kraft2020}'s~\cite{Kraft2020} recalibrated ``large'' benchmark ($d = 0.20$) for scalable educational interventions. Published positive findings likely reflect inflated estimates from small samples and absent controls, or false positives \cite{Ioannidis2005}; with only 9.5\% of studies reporting effect sizes (Table~\ref{tab:mrqs-prevalence}), the relative contribution of each source is unknowable \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Hattie2009}.

\textbf{Structural drivers.} \label{sec:structural-drivers} Methodological weakness is a predictable outcome of institutional structures rather than individual failure \cite{Chalmers2009}. Conference-paper dominance selects for underdeveloped methodology through less rigorous review, page limits, and preliminary findings that are never followed by full journal articles \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. The field's interdisciplinary fragmentation across educational technology, computer science, history education, and game design~-- each with different methodological norms~-- means no tradition alone provides the skills needed, and no existing assessment tool bridges all traditions \cite{CookReed2015, Glasziou2014}.

\textbf{History education specificity.} \label{sec:history-specificity} Three discipline-specific challenges limit cross-domain prescriptions. First, the \emph{measurement deficit}: history education lacks standardised achievement tests, as historical thinking involves interpretive skills resisting multiple-choice assessment \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Crymble2021}. Second, \emph{cultural embeddedness}: nationally specific curricula make cross-study comparison inherently more problematic than in STEM \cite{Wild2022, Torgerson202237}. Third, the \emph{authenticity--engagement tension}: game mechanics (points, time pressure) may conflict with historical thinking goals (careful source analysis, tolerance of ambiguity), requiring discipline-specific outcome frameworks \cite{Ditlmann2025, Clark2016}.

\textbf{Research priorities.} \label{sec:research-priorities} A Pareto analysis suggests three changes~-- reporting effect sizes, including control groups, and achieving $n \geq 64$ per group~-- would move the field from Very Low toward Low or Moderate GRADE certainty \cite{GuyattGRADE2008, Kraft2020}. The evidence gap map (Table~\ref{tab:evidence-gap-map}, Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-gap-map}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}) reveals critical gaps: historical thinking (14~studies, no RCTs), long-term retention (2~studies), and cost-effectiveness (total absence) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Campbell2020}. Four priorities emerge: (1)~adequately powered quasi-experimental studies of knowledge acquisition and historical thinking; (2)~delayed post-tests; (3)~multi-site studies; and (4)~cost-effectiveness analysis \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Borenstein2009, Schulz2010CONSORT}.

\subsection{Proposed reporting checklist} \label{sec:reporting-checklist}

\textbf{Positioning and rationale.} \label{sec:checklist-positioning} Existing reporting standards (CONSORT, PRISMA, MERSQI, MMAT, JBI) do not adequately address gamification research in education: they assume experimental designs, lack domain-specific items, or cannot accommodate definitional heterogeneity (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}). The proposed checklist is derived empirically from the MRQS analysis \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}, targeting specific failure modes of the 74-study corpus as a ``minimum viable reporting standard'' \cite{CookReed2015}. Items are organised into three priority tiers by prevalence and synthesis impact (Figure~\ref{fig:checklist-priority-tiers}).

\input{figures/checklist_priority_tiers}

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{T1.}] Report effect sizes (Cohen's $d$, $\eta^2$, or equivalent) alongside inferential statistics \cite{Borenstein2009, Cohen1960}. \emph{Prevalence: 9.5\%. Enables: meta-analytic synthesis.}
\item[\textbf{T1.}] Describe measurement instruments and provide evidence of validity and/or reliability. \emph{Prevalence: 21.6\%. Addresses: Stage~3 of the chain of validity failures (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}).}
\item[\textbf{T1.}] Provide an explicit definition of gamification (or game-based learning, serious games, etc.) and position the study within a recognised taxonomy \cite{Deterding2011, 36Scholz2021, Buckley2018}. \emph{Prevalence: 14.9\%. Addresses: Stage~1 of the chain of validity failures.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Include a comparison condition where the research question involves effectiveness claims \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, HigginsThomas2019}. \emph{Prevalence: 31.1\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Report inferential test statistics with exact $p$-values. \emph{Prevalence: 39.2\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Employ a pre--post design with clearly timed assessments. \emph{Prevalence: 39.2\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Achieve sample sizes of $n \geq 30$ per condition, with a target of $n \geq 64$ for medium effects ($d = 0.50$, $\alpha = .05$, power $= .80$). \emph{Prevalence: 50.0\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Discuss study limitations explicitly, including threats to internal and external validity. \emph{Prevalence: 47.3\%.}
\item[\textbf{T3.}] Report the sample size, sampling method, and available demographic characteristics \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}. \emph{Prevalence: 83.8\%.}
\item[\textbf{T3.}] Specify the theoretical framework guiding the design and interpretation. Describe game design elements employed and the rationale for their selection \cite{SEABORN201514, Sailer2017}. \emph{Prevalence: 94.6\%.}
\item[\textbf{H.}] Describe the historical content domain and its cultural specificity; acknowledge limitations on cross-cultural transferability. \emph{Rationale: the Q2 universal failure (Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}) is partly structural~-- history's cultural embeddedness means representativeness requires explicit scoping.}
\item[\textbf{H.}] If measuring historical thinking, specify which dimensions (source analysis, contextualisation, corroboration, perspective-taking) and report the validation status of instruments used \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}. \emph{Rationale: the disciplinary measurement deficit (Section~\ref{sec:implications-field}) means that instrument transparency is especially critical.}
\end{enumerate}

Adoption of Tier~1 items alone would transform the field's evidence base by enabling meta-analysis, addressing instrument validity, and resolving definitional ambiguity \cite{Glasziou2014}. The checklist is an analytical derivation, not an empirically validated instrument; prospective validation through future systematic reviews correlating compliance with GRADE upgrading potential remains a natural next step \cite{GuyattGRADE2008, Chalmers2009}.

The MRQS assessment confirms that the gamification evidence base is in a pre-paradigmatic state: all four validity types are simultaneously compromised, and GRADE certainty is Very Low across all outcome domains. The chain of validity failures (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}) explains how the definitional gap propagates through measurement, analysis, and synthesis. Six design requirements for the experimental study emerge directly from this quality critique and are evaluated against the 12-item reporting checklist in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. \section{Gamification and digital competence: implementation through DigComp 2.0} \label{sec:digcomp}

\subsection{The DigComp framework family}

The European Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp) comprises an evolving family sharing a stable five-area, 21-competence architecture: DigComp~1.0 (2013) established the structure; DigComp~2.0 \citep{Vuorikari2016} updated vocabulary descriptors; DigComp~2.1 \citep{Carretero2017} added eight proficiency levels; DigComp~2.2 \citep{Vuorikari2022} incorporated AI literacy examples; and DigComp~3.0 \citep{Cosgrove2025} introduced 500+ learning outcomes while consolidating proficiency levels to four. The related DigCompEdu \citep{Redecker2017} addresses educator competences but is not the primary reference here, as this study evaluates \textit{student} competence development. The framework landscape is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:digcomp-framework-landscape} (Appendix~\ref{app:F}).

This thesis uses DigComp~2.0 as the primary reference (consistent with \cite{Titova2025cte}), supplemented by DigComp~2.1 proficiency levels and DigComp~2.2 examples; findings remain structurally compatible with DigComp~3.0. In the Ukrainian context, \citeauthor{Ovcharuk2023}~\cite{Ovcharuk2023} surveyed 54,254 educators and identified challenges~-- lacking ICT equipment, unstable internet, insufficient digital competence~-- that directly mirror the wartime conditions documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} \citep{UNESCO2023}.

\subsection{The discipline-blindness problem: towards history-specific digital competence}

This discipline-blindness affects both the nascent digital history literature (6~studies, none with competence frameworks) and the larger gamification corpus (Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}), reinforcing the need for a history-specific competence-based approach.

DigComp's generality is a strength for policy but a weakness for disciplinary education: ``information and data literacy'' means something fundamentally different for a historian evaluating a digitised primary source than for a general citizen. The measurement deficit (only 21.6\% validated instruments, Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}) is partly a consequence of this discipline-blindness. This section proposes the first history-specific operationalisation: Area~1 requires evaluating primary source authenticity and digital archive provenance \citep{Crymble2021, ROMEIN}; Area~2 requires scholarly discourse about contested interpretations through digital platforms \citep{Graham20151}; Area~3 involves creating digital narratives, interactive timelines, and curated exhibitions \citep{Crymble2021, MonteSano2012}; Area~4 adds wartime-specific safety (navigating learning during air raids and power outages) \citep{UNESCO2023, Ovcharuk2023}; and Area~5 involves selecting appropriate digital tools for specific historical questions \citep{Graham20151, Reisman2012}. This operationalisation provides the conceptual vocabulary for discipline-specific assessment instruments (Figure~\ref{fig:digcomp-history-adaptation}, Appendix~\ref{app:F}).

\subsection{Gamification as a vehicle for digital competence development}

The relationship between gamification and digital competence is reciprocal: gamification develops digital competence by requiring navigation of digital environments, while digital competence enables more effective engagement with gamified platforms \citep{BarrosoTristan2025, Titova2025cte}. Building on \cite{Titova2025cte}, a three-layer integration model is proposed:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Layer~1 (Input):} Gamification design elements~-- the six principles identified in the systematic review (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}): historical authenticity, narrative integration, progressive difficulty, immediate feedback, social elements, and multiple modalities.
\item \textbf{Layer~2 (Framework):} DigComp competence areas, reinterpreted for history education as developed above, providing the vocabulary for specifying which competences each design element develops.
\item \textbf{Layer~3 (Outcomes):} Discipline-specific learning outcomes aligned with the evidence gap map (Table~\ref{tab:evidence-gap-map}): engagement and motivation, knowledge acquisition, attitudes and satisfaction, and historical thinking.
\item \textbf{Cross-cutting dimension:} Wartime resilience~-- affecting all three layers by constraining design choices (Layer~1), adding safety competence requirements (Layer~2), and introducing infrastructure-related outcome variability (Layer~3).
\end{itemize}

The model is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-digcomp-integration}. Its primary value is to make explicit the developmental pathways through which specific gamification elements contribute to specific competence outcomes~-- connections that remain implicit or untheorised in the existing literature \citep{Deterding2011, Sailer2017, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsection{Implementation guidelines} \label{sec:guidelines}

Based on the synthesis of the systematic reviews and the DigComp alignment analysis, the following guidelines for implementing gamification in history education were developed \citep{Korniienko2025cte, Vuorikari2016}. Each guideline is grounded in the evidence base established in Sections~\ref{sec:dh-review}--\ref{sec:mrqs}, connected to specific DigComp competence areas, and linked to the design principles identified in Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Start with clear learning objectives} (Areas~1,~5): Define historical content, thinking skills, and digital competencies before selecting gamification elements.
\item \textbf{Use progressive complexity} (Area~5): Structure activities from recognition (matching) through analysis (source evaluation) to synthesis (narrative construction).
\item \textbf{Maintain historical rigour} (Area~1): Ensure game elements do not trivialise historical content; align with curriculum standards.
\item \textbf{Provide multiple pathways} (Areas~3,~5): Offer required and optional activities to accommodate different ability levels.
\item \textbf{Integrate assessment with learning} (Areas~1,~5): Use gamified quizzes as both formative assessment and learning activities with immediate feedback and retry opportunities.
\item \textbf{Ensure inclusivity, accessibility, and resilience} (Area~4): Design for diverse learner needs, implement inclusive features (theme switching, font scaling, motion reduction, high contrast) \cite{McCrindle2013}, and ensure offline capability for conflict-zone resilience.
\item \textbf{Build community through gamification} (Area~2): Use creative naming, shared references, and collaborative elements to foster belonging.
\item \textbf{Document systematically} (Areas~1,~3): Maintain detailed records of implementation to address the critical reporting gaps identified in the MRQS assessment (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}).
\end{enumerate}

These guidelines operationalise the six design principles from the gamification review \citep{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} through the DigComp competence framework and the quality standards from the MRQS assessment \citep{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Their application in the experimental study is documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Conclusions to Chapter 1} \label{sec:ch1-conclusions}

This chapter pursued a progressive narrowing strategy: from digital history pedagogy as an emerging field (Section~\ref{sec:dh-review}) to gamification as its most-researched component (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}), through quality assessment exposing critical weaknesses (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}), to a competence framework for intervention design (Section~\ref{sec:digcomp}). The analysis, resting on two systematic reviews (80~studies), the MRQS instrument, and a three-layer integration model, reveals a double deficit: the field asks the wrong questions (engagement rather than historical thinking) and answers them unreliably (the chain of validity failures from definitional ambiguity through measurement incommensurability to synthesis failure). Yet these weaknesses define a design space: the six design principles (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}), the Competitive Quizzer archetype, and \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory predict that instructional quality~-- not game sophistication~-- determines learning outcomes. The chapter's three original contributions~-- the history-specific DigComp operationalisation, the three-layer integration model, and the wartime resilience dimension~-- provide the theoretical architecture for the experimental study, operationalised through the 12-item reporting checklist (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}) against which the study self-assesses in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents a gamification-enhanced digital history course that implements the eight guidelines from Section~\ref{sec:guidelines}, targets all five DigComp areas, and addresses the design requirements derived from the quality critique~-- documented with sufficient methodological detail to avoid replicating the reporting gaps that characterise the field.
