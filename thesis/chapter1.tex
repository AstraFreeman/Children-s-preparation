\chpt{Theoretical foundations of training future specialists using digital history} \label{ch:theory}

\section{Digital history in pedagogical research} \label{sec:dh-review}

Digital history is a modern interdisciplinary approach to the study of history using digital means \cite{ROMEIN, guiliano, Milligan}. There are various interpretations of this term, but they all relate to society, research, teaching, and communication. Digital history involves creating digital archives, databases, and multimedia resources to make historical data more accessible and interactive \citep{Cohen2005}. Unlike traditional historical methodology, digital history does not offer a revolutionary new approach but provides relevant tools in the era of information technologies, enabling the performance of scholarly work in significantly less time \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, Graham20151, Graham200810}.

The concept of ``digital history'' emerged in the mid-1990s alongside the development of the World Wide Web \cite{SeefeldtClark2009, NCPH2016}. Roy Rosenzweig and Daniel Cohen at the Center for History and New Media (George Mason University) were among the pioneers who articulated the theoretical foundations of this field \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006}. Since then, digital history has expanded to encompass a broad range of activities including text mining, Geographic Information Systems (GIS), digital storytelling, virtual museum exhibits, and computational approaches to historical analysis \cite{Crymble2021, Graham20151, Bordalejo2025, Lucchesi2019, Crymble202185}.

Despite this breadth, digital history has not yet received adequate theoretical grounding as a \textit{pedagogical} discipline. Most existing accounts treat it either as a research methodology \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021} or as a subset of digital humanities \cite{Berry2012, Moretti2013}, without articulating the specific pedagogical frameworks required for effective teaching and learning. This section addresses that gap by tracing the intellectual genealogy of digital history, situating it at the intersection of three scholarly domains, and reviewing the pedagogical theories that underpin its instructional implementation, before presenting a systematic review of empirical studies.

\subsection{Intellectual genealogy of digital history} \label{subsec:dh-genealogy}

The application of computational methods to historical inquiry has a longer history than the term ``digital history'' suggests. Understanding this genealogy is essential for grounding the pedagogical approach developed in this dissertation.

\textbf{Cliometrics and quantitative history (1960s--1980s).} The earliest computational approaches to history emerged from the cliometrics movement of the 1960s, when economic historians such as Robert Fogel and Stanley Engerman applied quantitative methods and econometric modelling to historical questions \cite{Fogel1974}. While cliometrics remained confined to economic history and was criticised for its narrow empiricism, it established the principle that computational tools could generate historical insights inaccessible through traditional methods alone.

\textbf{The CHNM founding era (1990s--2000s).} The modern concept of digital history crystallised with the founding of the Center for History and New Media (CHNM) at George Mason University in 1994 by Roy Rosenzweig. The publication of \textit{Digital History: A Guide to Gathering, Preserving, and Presenting the Past on the Web} by \citeauthor{CohenRosenzweig2006}~\cite{CohenRosenzweig2006} provided the first comprehensive theoretical framework, defining digital history as ``an approach to examining and representing the past that works with the new communication technologies of the computer, the Internet, and software systems''. Projects such as the Valley of the Shadow and H-Net demonstrated the potential of networked computing for democratising access to primary sources \cite{SeefeldtClark2009, NCPH2016}.

\textbf{Digital humanities expansion (2000s--2010s).} The broader digital humanities (DH) movement provided digital history with an expanded intellectual context. \cite{Berry2012} in \textit{Understanding Digital Humanities} argued that computation constitutes a ``third wave'' in the humanities, fundamentally altering how knowledge is produced. \cite{Moretti2013} in the concept of ``distant reading'' demonstrated that computational text analysis could reveal patterns invisible at the level of close reading, challenging assumptions about the primacy of individual texts. \cite{Drucker2011} critiqued the uncritical adoption of scientific visualisation conventions in the humanities, arguing that humanistic data require ``capta'' -- interpretive, situated representations rather than positivist data displays. For digital history pedagogy, these debates are consequential: they determine whether digital tools are taught as neutral instruments or as epistemologically significant mediators of historical knowledge.

\textbf{The pedagogical turn (2010s--present).} The most recent phase has seen a shift from digital history as primarily a research methodology to digital history as a subject of teaching and curricular design. \cite{Graham20151} in \textit{The Historian's Macroscope} explicitly addressed pedagogical questions, proposing computational literacy as a core competence for historians. \cite{Crymble2021} in \textit{Technology and the Historian} surveyed how digital tools have reshaped the discipline and highlighted the gap between tool adoption in research and tool integration in teaching. \cite{guiliano} in \textit{A Primer for Teaching Digital History} provided the first textbook-length treatment of instructional strategies. \cite{ROMEIN} offered a comprehensive ``state of the field'' review across nine European countries, concluding that digital history remains unevenly institutionalised and that pedagogical frameworks lag significantly behind research practice. Most recently, \citeauthor{Milligan}~\cite{Milligan} argued for a ``web-born'' historiography that acknowledges the internet as both primary source and research infrastructure.

Figure~\ref{fig:dh-timeline} visualises this evolution, identifying four distinct eras and the key publications that mark transitions between them.

The four-era chronology above follows a \textit{technical-institutional} criterion: each era is delineated by the tools, platforms, and institutional structures that shaped the field. An alternative --- and, we argue, complementary --- periodization can be constructed using a \textit{subject-of-inquiry} criterion that foregrounds \textit{what} digital history investigates rather than \textit{how} it operates technically:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Statistical period (1970s--2000s).} Digital history functions primarily as an instrument for processing large volumes of standardised information --- census data, trade records, price series. The object of inquiry is the aggregate pattern; the individual is subsumed within statistical distributions. Cliometrics \cite{Fogel1974} and early database projects exemplify this orientation, where computational power served quantification rather than interpretation.

\item \textbf{Humanistic period (2000s--2010s).} The focus shifts to the individual in history. Digital tools enable alternative perspectives on existing knowledge: prosopographical databases reconstruct individual lives, digitised personal archives recover marginalised voices, and hypertext narratives present multiple viewpoints on the same event \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, Graham20151}. The object of inquiry is no longer the aggregate but the particular; digital history becomes an instrument for interpretive pluralism.

\item \textbf{Postmodern period (2010s--present).} Digital history expands into domains previously outside its scope --- pedagogy, public history, popular culture, game-based learning. The object of inquiry is no longer history alone but the \textit{communication and teaching} of history through digital means \cite{Crymble2021, guiliano, ROMEIN}. The present dissertation, with its focus on gamification-mediated history pedagogy, is situated squarely within this third period.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent This subject-based periodization does not replace the technical chronology but runs parallel to it, revealing a trajectory from data processing through humanistic interpretation to pedagogical application. The two classifications intersect: the Statistical period spans the Cliometrics and CHNM eras; the Humanistic period aligns with the DH expansion; and the Postmodern period corresponds to the pedagogical turn. The distinction matters because it reframes the present study's contribution: this dissertation does not merely apply existing digital tools to history teaching (a technical innovation) but participates in the broader epistemological shift whereby digital history's subject of inquiry has expanded from data to persons to pedagogy itself.

\subsection{Digital history at the intersection of disciplines} \label{subsec:dh-intersection}

Digital history pedagogy exists at the convergence of three scholarly domains: History, Digital Humanities, and Pedagogy. Each domain contributes essential knowledge, skills, and epistemological orientations; effective teaching of digital history requires integration across all three.

\textbf{History} provides the disciplinary substrate: historical thinking skills (sourcing, contextualisation, corroboration, close reading) \cite{Wineburg2001}, the six ``big'' historical thinking concepts (historical significance, evidence, continuity and change, cause and consequence, historical perspectives, ethical dimension) \cite{Seixas2013}, and domain-specific knowledge about periods, events, and historiographical debates.

\textbf{Digital Humanities} contributes computational methods (text mining, network analysis, GIS, digital archiving), digital tools and platforms, and the critical data literacy necessary to evaluate the epistemological implications of algorithmic mediation \cite{Berry2012, Drucker2011, Moretti2013, Bordalejo2025}.

\textbf{Pedagogy} supplies learning theories (constructivism, connectivism), assessment frameworks, instructional design principles, and the knowledge of how students learn \cite{Vygotsky1978, Siemens2004, Hattie2009}.

The pairwise intersections of these domains produce established fields:

\textit{History $\cap$ Digital Humanities} yields digital history as a \textit{research} practice --- the application of computational methods to historical inquiry, from cliometric databases through GIS-based spatial analysis to large-scale text mining of digitised archives \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006, ROMEIN}.

\textit{History $\cap$ Pedagogy} yields history education and historical thinking research --- the study of how students develop disciplinary reasoning skills such as sourcing, contextualisation, and multiperspectivity \cite{Wineburg2001, Seixas2013}.

\textit{Digital Humanities $\cap$ Pedagogy} yields \textbf{digital pedagogy} --- the field concerned with how digital tools, platforms, and competence frameworks reshape teaching and learning processes across disciplines. Digital pedagogy encompasses the design of technology-enhanced learning environments, the development of digital competence standards (such as the DigComp framework family, Section~\ref{sec:digcomp}), and the study of how computational affordances --- interactivity, immediate feedback, adaptive difficulty, multimodal representation --- can be harnessed for instructional purposes \cite{Vuorikari2016, Voogt20161}. Unlike educational technology, which focuses on tool adoption, digital pedagogy addresses the epistemological question of how digitalisation transforms what it means to teach and to learn \cite{Striuk_2022, Hlianenko2024}. Gamification, as a design strategy that applies game elements to non-game educational contexts \cite{Deterding2011}, is one of the most actively researched instantiations of digital pedagogy (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}).

The triple intersection --- \textbf{digital history pedagogy} --- is the subject of this dissertation. As Figure~\ref{fig:dh-venn} illustrates, this central zone is where the disciplinary knowledge of history, the computational methods of DH, and the instructional frameworks of digital pedagogy converge. The present study contributes to this intersection by developing a gamification-mediated methodology that simultaneously engages historical thinking (History), digital competence development (DH $\cap$ Pedagogy), and evidence-based instructional design (Pedagogy).

This three-domain model allows us to classify digital history approaches by their primary orientation. Drawing on the literature, we propose a taxonomy of four approach types:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Archival-analytical approaches}: students work with digitised primary sources, databases, and computational text analysis tools to develop source criticism and evidence-based argumentation \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159, Crymble2021}.
\item \textit{Creative-productive approaches}: students create digital artefacts -- videos, exhibitions, websites, digital stories -- that communicate historical narratives to authentic audiences \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011, guiliano}.
\item \textit{Collaborative-communicative approaches}: students use wikis, blogs, discussion platforms, and social media to co-construct historical knowledge \cite{Soh2013, McLean20144, Graham20151}.
\item \textit{Immersive-experiential approaches}: students engage with history through games, simulations, virtual/augmented reality, and interactive timelines \cite{Connolly2012, Qian2016, Prensky2001}.
\end{enumerate}
The taxonomy implicitly assumes that students possess a baseline of factual historical knowledge --- the \textit{Remember} and \textit{Understand} levels of Bloom's revised taxonomy (\citeauthor{Anderson2001},~\cite{Anderson2001}). All four approach types presuppose that learners can recall dates, identify key figures, and recognise historical events before they engage in source criticism (archival-analytical), narrative creation (creative-productive), collaborative knowledge construction (collaborative-communicative), or immersive experience (immersive-experiential).

Recent evidence from Ukraine's standardised assessment system challenges this assumption. The National Multi-Subject Test (NMT) history results show mean scores of 143.2/200 in 2024 and 141.6/200 in 2025 \cite{UCEQA2024, UCEQA2025} --- a marginal downward trend. In the earlier ЗНО (External Independent Evaluation) format, failure rates in Ukrainian history rose from approximately 13\% in 2016--2017 to 18\% in 2021 \cite{UCEQA2025}. More broadly, PISA~2022 revealed that Ukrainian students' reading performance declined by 38~points compared to 2018 --- equivalent to approximately two years of schooling --- with mathematics and science showing declines of 12 and 19~points respectively \cite{OECDPISA2023}. These learning losses, compounded by pandemic disruptions in 2020--2021 and the full-scale invasion since February 2022, indicate that a substantial proportion of students now lack the foundational factual knowledge that the four-type taxonomy takes for granted \cite{BychkoTereshchenko2023, UNESCO2023}.

This observation carries direct methodological implications. Gamification, as implemented in this dissertation (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}), addresses precisely this foundational gap. Quiz-based game mechanics --- drag-and-drop matching, timed factual recall, chronological sorting --- operate primarily at Bloom's \textit{Remember} and \textit{Understand} levels, reinforcing the factual substrate upon which higher-order historical thinking depends. The four-type taxonomy thus represents a \textit{second stage} of a methodology that first secures factual foundations through gamified repetitive practice, then builds toward the analytical, creative, collaborative, and experiential competences that the taxonomy describes.

Of these four approach types, the immersive-experiential category -- and specifically gamification within it -- has attracted the most research attention, as the systematic review in Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review} demonstrates. The present dissertation develops and tests a methodology that combines elements of all four approach types, with gamification as its most extensively researched component.

\subsection{Pedagogical frameworks for digital history} \label{subsec:dh-frameworks}

Effective digital history teaching requires not only technological tools and historical content but also a theoretically grounded pedagogical framework. This subsection reviews four theoretical perspectives that collectively underpin the methodology developed in this dissertation.

\textbf{Constructivism and social constructivism.} Constructivist learning theory, rooted in the work of Piaget and elaborated by \citeauthor{Vygotsky1978}~\cite{Vygotsky1978}, holds that learners actively construct knowledge through interaction with their environment rather than passively receiving information. Vygotsky's concept of the zone of proximal development (ZPD) is particularly relevant to digital history pedagogy: digital tools can serve as scaffolding that enables students to engage with historical sources and methods beyond their unaided capacity. In the context of this dissertation, the gamified quiz system and collaborative online environment function as mediating tools in the Vygotskian sense, supporting learners' progression through increasingly complex historical reasoning tasks.

\textbf{Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK).} The TPACK framework, introduced by \citeauthor{Mishra2006}~\cite{Mishra2006} and subsequently refined \cite{Koehler2013}, describes the knowledge that teachers need to integrate technology effectively into instruction. TPACK identifies seven knowledge domains arising from the intersection of three foundational types: Content Knowledge (CK), Pedagogical Knowledge (PK), and Technological Knowledge (TK). For digital history, CK encompasses historical facts, concepts, and disciplinary epistemology; PK encompasses pedagogical strategies, assessment design, and classroom management; TK encompasses familiarity with digital tools, platforms, and computational methods. The critical insight of TPACK is that effective technology integration is not merely additive (combining CK + PK + TK) but transformative: the intersection produces qualitatively new knowledge about how specific technologies can represent specific content in pedagogically effective ways \cite{Mishra2006}. The systematic review presented later in this section reveals that instructor preparedness -- essentially TPACK competence -- is one of the key barriers to effective digital history pedagogy.

\textbf{DigComp 2.0 as a competence framework.} The European Digital Competence Framework (DigComp~2.0) \citeauthor{Vuorikari2016}~\cite{Vuorikari2016} provides a structured taxonomy of digital competences organised into five areas: information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety, and problem solving. DigComp~2.0 offers digital history educators a concrete vocabulary for specifying learning outcomes and aligning assessment with competence development \cite{Titova2025cte}. In this dissertation, the DigComp~2.0 framework informs both the design of the gamified assessment system (which targets information literacy and problem-solving competences) and the evaluation of student digital competence development.

\textbf{Connectivism.} \citeauthor{Siemens2004}'s~\cite{Siemens2004} connectivism proposes that in the digital age, learning is distributed across networks of people, technologies, and information sources. Knowledge resides not only in individuals but in the connections between nodes in a network. This perspective is directly relevant to digital history pedagogy, where students learn not only from instructors and textbooks but from digital archives, collaborative platforms, peer interactions in online forums, and the affordances of software tools. The course chat log analysed in Chapter~3 of this dissertation exemplifies connectivist learning: students' knowledge construction occurred through networked interactions across multiple channels (Viber group, Google Forms quizzes, gamification platform, Zoom sessions).

\textbf{Ukrainian and European educational context.} The pedagogical frameworks described above must be situated within the specific institutional and socio-political context of Ukrainian higher education. The Bologna process has driven competence-based curriculum reform across Ukrainian universities \cite{UNESCO2023}. At the same time, the full-scale Russian invasion since February 2022 has created unprecedented challenges for distance learning: power outages, air-raid disruptions, and psychological stress shape students' capacity to engage with digital tools. The present study is, to our knowledge, the first to examine digital history pedagogy under active wartime conditions, and the findings presented in Chapter~3 contribute to the growing literature on resilient education delivery during armed conflict.

\subsection{Methodology of the systematic review}

To establish the current state of digital history in pedagogical research, we conducted a systematic review following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines \citep{Page2021}. The review aimed to answer the primary research question \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}: \textit{How effective is teaching digital history to students compared to non-digital methods of teaching history in terms of improving learning outcomes?}

Subsidiary research questions addressed \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}: (1) the effect of course status (mandatory vs. elective) on effectiveness; (2) which specific digital tools demonstrate the highest effectiveness; (3) which outcome indicators are most sensitive to digital methods; and (4) whether intervention duration affects effectiveness.

\textbf{Search strategy.} The Scopus database was selected as the primary information source due to its comprehensive coverage of peer-reviewed literature across disciplines \cite{scopus}, indexing over 48,000 active titles from more than 5,000 international publishers. The search string employed Boolean operators and was applied to title, abstract, and keyword fields:

\begin{quote}
\texttt{TITLE-ABS-KEY ("digital history" AND student*)}
\end{quote}

The query was further limited to English-language publications and to document types including journal articles, conference papers, and book chapters. The search was executed on July 26, 2025, with no date restrictions applied during the search phase.

\textbf{Inclusion criteria.} Studies were included if they: (1) involved undergraduate or master's-level students enrolled in history courses at accredited higher education institutions, aged 18+; (2) implemented any digital technology intervention designed to enhance history education (collaborative writing platforms, digital archives, GIS, virtual reality, gamification, digital storytelling, computational text analysis) \cite{Wright-Maley2018, Block2021, Tilak2023, Nygren2014, Corlett-Rivera2017, Cheng2023, Koh2013, Ali2023, Joshi2021, Darmawan2025, Hastomo2024}; (3) employed quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods with empirical data; (4) were published as peer-reviewed articles, conference proceedings, or book chapters between 2011 and 2025.

\textbf{Exclusion criteria.} Studies were excluded if they: (1) involved pre-university (K-12) populations exclusively; (2) were theoretical or conceptual papers lacking empirical data \cite{Wright-Maley2018603, Block2021371, Nygren201478}; (3) focused on technology training for educators without student outcome assessment; (4) addressed disciplines other than history; (5) involved interventions lasting less than two weeks or fewer than five participants; (6) were published in languages other than English.

\textbf{Selection process.} The selection process followed a two-stage screening protocol \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The initial search yielded 46 records from Scopus. After automated deduplication (n=1), 45 unique citations were screened by title and abstract. To support the screening process, Claude~4 Opus and Claude~4 Sonnet were used as auxiliary tools for preliminary analysis, with all final inclusion/exclusion decisions made by human reviewers \cite{MalekiKarami2024, Taneri2025}. At the title-abstract screening stage, 28 records were excluded: 11 involved pre-university populations, 4 examined unrelated interventions, and 13 lacked empirical data.

Of the 17 studies advancing to full-text assessment, 2 could not be retrieved despite attempts to contact authors. The remaining 15 studies were assessed against all inclusion criteria using both human and LLM assistance. Nine additional studies were excluded: 2 were published outside the eligibility timeframe, 3 involved graduate students exclusively, 2 presented insufficient methodological detail, and 2 described theoretical frameworks without empirical testing. Ultimately, 6 studies satisfied all inclusion criteria. Figure~\ref{fig:dh-prisma} presents the complete selection process following PRISMA 2020 specifications \cite{Page2021}.

\textbf{Data collection.} Data from each included study were collected by three reviewers simultaneously: two generative AI-based services (Claude~4 Sonnet and Grok~3) and one human researcher \cite{Rubinstein2025, MalekiKarami2024}. The LLMs worked with identical prompts to ensure standardisation. When discrepancies were found, responses from all three reviewers were compared, with final decisions made by the human researcher. A standardised data extraction form captured study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details, outcome measures, and quality indicators \cite{KitchenhamCharters2007, HigginsThomas2019}.

\subsection{Results of the systematic review}

The identification of only 6 studies meeting the inclusion criteria underscores the nascent state of digital history as a pedagogical research area \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. This finding is particularly striking given the widespread use of digital tools in historical research \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021} and the growing emphasis on digital competencies in education policy \cite{Voogt20161, Vuorikari2016}. All six studies were published in English in peer-reviewed venues between 2011 and 2017 \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011, Davis2017, Lee2014159, McLean20144, Soh2013}.

The six studies represent diverse geographical contexts: three from the United States \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159, Soh2013}, and one each from Australia \citeauthor{Bell2016}~\cite{Bell2016}, New Zealand \cite{Coleborne2011}, and Canada \citeauthor{McLean20144}~\cite{McLean20144}. Sample sizes ranged from 27 to approximately 200 participants, with the sole quasi-experimental study \cite{Soh2013} having the second-largest sample (150 students). Only one study included a control group, reflecting the common challenge of implementing randomised designs in educational settings \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}.

\subsubsection{Study findings}

The included studies documented the following interventions and findings:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Digital storytelling with video} \cite{Bell2016}: Approximately 100 undergraduate students created 3-minute digital video stories about historical topics. The case study documented enhanced engagement with historical content and deeper understanding of historical concepts through the process of narrative construction. The digital format facilitated students' spontaneous pursuit of additional primary sources beyond course requirements.

\item \textit{Digital storytelling with multimedia} \cite{Coleborne2011}: Forty-three undergraduates used Windows Movie Maker to create digital stories about historical events. The study found improvements in both digital storytelling skills and historical thinking, demonstrating that multimedia production can serve as a vehicle for developing analytical capabilities.

\item \textit{Digital exhibition creation} \cite{Davis2017}: Students used the Omeka web platform to create public-facing digital exhibitions incorporating metadata standards and Dublin Core elements. The study reported that 45\% of students expressed enthusiasm about the digital format, and students demonstrated improved digital skills alongside historical learning.

\item \textit{Personal digital histories} \cite{Lee2014159}: This design-based research study spanned 5 years with 8 iterative cycles, involving approximately 200 students. Students created personal digital histories using various technologies. The iterative refinement process revealed that student engagement was highest when digital tools were directly aligned with historical inquiry tasks \cite{Reeves2003}.

\item \textit{Wiki and blog-based projects} \cite{McLean20144}: Twenty-seven master's students (8 of whom provided survey responses) used wiki and blog platforms for collaborative historical research projects. The mixed-methods study found improvements in technology skills and collaborative competencies, though the small response rate limited generalisability.

\item \textit{Collaborative wiki writing} \cite{Soh2013}: The only quasi-experimental study, involving 150 undergraduate students using ClassroomWiki compared to traditional approaches. The study documented a 340\% increase in peer-to-peer interactions compared to traditional discussion formats, with students spending an average of 12.4 hours on digital assignments (approximately 40\% more than on traditional assignments). Writing skills, engagement, and learning outcomes all showed positive effects.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cross-cutting analysis of outcomes}

Analysis of primary and secondary outcomes across the six studies revealed consistent patterns.

\textbf{Primary outcomes.} Historical knowledge acquisition was assessed in four studies, with three (67\%) reporting positive effects. \citeauthor{Soh2013}~\cite{Soh2013} provided the strongest evidence, with students in the wiki-based condition demonstrating significantly better writing quality and content accuracy than the control group. \citeauthor{Lee2014159}~\cite{Lee2014159} documented improved engagement with historical evidence across eight iterative cycles. \citeauthor{Bell2016}~\cite{Bell2016} and \citeauthor{Coleborne2011}~\cite{Coleborne2011} reported enhanced understanding of historical concepts through the digital storytelling process, though without quantitative comparison to control conditions.

\textbf{Secondary outcomes.} Student engagement was the most consistently positive outcome, with all six studies (100\%) reporting positive effects on at least one dimension of engagement \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011, Davis2017, Lee2014159, McLean20144, Soh2013}. Behavioural engagement was measured through platform analytics (time-on-task, number of contributions, peer interactions) and showed the most dramatic improvements. Cognitive engagement manifested through enhanced depth of historical analysis: \citeauthor{Bell2016}~\cite{Bell2016} reported that students spontaneously pursued additional primary sources beyond course requirements. Source analysis skills improved in three studies \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159, McLean20144}, particularly those employing digital archive platforms. The development of digital skills emerged as an unexpected but valuable outcome, with students acquiring competencies in metadata creation, digital curation, and platform-specific technical skills \cite{Davis2017, McLean20144}.

\textbf{Mechanisms of effectiveness.} Three mechanisms appeared to drive positive outcomes across studies \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}: (1) \textit{multimodal representation}, enabling students to engage with history through diverse sensory channels \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011}; (2) the \textit{``authentic audience'' effect}, where knowing their digital products would be publicly visible motivated higher-quality work \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159}; and (3) \textit{technological affordances} that enabled pedagogical approaches impractical in analog environments (version control making thinking visible, collaborative platforms distributing cognitive load) \cite{Soh2013, McLean20144}.

\textbf{Barriers and challenges.} Despite positive outcomes, implementation challenges were documented \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Technical difficulties consumed substantial instructional time (one study reported 15\% of sessions devoted to troubleshooting) \cite{McLean20144}. Instructor preparedness was a critical barrier, with faculty struggling to integrate technological and pedagogical knowledge effectively \cite{Davis2017, Lee2014159}. Assessment challenges were also noted: traditional evaluation methods proved inadequate for capturing learning in digital environments \cite{Coleborne2011}.

\subsection{Analysis and implications}

The small number of identified studies reveals a significant gap between the practice of digital history in research and its systematic integration into pedagogy. Several factors may explain this gap:

First, digital history has developed primarily as a research methodology rather than a teaching approach \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021}. The pioneers of the field (Rosenzweig, Cohen) focused on demonstrating the utility of digital tools for historical investigation \cite{CohenRosenzweig2006}, with less attention to how these tools can be effectively taught to students. The six included studies all represent individual instructors' initiatives rather than systematic programme-level implementations.

Second, the interdisciplinary nature of digital history creates challenges for pedagogical research \cite{Bordalejo2025, Lucchesi2019, Luther20106571}. As the three-domain model presented in Section~\ref{subsec:dh-intersection} illustrates, studies must address competencies in history, digital humanities, and pedagogy simultaneously. \citeauthor{Mishra2006}'s TPACK framework~\cite{Mishra2006, Koehler2013}, referenced in four of the six studies \cite{Bell2016, Davis2017, Lee2014159, McLean20144}, provides a theoretical lens for understanding this intersection: the barrier to effective digital history teaching is not merely a lack of technological knowledge (TK) or content knowledge (CK) in isolation, but the absence of integrated TPACK -- the knowledge of how specific technologies can represent specific historical content in pedagogically effective ways. This aligns with \citeauthor{Wineburg2001}'s~\cite{Wineburg2001} observation that historical thinking is itself ``unnatural'' and requires deliberate pedagogical scaffolding; adding digital tools compounds this challenge.

Third, the rapid evolution of digital tools means that pedagogical approaches may become obsolete before they can be rigorously evaluated. This creates a tension between innovation and evidence-based practice. The included studies span 2011--2017, using platforms (ClassroomWiki, Windows Movie Maker, Omeka) that have evolved substantially since publication \cite{Zielezinski20141137}.

Fourth, the absence of standardised outcome measures for digital history pedagogy makes it difficult to compare results across studies or to accumulate evidence systematically \cite{Hattie2009, Tamim2011}. Unlike STEM education, where standardised tests provide common outcome metrics, history education relies on diverse assessment approaches (essays, projects, portfolios) that resist direct comparison \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Manfra2008223, Sebring2012229}.

Fifth, the lack of standardised competence frameworks specific to digital history has hindered the design of curricula and the comparison of learning outcomes across studies. While the DigComp~2.0 framework \citeauthor{Vuorikari2016}~\cite{Vuorikari2016} provides a general taxonomy of digital competences, it does not address the domain-specific intersection of digital and historical reasoning. Similarly, historical thinking frameworks \cite{Wineburg2001, Seixas2013} do not account for the computational dimensions of contemporary historical practice. The absence of a unified framework that bridges these domains -- essentially, a TPACK-informed competence model for digital history -- remains an open challenge that this dissertation aims to address through its integrated methodology.

Of these five factors, the first two -- the orientation toward research rather than pedagogy, and the interdisciplinary demands on instructors -- are likely the most consequential structural barriers, as they reflect enduring features of the field rather than transient technological limitations.

The scarcity of rigorous pedagogical research on digital history motivates the broader examination of gamification in history education presented in the following sections, as gamification represents the most extensively researched digital approach to history teaching \cite{Connolly2012, DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}. While the digital history review identified only 6 studies, the gamification in history education review \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} identified 74.

The intellectual genealogy presented in Section~\ref{subsec:dh-genealogy} traced the evolution of computational approaches to history from cliometrics \cite{Fogel1974} through the CHNM founding era \citeauthor{CohenRosenzweig2006}~\cite{CohenRosenzweig2006}, the digital humanities expansion \cite{Berry2012, Moretti2013, Drucker2011}, and the contemporary pedagogical turn \cite{Graham20151, Crymble2021, guiliano, ROMEIN}. This genealogy reveals that digital history has progressed through four distinct phases, each broadening the scope of computational engagement with the past, yet pedagogical applications have consistently lagged behind research innovations.

The three-domain model (Section~\ref{subsec:dh-intersection}) situates digital history pedagogy at the intersection of History, Digital Humanities, and Pedagogy, and identifies four approach types -- archival-analytical, creative-productive, collaborative-communicative, and immersive-experiential -- that structure the field. The review of pedagogical frameworks (Section~\ref{subsec:dh-frameworks}) established constructivism \cite{Vygotsky1978}, TPACK \cite{Mishra2006, Koehler2013}, DigComp~2.0 \cite{Vuorikari2016}, and connectivism \cite{Siemens2004} as the theoretical underpinnings for the methodology developed in this dissertation.

The PRISMA-guided systematic review \cite{Page2021, KorniienkoSemerikov2024} confirmed that digital history in pedagogical research remains an underdeveloped field, with only 6 studies meeting rigorous inclusion criteria \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011, Davis2017, Lee2014159, McLean20144, Soh2013}. Five structural factors explain this gap: the research-over-pedagogy orientation, interdisciplinary demands on instructors, rapid tool obsolescence, absence of standardised outcome measures, and the lack of domain-specific competence frameworks. This finding establishes the need for: (1) more empirical research on digital history pedagogy; (2) examination of specific digital approaches (such as gamification) that have been more extensively studied \cite{Connolly2012, Qian2016}; and (3) development of comprehensive teaching methodologies that integrate multiple digital tools within a TPACK-informed, competence-based framework \cite{Voogt20161, Mishra2006}.

\section{Gamification in history education: definitions, effectiveness, and design principles} \label{sec:gamification-review}

\subsection{Background and rationale}

The digital transformation of education has fundamentally altered how students access, process, and engage with historical knowledge \cite{UNESCO2023}. Given that the systematic review of digital history pedagogy identified only 6 empirical studies (Section~\ref{sec:dh-review}), the present section examines gamification in history education --- the most extensively researched subset of digital approaches to history teaching --- to build the evidence base that digital history pedagogy currently lacks. The proliferation of digital sources, tools, and platforms creates both unprecedented opportunities and challenges: learners must navigate information overload, develop digital literacy, and construct historical understanding in increasingly mediated environments. Gamification -- the application of game design elements to non-game contexts \cite{Deterding2011} -- has emerged as a prominent pedagogical response to these challenges, attracting significant research attention in education over the past decade \cite{Hamari2014, Sailer2017, DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}.

\subsubsection{Conceptual boundaries of gamification}

The canonical definition by \citeauthor{Deterding2011}~\cite{Deterding2011} --- ``the use of game design elements in non-game contexts'' --- positions gamification within a broader continuum of game-informed pedagogical approaches. This continuum spans from minimal integration of game mechanics into existing activities to the deployment of purpose-built educational games (Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-spectrum}). \textit{Structural gamification} adds reward mechanisms (points, badges, leaderboards) to otherwise unchanged content. \textit{Content gamification} transforms the learning material itself to incorporate game-like elements such as narratives, quests, and role-playing scenarios. \textit{Game-based learning} (GBL) employs complete, pre-existing games as pedagogical tools \cite{Connolly2012, Prensky2001}. \textit{Serious games} are purpose-built applications designed for educational rather than entertainment objectives \cite{SEABORN201514}.

Existing taxonomies \cite{Deterding2011, SEABORN201514, Buckley2018} implicitly assume that the educational context is primary and game elements are added to it --- movement along the spectrum proceeds from education toward games. However, a distinct and undertheorised position exists where this directionality is reversed: an \textit{entertainment} game serves as the primary context, and educational micro-tasks (short quizzes, knowledge checks) are integrated as \textit{optional pathways to in-game rewards}. We term this position \textbf{reward-bridged learning} (RBL). In RBL, the learner's primary motivation is gameplay; educational engagement is incentivised through game-currency bonuses, unlockable content, or progression advantages earned by completing knowledge tasks. The educational content is explicit (the learner knows they are answering a quiz) but unobtrusive (it does not interrupt core gameplay and remains optional).

The most prominent commercial example is the Assassin's Creed Discovery Tour series (Ubisoft, 2018--2025), where an entertainment action game offers a separate educational mode with guided historical tours and quizzes \cite{12Gilbert2019108}. Recent experimental work by \cite{RBL2025} formalised this mechanism through a dual-application system (EduGym + EduShooter), demonstrating that coins earned in educational quizzes, when transferable to an entertainment game as upgrades, significantly increased learners' engagement with educational content compared to standalone quiz conditions. The concept is related to but distinct from \textit{tangential learning} \cite{frith2021}, where entertainment games inspire self-directed study without providing explicit educational tasks, and from \textit{stealth learning} \cite{ShuteFormative2008}, where educational content is embedded invisibly within gameplay. In RBL, education is neither tangential (it is explicit) nor stealthy (the learner knows they are being quizzed), but it is \textit{voluntary and rewarded} --- a motivational structure that leverages the entertainment context as an extrinsic incentive for educational effort.

While these categories are analytically distinct, in practice they often overlap --- a finding confirmed by the definitional analysis in Section~1.2.3, where 85.1\% of reviewed studies lacked explicit definitions of gamification \cite{Titova2025cte, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The identification of RBL as a distinct spectral position is an original contribution of this dissertation.

\subsubsection{Theoretical foundations of gamification in education} \label{sec:theory}

Understanding why and how gamification affects learning requires grounding in established psychological and design theories. Four theoretical perspectives provide complementary explanations for gamification's mechanisms of action (Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-theory}).

\textbf{Self-Determination Theory (SDT).} \citeauthor{RyanDeci2000}~\cite{RyanDeci2000} identified three innate psychological needs whose satisfaction drives intrinsic motivation: \textit{autonomy} (sense of volition and choice), \textit{competence} (feeling effective and capable), and \textit{relatedness} (connection to others). Gamification elements map directly onto these needs: points and progression systems provide competence feedback through visible indicators of mastery; choice mechanics, branching quests, and optional challenges support autonomy by granting learners agency over their learning path; leaderboards, team challenges, and collaborative quests foster relatedness through social comparison and cooperation \cite{Sailer2017}. Experimental evidence from \citeauthor{Sailer2017}~\cite{Sailer2017} demonstrated that specific game design elements satisfy specific SDT needs -- badges and leaderboards increased competence need satisfaction, while avatars and meaningful stories enhanced social relatedness. The SDT framework is particularly relevant for understanding why some gamification implementations succeed while others fail: designs that support all three needs tend to promote sustained intrinsic motivation, whereas those relying solely on extrinsic rewards (points without meaning, leaderboards without choice) may undermine it \cite{Gupta2022341}.

\textbf{Flow Theory.} \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}~\cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} described \textit{flow} as the state of optimal experience that occurs when there is a balance between the challenge of an activity and the individual's skill level, accompanied by clear goals and immediate feedback. Gamification supports flow through adaptive difficulty mechanisms that calibrate challenge to the learner's current ability, clearly defined objectives that reduce ambiguity, and instant feedback loops (points, progress bars, achievement notifications) that maintain the learner's awareness of performance \cite{Ho2024139}. In history education, where abstract concepts like causation and chronological reasoning can disengage learners, flow-inducing game structures help sustain concentration and reduce anxiety associated with complex historical analysis.

\textbf{MDA Framework.} \citeauthor{Hunicke2004}~\cite{Hunicke2004} proposed the Mechanics--Dynamics--Aesthetics (MDA) framework as a formal approach to understanding games. \textit{Mechanics} are the rules, actions, and control mechanisms designed by the creator; \textit{dynamics} are the emergent run-time behaviours that arise when learners interact with mechanics; \textit{aesthetics} are the emotional responses evoked by the experience. Applied to educational gamification, this framework highlights a critical insight: instructors design mechanics (point systems, quest structures, narrative frameworks), but learning emerges from dynamics -- the unpredictable interactions between learners, game elements, and content \cite{Bozic2024538}. The MDA framework thus cautions against evaluating gamification solely by its designed features (mechanics) without observing how learners actually engage with them (dynamics).

\textbf{Behaviourist reinforcement perspective.} Operant conditioning provides the most straightforward explanation for gamification's effects: immediate rewards (points, badges), feedback loops, and variable reinforcement schedules shape learner behaviour through positive reinforcement \cite{Nunez-Naranjo2024106}. While effective for establishing initial engagement, this perspective also identifies a significant risk -- the \textit{overjustification effect}, whereby the introduction of extrinsic rewards for an already intrinsically interesting activity can undermine intrinsic motivation once the rewards are removed \cite{Prieto-Andreu2024244}. This tension between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation is a recurring theme in the effectiveness literature reviewed in Section~1.2.5.

\subsubsection{Research landscape and rationale for the systematic review}

The systematic review of digital history pedagogy in Section~\ref{sec:dh-review} identified only 6 studies meeting rigorous inclusion criteria -- confirming that the field remains empirically underdeveloped. Gamification represents the most extensively researched subset of digital history methodology: a recent bibliometric analysis by \citeauthor{Shen2025}~\cite{Shen2025} of 249 publications spanning 29 years (1994--2023) confirmed steady growth in the field, identified six distinct research clusters, and highlighted the multidisciplinary nature of gamification research across history, education, and computer science.

Despite this volume of primary research, systematic synthesis has been limited. \cite{33Oceja2022419} conducted a systematic review focused specifically on secondary education, but no comprehensive review has covered all educational levels and the full range of gamification approaches. The author's systematic review \citeauthor{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}~\cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} addressed this gap by identifying 74 studies -- a twelve-fold increase over the digital history review -- providing the empirical foundation for the analysis that follows in Sections~1.2.2--1.2.8.

\subsection{Methodology}

The systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines \citep{Page2021}. Four research questions guided the review:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How is gamification defined and conceptualised in history education?
\item What is the current state of research on gamification effectiveness in history education?
\item What are the key design principles, strategies, and technologies used?
\item What are the main challenges, limitations, and potential negative effects?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Search strategy.} Three databases were searched: Scopus \cite{scopus}, Web of Science \cite{wos}, and Dimensions \cite{dimensions}. The search strategy combined gamification-related terms with history education terms in paper titles:

\begin{quote}
\texttt{TITLE (history AND (game* OR gamification) AND (education OR teaching OR learning OR student* OR instruction))}
\end{quote}

Limits were applied for time frame (2000--2024), language (English or Ukrainian), and publication type (peer-reviewed articles, conference papers, book chapters).

\textbf{Inclusion criteria:} (1) students in primary, secondary, or higher education learning history as a discipline; (2) gamification techniques, elements, or strategies applied in history education; (3) empirical studies using quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods; (4) formal or informal educational settings; (5) published between 2000 and 2024; (6) indexed in Scopus, Web of Science, or Dimensions.

\textbf{Exclusion criteria:} (1) studies not focused on history as a discipline (e.g., history of mathematics, history of science); (2) secondary research (systematic reviews, meta-analyses); (3) studies with no gamification intervention; (4) non-empirical publications (theoretical essays, editorials, position papers).

\textbf{Screening process.} The initial search yielded 4,011 records across the three databases \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. After duplicate removal and title-abstract screening, 80 potentially relevant studies were retained for full-text assessment. During full-text analysis, 6 studies were excluded against the exclusion criteria: 3 were not about history as a discipline (violating exclusion criterion~1), 1 was a systematic review rather than a primary study (criterion~2), 1 addressed the history of mathematics rather than history education (criterion~1), and 1 contained no gamification intervention (criterion~3). The final corpus comprised 74 studies published between 2004 and 2024 across 27 countries. Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-prisma} presents the complete selection process.

\subsection{Definitional analysis} \label{sec:definitional-analysis}

\subsubsection{Prevalence of definitional ambiguity}

A critical finding of this review concerns the use of definitions. Of the 74 included studies:

\begin{itemize}
\item Only 11 studies (14.9\%) provided a clear, explicit definition of gamification;
\item 63 studies (85.1\%) used the term without providing an operational definition;
\item Among those providing definitions, the most commonly cited was that of Deterding et al. (2011): ``the use of game design elements in non-game contexts'' \citep{Deterding2011};
\item Several studies conflated gamification with game-based learning, serious games, or educational games without distinguishing between these related but distinct concepts \cite{36Scholz2021, 42Huffer201581, 43ali2018indonesian}.
\end{itemize}

This definitional ambiguity has significant implications for research synthesis and practice \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019, encyclopedia3040089}. Without clear definitions, it is difficult to compare findings across studies, replicate research, or develop evidence-based guidelines for practitioners \cite{Clark2016, Buckley2018}.

The definitional approaches differed systematically by disciplinary orientation. Studies rooted in educational technology and human--computer interaction tended to define gamification narrowly as the addition of game elements to non-game contexts, emphasising mechanics such as points, leaderboards, and badges \cite{Deterding2011, SEABORN201514}. In contrast, studies from history education and the humanities conceptualised the approach more broadly as game-based or experiential learning, foregrounding narrative immersion, historical empathy, and perspective-taking \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 55Squire2004, 56Akkerman2009449}. A third group of studies from computer science and design framed the concept through a software engineering lens, emphasising system design and usability \cite{Souza2018201, Striuk_2022}. This disciplinary fragmentation helps explain both the terminological inconsistency and the diversity of theoretical frameworks employed \cite{Buckley2018, Calderon2016}.

The low definitional rate is not mere carelessness but reflects structural factors. Many authors are historians or educators rather than gamification specialists and may view the concept as self-evident. As \citeauthor{Buckley2018}~\cite{Buckley2018} observed, the rapid popularisation of gamification terminology has outpaced conceptual consolidation --- terms become fashionable before their boundaries are established. The interchangeable use of ``gamification,'' ``game-based learning,'' ``digital game-based learning,'' and ``serious games'' observed in the corpus confirms this terminological drift \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}. Furthermore, the field's multidisciplinary character means that authors import terminology from their home disciplines without cross-referencing competing usages: a computer scientist's ``gamification'' (system with point mechanics) and a historian's ``gamification'' (immersive role-play scenario) may describe fundamentally different interventions \cite{Calderon2016}.

This ambiguity has measurable methodological consequences. Studies providing explicit definitions demonstrate higher reporting quality overall (Section~1.3), and the declining definitional rate over time (Section~1.3) suggests that the field's growth since 2018 has not been accompanied by conceptual tightening. Cross-domain comparison reveals that even within gamification research more broadly, history education exhibits notably low definitional rates (Section~1.2.4). These patterns collectively indicate that definitional analysis is not a merely taxonomic exercise but a prerequisite for meaningful synthesis of the evidence base.

\subsubsection{Conceptual framings of explicit definitions}

Among the 11 studies that provided definitions (Table~\ref{tab:explicit-definitions}), the definitions cluster into three distinct framings that reflect fundamentally different understandings of the relationship between game and learning \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Titova2025cte}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Game mechanics overlay} (Ali 2018, Serrano 2023, Scholz 2021): gamification as adding discrete game elements to unchanged educational activities. Core learning activities remain educational; game elements are \textit{layered onto} them as a motivational framework \cite{Titova2025cte}. This framing corresponds most closely to \citeauthor{Deterding2011}'s canonical definition \cite{Deterding2011}.

\item \textbf{Serious games framework} (Huffer 2015, Petersen 2023, Lin 2011): the game \textit{is} the pedagogical tool --- a purpose-built artefact where gameplay directly teaches content \cite{42Huffer201581, 41Petersen2023301, 52Lin2011265}. Gameplay is not a motivational overlay but the primary delivery medium \cite{Mitchell2024184}.

\item \textbf{Educational integration} (Zin 2008, Zin 2009, Razak 2011, with Shih 2015 and Lynch 2015 as boundary cases): definitions blur the game/pedagogy boundary, conceptualising the intervention as a ``melding'' of educational content, learning principles, and game design \cite{30Zin2008, 23Zin2009322, 65Razak201139}. The distinction between gamification and game-based learning is explicitly treated as porous.
\end{enumerate}

These three framings form a continuum of increasing pedagogical transformation (Figure~\ref{fig:definitional-framings}). In the overlay model, games \textit{support} learning; in the serious games model, games \textit{deliver} learning; in the integration model, games and learning become \textit{indistinguishable}. This tripartite classification reveals why definitional ambiguity persists: researchers within different framings describe genuinely different interventions under the same umbrella. The teacher adding quiz points (overlay) and the developer building a VR historical simulation (serious games) may both use ``gamification,'' yet their interventions differ in scope, complexity, and mechanism \cite{Titova2025cte, Buckley2018}. This classification aligns with but extends the conceptual spectrum in Section~1.2.1 (Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-spectrum}) by grounding it in the empirical definitions extracted from the 74-study corpus.

\subsubsection{Pedagogical conceptualisations in history education}

Beyond formal definitions, the 74 studies revealed how gamification is \textit{conceptualised} --- what pedagogical role researchers assign to it within history education. Five recurring pedagogical lenses were identified \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Immersive experiential learning} --- environments where students interact with historical content through exploration and discovery; VR, 3D, and interactive storytelling applications \cite{55Squire2004}.
\item \textit{Student-centred engagement} --- shifting from teacher-centred exposition to student-centred interaction through strategic decision-making and active participation.
\item \textit{Historical empathy and perspective-taking} --- narrative video games and board games as tools for fostering emotional connections to historical events through role-play \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 56Akkerman2009449}.
\item \textit{Problem-solving and inquiry} --- games as instruments for historical inquiry and critical thinking, allowing interactive engagement with content \cite{55Squire2004}.
\item \textit{Visualisation and engagement} --- making history ``alive'' through visual interactive gameplay, improving memorisation through concrete representation of abstract historical processes.
\end{itemize}

Four of the five lenses are \textit{generic educational constructs} applying equally to science, mathematics, or language instruction. Only the third --- historical empathy and perspective-taking --- engages directly with the epistemological traditions of the history discipline. This aligns with the theoretical frameworks data in Section~1.2.4, where game-based learning (27 studies) and flow theory (11 studies) dominated while historical thinking appeared in only 7 studies \cite{Wineburg2001}. Researchers conceptualise gamification in history through general pedagogical language rather than the disciplinary vocabulary of sourcing, contextualisation, corroboration, and close reading.

The sparse connections reveal a conceptual gap with practical consequences: gamification designed through generic lenses may enhance engagement but fail to develop the specific analytical competences characterising expert historical reasoning \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. This gap provides a mandate for the methodology in Chapter~3, which explicitly integrates gamification mechanics with historical thinking competences \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsubsection{Conceptual approaches} \label{sec:conceptual-approaches}

We identified four distinct conceptual approaches to gamification in the literature:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Structural gamification}: Adding game elements (points, badges, leaderboards) to existing educational content without modifying the content itself \cite{Deterding2011, SEABORN201514}. Core activities remain educational; game elements are layered as a motivational framework \cite{Humeniuk2022113, Titova2025cte}. This is the most common and readily implementable approach, requiring minimal redesign of existing curricula.

\item \textbf{Content gamification}: Transforming educational content to make it more game-like (narratives, characters, challenges, choices) \cite{43ali2018indonesian, 34Serrano2023}. Unlike structural gamification, this modifies \textit{what} students engage with, not merely the reward framework. Historical quests, role-playing scenarios, and branching decision trees exemplify this approach.

\item \textbf{Full gamification}: Comprehensive integration of game mechanics, dynamics, and aesthetics into the learning experience \cite{36Scholz2021, Sailer2017}. The MDA framework (\citeauthor{Hunicke2004},~\cite{Hunicke2004}) is systematically applied: mechanics (rules, progression), dynamics (emergent learner behaviour), and aesthetics (emotional engagement) are jointly designed to serve learning objectives.

\item \textbf{Game-based learning}: Using complete games (digital or analogue) as the primary pedagogical tool, which some studies classified under gamification \cite{30Zin2008, 23Zin2009322, Qian2016, Prensky2001}. Gameplay itself teaches content rather than being layered onto activities \cite{Mitchell2024184, Titova2025cte}. The inclusion of GBL under the gamification umbrella in some studies contributes to the terminological confusion documented above.
\end{enumerate}

These four approaches form a gradient of increasing transformation that maps onto the tripartite framing in Figure~\ref{fig:definitional-framings}. Structural gamification corresponds to the ``overlay'' framing, content and full gamification bridge into ``educational integration,'' and GBL aligns with the ``serious games'' framing. A critical practical implication from \cite{Titova2025cte}: structural gamification is ``potentially easier to implement within existing educational structures but may limit its transformative potential.''

The terminological heterogeneity documented in this section presents fundamental challenges for systematic comparison \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Buckley2018}. The classification proposed here --- three conceptual framings, five pedagogical lenses, and four implementation approaches --- offers an initial integrative framework grounded in the empirical analysis of 74 studies \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. This framework informs the definitional choices and design principles adopted in the experimental methodology of Chapter~3.

The convergence of findings across independently constructed corpora strengthens confidence in the periodisation proposed here.

Notably, the 2020--2021 period sustained research output (4--5 studies per year) despite the COVID-19 pandemic. Remote education created new contexts and institutional demand for digital gamification tools, which may have partially offset the disruption to classroom-based experimental research \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsubsection{Publication types, database coverage, and evidence maturity}

The majority of included studies were conference papers (n=40, 54.1\%), followed by journal articles (n=24, 32.4\%), book chapters (n=9, 12.2\%), and one preprint (1.4\%). Scopus was the most comprehensive source, covering 62 of 74 studies (83.8\%). Dimensions contributed 11 unique studies (14.8\%) not indexed in Scopus or Web of Science, demonstrating the value of searching multiple databases --- a single-database search would miss approximately 15\% of the available evidence, with implications for future systematic reviews in this domain.

The temporal evolution of publication types reveals a gradual maturation trend. During the early exploration stage (2004--2012), journal articles constituted only 28\% of output (5 of 18 studies), with conference papers and book chapters dominating initial dissemination. In the narrative and engagement stage (2013--2017), the journal proportion rose to 42\% (8 of 19 studies). During the immersive technology stage (2018--2024), journal articles reached 46\% (17 of 37 studies), though conference papers remained the single largest category. Figure~\ref{fig:publication-type-evolution} visualises this trend.

\input{figures/publication_type_evolution}

The persistent conference-paper dominance (54.1\% overall) is a diagnostic indicator of field maturity. In established fields such as medical education, where gamification has also attracted systematic review attention, journal articles dominate the evidence base: \citeauthor{vanGaalen2021}~\cite{vanGaalen2021} found that their 44-study corpus on gamification in health professions was drawn predominantly from peer-reviewed journals. The contrast reflects different disciplinary norms and the relative youth of gamification research in history education. Conference papers undergo shorter peer review, receive less rigorous methodological scrutiny, and --- critically --- their findings may never reach journal publication. A corpus in which the majority of evidence comes from such venues warrants interpretive caution.

The 54.1\% conference rate, combined with the definitional instability documented in Section~\ref{sec:definitional-analysis} (only 14.9\% of studies providing explicit definitions) and the near-absence of randomised controlled trials (Section~\ref{sec:research-designs}), collectively indicate that gamification in history education remains in a \textit{pre-consolidation phase} of evidence development. This interpretation is not merely descriptive but diagnostic: it motivates both the methodological quality assessment framework developed in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs} and the stronger experimental design adopted in Chapter~3 \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsubsection{Education levels and target populations}

Secondary education was the most studied context (n=35, 47.3\%), followed by higher education (n=21, 28.4\%) and primary education (n=13, 17.6\%). Five studies (6.8\%) addressed multiple levels or did not specify.

The predominance of secondary education reflects several converging factors. Secondary curricula across many national systems include substantial history content, providing a natural context for gamification interventions. Adolescent learners are at a developmental stage where game-based motivation aligns well with emerging autonomy and peer-oriented social dynamics \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 11Ternblad2023115}. Institutional support is also stronger: secondary schools often adopt technology integration initiatives that provide infrastructure and ethical approval pathways for classroom-based research \cite{15Slater2022, Adams2022119}.

Despite constituting 28.4\% of the corpus, higher education studies present distinct characteristics that warrant separate consideration. University contexts involve voluntary or elective enrollment, different motivation profiles (intrinsic academic interest vs. compulsory attendance), and different assessment traditions compared to secondary schools. These contextual differences mean that gamification findings from secondary settings cannot be straightforwardly generalised to higher education \cite{Ahlfeld2021493}. The present dissertation's NMT preparatory course context occupies a unique position at the secondary-to-tertiary transition, where participants are secondary school graduates preparing for university entrance --- a population underrepresented in both subcorpora.

A cross-tabulation of education level against game type reveals patterns not previously articulated in the literature (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-cross-dimensions}). Secondary education studies disproportionately employ board and card games and commercial off-the-shelf titles, reflecting both the availability of age-appropriate commercial games and the lower technological barriers of non-digital formats in classroom settings. Higher education studies show greater concentration of VR/AR interventions and custom digital games, which require specialised equipment and technical infrastructure more commonly available in university laboratories. Primary education studies favour web-based and mobile formats, consistent with the simpler interfaces appropriate for younger learners and the ubiquity of tablets in primary school EdTech programmes.

This education-level--game-type interaction has implications for evidence interpretation: the predominantly positive engagement findings reported across education levels (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}) may partly reflect the matching of game format to learner context rather than a universal effect of gamification per se.

The field thus grows in volume without commensurately strengthening its causal foundations --- a maturity indicator consistent with the publication type analysis above.

\subsubsection{Theoretical frameworks and disciplinary grounding}

The studies cited diverse theoretical frameworks. The detailed distribution across the corpus is as follows: game-based learning frameworks (GBL) were the most frequently referenced (27 studies) \cite{30Zin2008, 23Zin2009322, Prensky2001, Qian2016}, followed by flow theory (11 studies) \cite{16Chen201447, 19Chou2019, 70Hou201519}, experiential learning (9 studies) \cite{Constructivism, Bonwell1991}, constructivism (9 studies), collaborative learning (8 studies), situated learning (8 studies), motivation theories (7 studies) \cite{Sailer2017, Hamari2014}, historical thinking frameworks (5 studies), design frameworks (7 studies), the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM, 3 studies), historical empathy (2 studies), and self-determination theory (SDT, 2 studies).

These frameworks can be classified into a three-tier typology that reveals the disciplinary grounding of the field:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tier~1 --- Generic educational theories} (constructivism, experiential learning, collaborative learning, situated learning): approximately 34 citations across studies. These frameworks are applicable to any subject area and provide pedagogical grounding without discipline-specific content.

\item \textbf{Tier~2 --- Technology and game-specific frameworks} (GBL, flow theory, gamification frameworks, motivation theories, design frameworks, TAM, SDT): approximately 50 citations. These frameworks address the game or technology dimension of the intervention but are equally applicable to gamification in mathematics, science, or language learning.

\item \textbf{Tier~3 --- History-discipline-specific frameworks} (historical thinking, historical empathy): only 7 citations across the entire corpus (9.5\%).
\end{itemize}

\input{figures/theoretical_framework_tiers}

The \textbf{34:50:7 ratio} across tiers constitutes the most analytically significant finding of this evidence base characterisation. It reveals that gamification in history education is overwhelmingly theorised from \textit{outside} the history discipline. The field draws its conceptual foundations from general educational psychology (Tier~1) and from game/technology studies (Tier~2), while the epistemological traditions that define what it means to know and do history are nearly absent from its theoretical architecture.

This finding is not entirely surprising in the context of broader educational technology research. \citeauthor{Hew2019}~\cite{Hew2019} found that across 503 studies in the field of educational technology, explicit engagement with theory was absent or superficial in the majority. The gamification-in-history corpus performs better on theory \textit{presence} --- 94.6\% of studies cite at least one theoretical framework, as captured by MRQS item M9 (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}). However, citing a theory is not equivalent to using it to generate hypotheses, guide intervention design, or interpret findings. The distinction between theoretical \textit{citation} and theoretical \textit{integration} remains a concern across all three tiers.

The consequences of the Tier~3 gap are threefold. First, outcome measures default to generic engagement and satisfaction metrics rather than historical thinking competences --- only 18.9\% of studies measured higher-order historical reasoning (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}). Second, game design prioritises generic mechanics (points, badges, leaderboards) over history-specific affordances such as source analysis, perspective-taking, or chronological reasoning. Third, and most fundamentally, the evidence base cannot answer whether gamification develops the competences that distinguish history from other subjects, because the theoretical frameworks employed do not conceptualise those competences.

The 7 studies that do engage with historical thinking frameworks \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 56Akkerman2009449, 55Squire2004, 78Rouse2022357} report qualitatively different findings: they assess historical empathy, source evaluation, and perspective-taking rather than factual recall, and they describe more nuanced relationships between game design and disciplinary outcomes. This contrast --- between the 67 studies using generic frameworks and the 7 using discipline-specific ones --- suggests that theoretical grounding does not merely label research but actively shapes what it can discover.

This disciplinary identity gap directly informs the methodological choices of the present dissertation. The experimental design in Chapter~3 grounds gamification in historical thinking competences derived from \cite{Wineburg2001}, \cite{MonteSano2012}, and \cite{Reisman2012}, addressing the Tier~3 deficit by connecting game mechanics to discipline-specific learning objectives rather than generic engagement proxies.

\subsubsection{Comparison with gamification in other domains}

The characteristics of the history education evidence base can be contextualised by comparison with gamification research in other domains. A parallel systematic review of gamification in software engineering education \citep{Korniienko2025aredu} identified 29 studies from an initial pool of 486 records. Several patterns are consistent across domains:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Positive but uncertain effects}: Both reviews found predominantly positive effects on engagement and motivation, with more mixed evidence for learning outcomes \cite{Korniienko2025aredu, DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}. In software engineering, the average effect on learning outcomes was small (Cohen's d = 0.23), with low certainty of evidence according to GRADE assessment \cite{GRADE, GuyattGRADE2008, Guyatt2008}.
\item \textbf{Methodological limitations}: Both domains are characterised by small sample sizes, lack of control groups, and reliance on unvalidated instruments \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}. In software engineering, 18 of 29 studies (62.1\%) had serious overall risk of bias \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}.
\item \textbf{Game element preferences}: Software engineering gamification more commonly employed structural elements (points in 17/29 studies, leaderboards in 11/29, badges in 9/29) \cite{Souza2018201, Striuk_2022, Polat_2023, SEABORN201514, Akpolat2014149, Berkling2013, DetelingRiedl2015, Mora2016, Iosup2014, Hasan2018, Matsubara2017, Bartel2016, Fuchs2016}, while history education relied more heavily on narrative and immersive elements (narrative/storytelling in 44/74 studies, immersion/VR/AR in 55/74).
\item \textbf{Definitional consistency}: Neither domain showed strong definitional practices, though the history education review found even lower rates of explicit definition (14.9\% vs. approximately 30\% in software engineering) \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}.
\end{itemize}

A comparison with health professions education further contextualises these patterns. \cite{vanGaalen2021} systematically reviewed 44 studies of gamification in health professions and found no negative outcomes (compared to 10.8\% negative findings in the history education corpus), assessment attributes reported in 40 of 44 studies (compared to only 25.7\% providing adequate outcome description in history, per MRQS M5), and only 7 studies explaining the mechanisms through which gamification produced effects. History education relies more heavily on narrative and immersive elements while health professions uses assessment-driven feedback loops --- a difference that reflects disciplinary culture rather than gamification design principles.

A parallel pattern emerges in K-12 education broadly. \citeauthor{Dehghanzadeh2024}~\cite{Dehghanzadeh2024} reviewed 54 studies of gamification across K-12 subjects and found similar characteristics: predominantly positive engagement outcomes, less clear cognitive effects, and geographic concentration in developed-country contexts. The consistency of these limitations across history, software engineering, health professions, and general K-12 education suggests that the methodological challenges identified here are structural features of the gamification evidence base rather than discipline-specific shortcomings \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019, Hamari2014, Sailer2017, Morales-Trujillo2021, Korniienko2025aredu}.

\subsubsection{Evidence base maturity profile} \label{sec:evidence-maturity}

Rather than treating geographic distribution, publication types, research designs, theoretical frameworks, and sample sizes as independent descriptors, the preceding analysis permits their synthesis into a jointly diagnostic \textit{evidence maturity profile}. A mature evidence base in any educational intervention domain would exhibit five characteristics: (1)~diverse geographic representation ensuring cross-cultural generalisability, (2)~journal-dominant publication ensuring rigorous peer review, (3)~experimental design prevalence supporting causal inference, (4)~discipline-specific theoretical grounding connecting interventions to subject-matter epistemology, and (5)~adequate sample sizes providing statistical power for effect detection.

The gamification-in-history-education corpus exhibits none of these characteristics fully (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-maturity-profile}).

\input{figures/evidence_maturity_profile}

Four countries account for 55\% of studies (geographic diversity: low). Conference papers constitute 54.1\% of the corpus (journal dominance: low). Only 24.3\% of studies support causal inference (experimental designs: low). Only 33.9\% of studies reporting sample sizes exceed $n = 30$ (adequate samples: low). And only 9.5\% of theoretical citations engage with the history discipline (disciplinary theory: very low).

These indicators are not independent. Geographic concentration in East Asia and Europe correlates with the dominance of VR/AR game types, which require technological infrastructure unavailable in underrepresented regions. Conference-paper dominance is associated with lower methodological rigour (as the MRQS assessment in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs} demonstrates). Small samples and the absence of control groups produce underpowered studies whose positive findings --- however consistent --- cannot be interpreted as causal evidence in the GRADE sense \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}.

The predominantly positive effectiveness findings reported in Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness} must therefore be interpreted with explicit reference to this maturity profile. Positive results from conference papers with small samples, no control groups, and generic theoretical frameworks do not constitute robust evidence of gamification's effectiveness for history learning. The field produces encouraging signals but not causal evidence. This conclusion is not new \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}, but grounding it in specific, quantified maturity indicators adds empirical precision to what has previously been stated in general terms.

Three priorities emerge for the field's development: (1)~geographic diversification, particularly in post-Soviet, African, and Latin American contexts where history education traditions differ markedly from the East Asian and Western European norm; (2)~methodological strengthening through RCTs, validated instruments, and effect size reporting; and (3)~disciplinary integration, connecting gamification design and assessment to historical thinking frameworks rather than generic educational theories \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The present dissertation addresses all three priorities: it introduces a post-Soviet Ukrainian context, employs a quasi-experimental design with validated instruments, and grounds its gamification approach in historical thinking competences.

These evidence base characteristics motivate both the detailed methodological quality assessment in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs} (which diagnoses quality at the individual study level) and the experimental methodology in Chapter~3 (which demonstrates a methodologically stronger approach to gamification research in history education).

\subsection{Effectiveness findings} \label{sec:effectiveness}

\subsubsection{Outcome types and the ease-of-measurement hierarchy}

The review synthesised effectiveness evidence across multiple outcome categories \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The most frequently measured outcome was student engagement (52 studies, 70.3\%), followed by knowledge acquisition (45 studies, 60.8\%), attitudes and satisfaction (39 studies, 52.7\%), and motivation (26 studies, 35.1\%). Higher-order thinking skills, including historical thinking and empathy, were assessed in only 14 studies (18.9\%) \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 56Akkerman2009449, 55Squire2004, 78Rouse2022357, 59Kee2014270}. Flow and immersion were measured in 15 studies \cite{16Chen201447, 19Chou2019, 70Hou201519, 28Chao201734}, collaboration in 18, and usability in 13.

This distribution is not random. It reflects a structural \textit{ease-of-measurement hierarchy} (Figure~\ref{fig:outcome-taxonomy}): outcome frequency inversely correlates with measurement difficulty. Engagement --- the most frequently assessed outcome --- is also the easiest to operationalise through platform analytics, attendance logs, and time-on-task metrics. Knowledge acquisition requires only pre/post testing with factual recall items. Attitudes rely on Likert-scale surveys. Motivation demands validated instruments (e.g., ARCS model) and careful control for social desirability bias. Historical thinking --- the least measured outcome --- requires discipline-specific instruments, trained raters, and expertise in historical epistemology to design assessments that capture source analysis, contextualisation, and perspective-taking competences \cite{MonteSano2012, Seixas2013}.

\input{figures/outcome_taxonomy}

The hierarchy reveals a structural bias in the evidence base: the field measures what is methodologically convenient, not what matters most for history education. In health professions gamification, 40 of 44 studies measured assessment outcomes directly \cite{vanGaalen2021}; in history education, only 18.9\% assess discipline-specific outcomes. The gap is wider than in comparable fields, suggesting that historical thinking outcomes are not merely difficult to measure but are systematically excluded from research designs that lack disciplinary theoretical grounding --- a pattern linked to the 9.5\% disciplinary theory engagement rate identified in Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}. The predominantly positive findings reported below must therefore be interpreted with this hierarchy in mind: they are disproportionately about engagement and satisfaction --- the least informative outcomes for evaluating whether gamification develops historical understanding.

\subsubsection{Overall direction of effects and the engagement-learning decoupling}

Of the 74 studies, 12 (16.2\%) reported exclusively positive results across all measured outcomes, 37 (50.0\%) reported mixed results (both positive and null or negative findings across different outcome measures), 18 (24.3\%) reported null or negative results, and 7 (9.5\%) did not measure learning or engagement outcomes quantitatively \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The high proportion of mixed results underscores the complexity of gamification effects and the importance of examining specific outcome domains rather than drawing blanket conclusions about ``effectiveness'' \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Clark2016}.

Cross-tabulating outcome direction by domain reveals the section's central finding --- the \textit{engagement-learning decoupling}. Engagement outcomes were positive in approximately 85\% of reporting studies; attitudes and satisfaction in approximately 84\%. However, learning outcomes were positive in only 51.4\% of studies. The approximately 19 percentage point gap between engagement-positive and learning-positive rates demonstrates that gamification reliably increases participation and motivation but does not automatically transfer to learning gains. This pattern aligns with recent meta-analytic evidence: \citeauthor{SailerHomner2020}~\cite{SailerHomner2020} found that gamification has significant effects on both cognitive learning outcomes ($g = 0.49$) and motivational outcomes ($g = 0.36$), but with substantial heterogeneity ($I^2 > 80\%$), indicating that average effects mask wide variation in individual study results. \cite{BaiHewHuang2020}, synthesising 30 experimental and quasi-experimental studies, confirmed overall positive but heterogeneous effects, with competition as the only game element showing a consistent positive association with learning outcomes.

The 50\% ``mixed'' category is analytically revealing. Most ``mixed'' studies reported positive engagement alongside null or negative learning outcomes --- confirming the decoupling pattern. Only 5 studies showed the reverse (negative engagement, positive learning). This asymmetry suggests that engagement is a necessary but insufficient condition for learning: gamification can produce enjoyable experiences that do not translate into measurable knowledge or skill acquisition without explicit pedagogical scaffolding.

The 10.8\% negative rate is higher than the 0\% reported in health professions gamification \cite{vanGaalen2021} but lower than in EdTech broadly. Notably, the history-specific negative effects --- historical misconceptions, ideological bias, fact-fiction confusion --- have no parallel in other disciplines and are examined in detail in Section~\ref{sec:harm-landscape} below. These discipline-specific harms are a unique contribution of the present review.

\subsubsection{Student engagement and motivation}

The majority of studies (n=52, 70.3\%) reported positive effects of gamification on student engagement or motivation. Common findings included increased participation in voluntary activities, longer time-on-task, and higher self-reported motivation. Behavioural engagement metrics (platform analytics, participation rates) showed the most consistent positive effects. Specific quantitative findings included:

\begin{itemize}
\item A significant correlation between perceived reality and engagement (r = .70, p $<$ .001) in a VR-based application \citeauthor{1Barbatsis201159}~\cite{1Barbatsis201159};
\item 73\% of students positively motivated in a collaborative gaming study, with 93\% having prior experience with gaming \cite{3Watson2011466};
\item 100\% of surveyed students expressing willingness to play the game again in one mobile-based study \cite{58Shih2015173};
\item Mean engagement scores above 5 on a 7-point scale across multiple VR studies \cite{10Huaman2019220, 31DeAmicis2009451};
\item Over 90\% of students showing positive motivation toward a game-based system, with satisfaction scores averaging 4.51 out of 5 \cite{64Hsu201324};
\item A significant gender difference in engagement, with boys demonstrating statistically higher engagement compared to girls (F(1,64) = 4.408, p = .040) \cite{49Huizenga2007127}.
\end{itemize}

However, the sustainability of these effects beyond initial novelty periods was rarely examined \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Hamari2014}. Some studies documented that not all gamification strategies were equally motivating: competitive timed games generated stress in some students \cite{11Ternblad2023115}, approximately 10\% of participants in one study reported being negatively motivated \cite{47Clara2010156}, and female students were less familiar with or interested in strategy-based games \cite{49Huizenga2007127}. These findings highlight the importance of considering individual differences and offering multiple pathways within gamified environments \cite{Sailer2017}.

While these engagement findings are robust, their value for educational effectiveness must be assessed against the learning outcome data below. Behavioural engagement measured through platform analytics shows more consistent positive effects than self-reported engagement --- measurement method matters for interpreting the strength of these findings.

\subsubsection{Learning outcomes and calibrated effect size interpretation}

Results regarding academic achievement were more mixed \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}:
\begin{itemize}
\item 38 studies (51.4\%) reported positive effects on learning outcomes;
\item 28 studies (37.8\%) reported no significant difference compared to control conditions;
\item 8 studies (10.8\%) reported negative or mixed effects \cite{47Clara2010156, Oceja2022419}.
\end{itemize}

Of these, only 7 included control or comparison groups, limiting causal inference for the remainder. The reported effect sizes ($d = 0.28$--$0.61$) require contextualisation against established educational benchmarks (Figure~\ref{fig:effect-size-forest}). \citeauthor{Kraft2020}~\cite{Kraft2020} demonstrated that for scalable educational interventions evaluated with rigorous designs, $d > 0.20$ qualifies as ``large'' and $d > 0.05$ as ``medium.'' By this standard, gamification effects are substantial. Against Hattie's meta-analytic ``hinge point'' of $d = 0.40$ \cite{Hattie2009}, most gamification effects cluster near or above this threshold.

However, 9 of the 16 studies reporting inferential statistics are pre-post designs without control groups. Pre-post designs systematically inflate effect sizes by $0.15$--$0.30$ due to maturation, testing effects, and regression to the mean. After adjusting for this inflation, the true causal effect of gamification on history learning is likely in the range $d = 0.15$--$0.30$ --- still meaningful by Kraft's benchmarks but substantially smaller than headline figures suggest. This aligns with \cite{BaiHewHuang2020}, who found that experimental studies consistently show smaller effects than quasi-experimental ones, and with \cite{SailerHomner2020}, whose meta-analytic pooled effect ($g = 0.49$) is moderated by study quality. The field's positive findings are partially a methodological artifact of study designs that cannot separate gamification effects from confounding factors.

This pattern suggests that gamification may be more effective for developing surface-level knowledge than for fostering deep historical thinking \cite{Clark2016, Connolly2012}. Qualitative studies provided additional evidence of learning enhancement, including higher retention scores in game-based learning compared to traditional methods \cite{43ali2018indonesian}, increased critical thinking \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 78Rouse2022357}, improved collaboration \cite{3Watson2011466, 46Stirling2022}, and the development of systemic understanding of history through recursive cycles of failure and strategy revision \cite{55Squire2004, 57Huntley2011567}.

\subsubsection{Flow and immersion as causal mechanism}

Eight studies specifically measured flow states using validated instruments \cite{16Chen201447, 19Chou2019, 28Chao201734, 44Shiue2016, 45Yu2016727, 70Hou201519, 64Hsu201324, 67Lin201296}, providing evidence that history games can achieve meaningful immersion. All reported flow scores above scale midpoints.

Detailed analysis of flow dimensions across six studies reveals a consistent pattern. Concentration and immersion scored highest: \citeauthor{16Chen201447}~\cite{16Chen201447} reported concentration $M = 3.80$ and time distortion $M = 3.79$ (5-point scale); \citeauthor{63Liu2021}~\cite{63Liu2021} found autotelic experience $M = 4.54$ and concentration $M = 4.50$; \citeauthor{64Hsu201324}~\cite{64Hsu201324} reported flow antecedents $M = 4.06$ with four dimensions above 4.00; \citeauthor{70Hou201519}~\cite{70Hou201519} found all nine flow dimensions above scale median with concentration highest; \citeauthor{48Moketar2021}~\cite{48Moketar2021} reported immersion $M = 4.23$ and concentration $M = 4.13$. By contrast, loss of self-consciousness consistently scored lowest ($M = 3.29$--$3.68$) across all studies reporting this dimension \cite{63Liu2021, 64Hsu201324, 16Chen201447}.

This pattern suggests that history games achieve focused attention and temporal absorption but not the complete ``loss of self'' characteristic of full flow states. The distinction is educationally significant: historical content requires reflective processing --- evaluating sources, considering perspectives, questioning narratives --- that is fundamentally incompatible with complete self-dissolution. History games may achieve what \cite{16Chen201447} termed ``partial flow'': sufficient immersion to sustain attention without eliminating the metacognitive awareness necessary for critical historical thinking.

The flow-learning relationship has limited direct evidence. Only \citeauthor{45Yu2016727}~\cite{45Yu2016727} reported a significant correlation between flow state and learning achievement ($p < .05$). This single finding, though widely cited, remains unreplicated in the corpus. Flow therefore functions as an \textit{engagement amplifier} rather than a direct learning mechanism: the causal chain from flow through sustained attention to deeper processing (Path~A in Section~\ref{sec:harm-landscape}) requires pedagogical scaffolding to translate enjoyable experience into historical understanding. Without such scaffolding, flow produces immersion without learning gain --- another manifestation of the engagement-learning decoupling identified above.

\subsubsection{Historical thinking skills and the disciplinary gap}

Eighteen studies specifically examined the development of historical thinking competences, though only 14 (18.9\%) formally assessed these through validated instruments \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Seixas2013}. Of the 18, 12 reported positive outcomes and 6 found no significant effects.

These 14 studies can be categorised by which historical thinking competence they assessed, using the ``Big Six'' framework of \cite{Seixas2013}: source analysis (5 studies), contextualisation (4 studies), perspective-taking and historical empathy (7 studies), and causal reasoning (2 studies). The distribution is revealing: perspective-taking --- the most ``game-friendly'' competence, naturally served by narrative role-playing --- dominates. Causal reasoning and corroboration --- competences requiring systematic analytical work with multiple sources --- are nearly absent from the gamification literature.

Game type determines which historical thinking competences develop. Studies using narrative role-playing games \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 78Rouse2022357, 56Akkerman2009449} consistently reported empathy and perspective-taking gains. Studies employing multi-perspectival digital archives and document-based games \cite{59Kee2014270, 60Hoy2018} found source analysis improvements, as students encountered conflicting accounts requiring critical evaluation. Strategy games \cite{55Squire2004, 40Kuran2018} fostered systemic thinking about causation and political economy, while board games promoted perspective-taking through role-playing elements \cite{46Stirling2022, 76Corbeil2011462}. This pattern maps directly onto the causal pathways framework presented in Section~\ref{sec:harm-landscape}: narrative games (Path~B) develop empathy, document games (Path~D) develop source analysis, and competitive quiz games (Path~C) develop neither.

The 18.9\% rate is not merely a gap but a structural consequence of the theoretical framework deficit identified in Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}: without discipline-specific theory, researchers default to generic outcome measures. The 7 studies citing historical thinking frameworks (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-base}) largely overlap with the 14 assessing historical thinking outcomes --- theory drives measurement. This circularity has practical implications: expanding the assessment of historical thinking requires not just better instruments but deeper integration of historical epistemology into gamification research designs. The experimental methodology in Chapter~3 addresses this gap directly, targeting historical thinking competences through Viber-based gamification aligned with \cite{Wineburg2001} and \cite{Seixas2013}.

\subsubsection{Affective outcomes and attitudes toward history}

Studies examining affective outcomes such as attitude toward history, enjoyment, and self-efficacy generally reported positive results ($n = 31$ of 37 reporting on these outcomes, 83.8\%). The strongest positive effects were observed in studies using narrative-rich game environments, where historical content was embedded in engaging storylines \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 74Fendt2019, 29Stiso20201165}. Narrative creates emotional connection to historical actors and events --- a pathway distinct from behavioural engagement and cognitive flow. This affective-epistemic pathway is unique to history education: emotional resonance with historical experience can motivate sustained inquiry, but it also carries the risk of uncritical identification (examined in Section~\ref{sec:harm-landscape}).

One large-scale study ($N = 2{,}417$) identified five distinct engagement profiles through latent class analysis, finding that modifying game dialogue scripts shifted some students toward more positive evaluations of history learning \cite{15Slater2022}. This finding suggests stable individual differences in response to gamification: not all students respond positively, with approximately 15--20\% showing neutral or negative attitudinal shifts. Other studies documented transformations in students' relationships with history, with some moving from viewing it as ``boring'' to developing genuine interest through game-based exploration \cite{55Squire2004, 80gottlieb2018your}.

Structural equation modelling in \citeauthor{45Yu2016727}~\cite{45Yu2016727} confirmed that perceived usefulness had the strongest influence on game preference, and that more sophisticated epistemological beliefs positively affected attitudes toward game-based history learning. This latter finding has an important equity implication: students who already think critically about knowledge benefit more from gamification than those with naive epistemological beliefs. Gamification may therefore produce a Matthew effect --- widening the gap between epistemologically sophisticated and naive learners rather than closing it.

\subsubsection{Harm landscape and the four causal pathways} \label{sec:harm-landscape}

The synthesis of effectiveness findings reveals that gamification effects in history education are mediated by four distinct causal pathways, each with characteristic positive outcomes and associated risks (Figure~\ref{fig:causal-pathways}).

\input{figures/causal_pathways}

\textit{Path~A (Flow):} Game complexity generates flow states, which sustain attention and enable deeper cognitive processing. When complexity exceeds learner capacity, the pathway degrades to cognitive overload and no learning. Six studies \cite{16Chen201447, 63Liu2021, 64Hsu201324, 70Hou201519, 48Moketar2021, 45Yu2016727} document this pathway through flow measurement.

\textit{Path~B (Narrative):} Story and role-play elements produce narrative engagement, creating emotional connections that foster historical empathy. When immersion becomes uncritical, students confuse game fiction with historical fact \cite{71lynch2015pedagogical, 61Karsenti202027, 12Gilbert2019108}. This pathway is characteristic of studies using Assassin's Creed and similar commercial narrative games.

\textit{Path~C (Competition):} Points, leaderboards, and timed challenges generate extrinsic motivation, increasing task completion but typically producing only surface-level learning. Competition pressure can create stress, anxiety, and gender-differentiated effects \cite{34Serrano2023, 49Huizenga2007127, 8Yu2014}. Path~C explains why some gamification produces engagement without learning --- the most common ``mixed'' result pattern.

\textit{Path~D (Source interaction):} Document-based and archive-based games require critical analysis of historical evidence, developing source evaluation and historical thinking skills. When information density is too high, cognitive overload occurs \cite{52Lin2011265, 54Gibson2007, 41Petersen2023301}. This pathway produces the deepest learning but is the least common in the corpus (18.9\% of studies).

The four pathways framework synthesises the evidence from Sections 1.2.5.1--1.2.5.7 and explains the engagement-learning decoupling: Paths~A, B, and D can produce deep learning outcomes when properly scaffolded, but Path~C --- the most commonly implemented through points and leaderboard mechanics (see Section~\ref{sec:game-elements}) --- generates engagement without learning transfer.

The pathway framework also organises the negative effects reported across the corpus. Of the 74 studies, 60 (81.1\%) reported at least one negative effect \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Figure~\ref{fig:harm-landscape} systematises these into four categories:

\input{figures/harm_landscape}

\textit{Cognitive harms} (13 studies): cognitive overload from information density \cite{52Lin2011265, 54Gibson2007, 41Petersen2023301, 21Ghulamani2017}, distraction from learning goals \cite{60Hoy2018, 67Lin201296, 14Forsyth2023}, shallow learning replacing deep understanding \cite{56Akkerman2009449, 29Stiso20201165, 47Clara2010156, 74Fendt2019}, and development of historical misconceptions \cite{71lynch2015pedagogical, 61Karsenti202027, 12Gilbert2019108, 40Kuran2018, 14Forsyth2023}.

\textit{Affective harms} (8 studies): stress and anxiety from time-pressured activities \cite{34Serrano2023, 38Mercik2024}, emotional distress when games confront students with difficult historical content such as racial privilege \cite{78Rouse2022357, 36Scholz2021}, and frustration with game complexity \cite{55Squire2004, 3Watson2011466, 22Nugroho2020149}.

\textit{Equity harms} (6 studies): gender disparities in motivation and engagement \cite{8Yu2014, 19Chou2019, 61Karsenti202027}, reading level effects disadvantaging weaker readers \cite{15Slater2022, 49Huizenga2007127}, and game addiction risk \cite{52Lin2011265, 67Lin201296}.

\textit{Epistemic harms} (5 studies): reinforcement of colonialist and hegemonic ideologies through game narratives \cite{46Stirling2022, 55Squire2004}, historical inaccuracies in game content \cite{61Karsenti202027, 12Gilbert2019108}, and violent content producing psychological effects \cite{65Razak201139, 20Erandaru2014, 4Cruz2017}.

The epistemic harms are unique to history education and have no parallel in gamification research in other disciplines \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Connolly2012}. Historical misconceptions, ideological bias, and fact-fiction confusion arise specifically because history games make claims about real events and real people, unlike gamification in mathematics or language learning. This observation elevates the role of teacher mediation and critical pedagogical scaffolding from a desirable addition to a necessary condition for responsible gamification in history education.

The synthesis of pathways and harms yields a design principle: effectiveness depends on \textit{alignment} between game mechanics, intended learning outcomes, and assessment methods. Path~C (competition) paired with historical thinking goals represents poor alignment --- it produces engagement without the analytical engagement necessary for disciplinary learning. Path~D (source interaction) paired with source analysis assessment represents strong alignment --- game mechanics directly scaffold the target competence. This pedagogical alignment principle directly informs the experimental methodology in Chapter~3, where Viber-based gamification elements are designed to activate Paths~B and D while mitigating the risks associated with Path~C.

While this section has addressed outcome-level effects and the causal mechanisms producing them, implementation-level challenges --- technical infrastructure, resource constraints, curriculum integration, and teacher preparation --- are examined in Section~\ref{sec:implementation-challenges}.

\subsection{Implementation challenges} \label{sec:implementation-challenges}

The review identified five categories of implementation barriers, whose frequency distribution across the 74-study corpus is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-frequency}: student-related challenges (44 studies, 59.5\%), pedagogical and curriculum integration difficulties (38 studies, 51.4\%), technical and infrastructure constraints (30 studies, 40.5\%), resource limitations (13 studies, 17.6\%), and time constraints (8 studies, 10.8\%). Two categories exceed the 50\% threshold, indicating majority barriers; the remaining three, while less frequently reported, are no less consequential when present.

\input{figures/barrier_frequency}

To move beyond a descriptive inventory, this section organises the evidence through Ertmer's \citeauthor{Ertmer1999}~\cite{Ertmer1999} taxonomy of first-order and second-order barriers. First-order barriers are external to teachers: technology, resources, time, and institutional support. Second-order barriers are internal: teacher beliefs, pedagogical knowledge, and willingness to change established practice. The corpus overwhelmingly documents first-order barriers (technology 40.5\%, resources 17.6\%, time 10.8\%) while under-reporting second-order barriers (teacher beliefs, institutional culture). Yet second-order barriers determine whether first-order resources are used effectively --- a pattern that shapes the analysis below \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsubsection{First-order barriers: technical infrastructure and the digital divide}

The 30 studies reporting technical barriers cluster into three tiers of infrastructure demand. At the \textit{hardware tier}, AR and VR implementations face recognition failures, GPS inaccuracies, and device costs that restrict deployment to well-equipped schools \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 49Huizenga2007127, 77Ramansyah2021, 6Czerwinski2018, 73Wolfartsberger2011160}. At the \textit{software tier}, custom development platforms (Unity 3D, Unreal Engine) impose steep learning curves on research teams, while browser compatibility and operating system fragmentation complicate web-based delivery \cite{63Liu2021, 14Forsyth2023}. At the \textit{connectivity tier}, limited internet access in rural and remote areas constrains real-time features, cloud-based content, and multiplayer interactions \cite{77Ramansyah2021, 22Nugroho2020149}.

A \textit{technology-access gradient} emerges from this evidence: the correlation between game sophistication and required infrastructure creates differential access. VR/AR implementations (25.7\% of the corpus) demand the highest infrastructure tier; web-based and mobile formats (13.5\%) demand the lowest \cite{58Shih2015173, 48Moketar2021}. This gradient interacts with a \textit{geographic blind spot}: the evidence base is produced predominantly in well-resourced contexts (Taiwan, USA, Malaysia, and Indonesia account for 55.4\% of all studies), creating systematic under-representation of barriers in under-resourced settings where technical constraints are most severe.

The present study's wartime Ukrainian context (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}) represents an extreme case of infrastructure instability, where power outages and internet disruptions caused 33.3\% of documented absences \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} --- conditions absent from the existing literature.

\subsubsection{Resource constraints and the scalability paradox}

The 13 studies reporting resource constraints and 8 reporting time constraints collectively define the \textit{resource envelope} within which gamification must operate. A fundamental \textit{build versus buy} trade-off structures this evidence: custom development requires substantial investment in design, programming, and content creation \cite{50Lye2013, 20Erandaru2014, 9Lin2018, Gasca-Hurtado2016} but grants full pedagogical control, while commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) titles (used in 10.8\% of studies, see Section~\ref{sec:game-elements}) reduce development costs at the expense of curricular alignment \cite{55Squire2004, 12Gilbert2019108, Castronovo2015}.

This trade-off generates what we term the \textit{scalability paradox}: the most effective gamification implementations are custom-designed, multi-modal, long-duration, and scaffolded --- and therefore the most resource-intensive, the hardest to scale, and the least likely to be studied with adequate sample sizes. Resource intensity thus produces better pedagogy \textit{and} worse research simultaneously. The paradox partially explains both the field's positive findings (resource-intensive implementations work) and its methodological weakness (they cannot afford large samples or long follow-ups). Yu \citeauthor{8Yu2014}~\cite{8Yu2014} documented that game-based instruction significantly increases instructor workload, a finding that reinforces the resource constraint but is rarely discussed as a sustainability concern.

The present study's approach --- a hub-and-spoke static HTML platform with a shared infrastructure layer of $\approx$88~KB powering 62 modules across 9 historical periods --- represents an attempt to resolve this paradox through lightweight design that minimises both development and deployment costs (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}).

\subsubsection{Second-order barriers: teacher preparation and TPACK deficits}

Effective gamification in history education requires not merely technical competence but the intersection of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge that Mishra and Koehler \cite{Mishra2006} formalised as the TPACK framework. A history teacher implementing gamification must simultaneously understand how game mechanics work (TK), how students learn historical thinking (PK), what constitutes valid historical knowledge (CK), and --- critically --- how game mechanics can scaffold specific historical thinking competences (TPACK) \cite{Koehler2013}.

Only 4 of 74 studies specifically addressed teacher training needs, while 38 (51.4\%) reported curriculum integration difficulties --- a disparity suggesting that the teacher is the under-studied bottleneck. When teachers lack TPACK, gamification degrades from a pedagogical strategy to a technological novelty: the game elements are present, but the connection between mechanics and learning outcomes is severed. Mercader's \cite{Mercader2020} barrier model illuminates this dynamic: institutional, personal, and contextual barriers interact to produce inertia, where even motivated teachers cannot implement gamification without institutional support for training and resource allocation.

Studies where instructors received substantial training or co-designed the gamification elements reported better outcomes than those where technology was introduced without adequate pedagogical support \cite{61Karsenti202027, 36Scholz2021, Buisman2014}. Conversely, Hoy \cite{60Hoy2018} and Cruz et~al. \citeauthor{4Cruz2017}~\cite{4Cruz2017} documented implementation failures attributable to insufficient instructor preparation. This evidence converges with the DigComp 2.0 framework (Section~\ref{sec:digcomp}), which positions educator digital competence as a prerequisite for meaningful technology integration \cite{UNESCO2023, Qian2016}.

\subsubsection{Curriculum integration and institutional inertia}

The 38 studies reporting pedagogical and curriculum barriers reveal a structural tension between gamification's affordances and institutional constraints. Most implementations adopted a \textit{bolt-on} approach --- adding game elements to existing curricula --- rather than a \textit{built-in} approach that redesigns instruction around gamification principles. The 51.4\% barrier rate reflects the friction generated by this bolt-on strategy: game activities compete for time with established content, disrupt familiar assessment rhythms, and require scheduling flexibility that rigid timetables rarely permit \cite{61Karsenti202027, 57Huntley2011567, 49Huizenga2007127, 46Stirling2022}.

An \textit{assessment mismatch} compounds this friction. Gamification develops competences --- collaboration, empathy, systemic thinking, perspective-taking --- that traditional assessment instruments (multiple-choice questions, factual recall tests) cannot measure \cite{3Watson2011466, 4Cruz2017, 20Erandaru2014}. This creates a validity threat that discourages adoption: if gamification's benefits are invisible to the assessment system, administrators and teachers perceive no return on the invested effort.

Time operates as a \textit{structural constraint}: gamified activities require more class time than lecture-based instruction, but curricula are calibrated for lecture-based time budgets. Many studies resolved this by positioning gamification as extracurricular or voluntary \cite{60Hoy2018, 34Serrano2023, 42Huffer201581} --- a strategy that eliminates scheduling barriers but introduces self-selection bias. The present study's NMT preparatory course operates outside the formal university curriculum (CDPO continuing education), removing some institutional barriers (prescribed syllabi, standardised assessment) while introducing others (voluntary participation, rolling enrolment, variable attendance).

\subsubsection{Student heterogeneity and the differential response problem}

The largest barrier category --- 44 studies, 59.5\% --- documents the challenge of student heterogeneity. This barrier is multi-dimensional, operating along at least three axes simultaneously.

\textit{(a) Prior gaming experience and digital literacy variation.} Students arrive with vastly different levels of familiarity with game mechanics, from daily gamers to those who have never used a game controller \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 6Czerwinski2018, 77Ramansyah2021}. This variation means that a single gamification design may simultaneously bore experienced gamers and overwhelm novices.

\textit{(b) Motivational heterogeneity.} Approximately 10\% of students respond negatively to gamification, perceiving it as trivialising serious historical content or as an unwelcome departure from familiar instructional formats \cite{47Clara2010156}. Ternblad et~al. \citeauthor{11Ternblad2023115}~\cite{11Ternblad2023115} found that achievement level moderates feedback processing in gamified environments, while 15--20\% of participants across the corpus showed neutral or negative attitudinal shifts \cite{3Watson2011466, 42Huffer201581}.

\textit{(c) Demographic moderators.} Gender effects were statistically significant in multiple studies (e.g., $F(1,64) = 4.408$, $p = .040$ for motivation; \citealt{8Yu2014}), with boys generally reporting higher motivation in competitive formats. Reading level affected comprehension of game narratives \cite{15Slater2022}, and Rahmawati et~al. \citeauthor{66Rahmawati2023163}~\cite{66Rahmawati2023163} documented cultural background as a moderating variable.

The analytical contribution here is that heterogeneity is not a single variable but a multi-dimensional space. Different students activate different causal pathways (Section~\ref{sec:harm-landscape}): competition-oriented students engage Path~C while narrative-oriented students engage Path~B, producing divergent outcomes from identical game designs. \subsubsection{Scalability, sustainability, and the long-term evidence gap}

Seven studies noted the absence of long-term assessment; most implementations were single-site pilots with no replication, follow-up, or cost-benefit analysis \cite{47Clara2010156, 2Mahamarowi2023133, 5kazanidis2018alpha}. This gap is not merely an omission --- it conceals a critical question about \textit{implementation decay}. Effective gamification may degrade as novelty wears off (47\% of studies in Clara's \cite{47Clara2010156} meta-review expressed concern about novelty effects), infrastructure ages, or instructor expertise leaves the institution.

The field's maturity profile (Section~\ref{sec:evidence-maturity}) concentrates evidence at the proof-of-concept stage. No research has examined the scaling of a successful pilot to multiple classrooms, the sustaining of gamification across multiple academic years, or the institutionalising of gamification as standard practice \cite{KoivistoHamari2019, DichevDicheva2017}. Cheng et~al. \cite{32Cheng20241718} and Kuran et~al. \citeauthor{40Kuran2018}~\cite{40Kuran2018} identified this as a critical gap, but neither proposed methodological solutions.

The present study's seven-month duration (October 2024 -- May 2025) represents one of the longest documented implementations in this corpus, providing preliminary evidence on engagement sustainability across 23 gamification modules delivered over 38 sessions.

\subsubsection{Synthesis: the barrier interaction model}

The preceding subsubsections demonstrate that implementation barriers are not independent obstacles to be addressed in isolation. Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-interaction-model} presents a systems-level model of barrier interactions.

\input{figures/barrier_interaction_model}

Technical constraints increase resource costs through workaround requirements; resource constraints eliminate teacher training budgets; untrained teachers lack the TPACK to adapt curricula; rigid curricula cannot accommodate diverse student profiles; and student resistance to poorly implemented gamification generates additional technical support demands, completing a reinforcing cycle. Each arrow in Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-interaction-model} represents an evidence-based interaction documented across the 74-study corpus \cite{Ertmer1999, Mercader2020}.

This systems-level view identifies an \textit{implementation threshold} --- a minimum level of resources, training, and institutional support below which gamification cannot succeed regardless of design quality. Below this threshold, barrier interactions amplify each other; above it, each barrier addressed reduces the load on others. The threshold concept reframes implementation from a checklist (technology: done; training: done; curriculum: done) to a systems problem requiring simultaneous, coordinated investment.

Finally, this barrier analysis provides a partial explanation for the field's methodological weakness documented in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}. The 83.8\% high-risk-of-bias rate is not purely a matter of methodological ignorance --- it is partly a consequence of implementation barriers. Resource-constrained researchers produce small, uncontrolled, short-duration studies because that is all their implementation conditions permit. Improving research quality therefore requires addressing implementation conditions, not merely issuing methodological guidelines \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, UNESCO2023}.

The experimental methodology in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} addresses specific barriers identified in this section: lightweight technology (static HTML, Viber integration) mitigates first-order technical barriers; instructor-designed content resolves the build-versus-buy trade-off; and voluntary participation within a non-formal course context removes some institutional constraints. The wartime Ukrainian context of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} transforms implementation barriers from stable background conditions into dynamic, fluctuating variables --- an empirical situation without precedent in the existing literature.

To move beyond cataloguing individual elements, this taxonomy can be mapped onto \citeauthor{Werbach2012}'s \cite{Werbach2012} three-level DMC framework, which organises game design into a hierarchy: \textit{Dynamics} (big-picture patterns that shape the overall experience --- narrative arcs, relationships, progression trajectories), \textit{Mechanics} (processes that drive player action --- challenges, feedback loops, reward schedules), and \textit{Components} (specific instantiations of mechanics and dynamics --- points, badges, leaderboards, avatars). The DMC hierarchy complements the MDA framework introduced in Section~\ref{sec:conceptual-approaches}: MDA describes the designer-to-player experiential flow (Mechanics $\to$ Dynamics $\to$ Aesthetics) \cite{Hunicke2004}, while DMC describes the structural hierarchy of game elements themselves (Dynamics $\to$ Mechanics $\to$ Components). Both frameworks illuminate different dimensions of the same design space \cite{Toda2019, Landers2014}.

Mapping the 12 element categories from Table~\ref{tab:game-elements} onto the DMC hierarchy yields a striking pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:dmc-pyramid}). \textit{Dynamics-level elements} --- narrative/storytelling (44 studies), role-playing (44), and exploration (29) --- are the most abstract and hardest to implement, yet they dominate the history education corpus with a cumulative presence of 117 study-element instances. \textit{Mechanics-level elements} --- collaboration/competition (36), levels/progression (35), quiz/assessment (19), puzzles/challenges (18), rules/goals (18), and time pressure (12) --- represent the operational processes, with 138 cumulative instances. \textit{Components-level elements} --- points/rewards/scoring (31) and feedback (17) --- the most concrete and readily implementable tier, account for only 48 instances. Immersion/VR/AR (55 studies) functions as a cross-cutting technological implementation that spans all three levels, providing the infrastructure through which dynamics, mechanics, and components are delivered \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\input{figures/dmc_pyramid_mapping}

The resulting profile is ``top-heavy'': history education concentrates at the Dynamics level, whereas general gamification literature concentrates at the Components level (points, badges, leaderboards) \cite{Deterding2011, SEABORN201514, DichevDicheva2017}. This structural inversion explains both the field's rich pedagogical potential --- dynamics-level elements naturally engage historical thinking through narrative, empathy, and perspective-taking --- and its high implementation barriers documented in Section~\ref{sec:implementation-challenges}, since abstract elements require more design expertise and institutional support than concrete components \cite{Werbach2012, Toda2019}.

\subsubsection{Domain-specificity: the inverted element profile}

The dynamics-dominant pattern becomes even more striking when compared quantitatively across educational domains. Figure~\ref{fig:domain-element-comparison} presents element prevalence across three domains: history education (74 studies, this review), software engineering education (29 studies \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}), and general gamification (meta-reviews: \cite{Hamari2014, SEABORN201514, DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019}).

\input{figures/domain_element_comparison}

The comparison reveals an \textit{inverted element profile} unique to history education. In history, experiential elements dominate: immersion 74.3\%, narrative 59.5\%, collaboration 48.6\%, with points/rewards at only 41.9\%. In software engineering education, the pattern reverses: points 58.6\% (17/29 studies), leaderboards 37.9\% (11/29), badges 31.0\% (9/29), with narrative elements rarely exceeding 15\% \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}. General gamification meta-reviews consistently identify points and rewards as the dominant elements, with prevalence rates approaching 80\% \cite{Hamari2014, SEABORN201514, Souza2018201}. Health professions education shows yet another pattern, with assessment-driven feedback loops dominant \cite{vanGaalen2021}.

This inversion can be theoretically explained through \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} Theory of Gamified Learning, which posits that game elements do not directly cause learning but rather \textit{moderate} the relationship between instructional content and learning outcomes. The moderating elements must engage the cognitive processes specific to each discipline. In history, these processes --- empathy, multiperspectivity, source criticism, temporal reasoning --- are inherently narrative and experiential \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}. Point-based mechanics cannot meaningfully moderate the development of historical empathy; narrative immersion and role-playing can. The inverted profile thus constitutes a \textit{disciplinary signature}: a characteristic element distribution that reflects the epistemological demands of historical thinking and should inform design choices \cite{Hong2024, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsubsection{Technological platforms and the access--complexity spectrum}

The 74 studies employed six categories of technological platforms, which can be analytically organised along an access--complexity spectrum (Figure~\ref{fig:platform-pedagogy-matrix}).

\textbf{Game engines} (Unity 3D, Unreal Engine) appeared in 14 studies, offering high-fidelity 3D environments but requiring specialist development skills \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 10Huaman2019220, 6Czerwinski2018, 32Cheng20241718, 17Manzanares2019}. \textbf{Web-based technologies} (HTML5, CSS3, JavaScript, PHP) powered browser-accessible games in 8 studies, providing moderate fidelity with low access barriers and inherent scalability \cite{48Moketar2021, 5kazanidis2018alpha, 39Tegos2014838}. \textbf{VR/AR systems} ranged from low-cost solutions (Google Cardboard) to high-fidelity hardware (HTC Vive) across approximately 19 studies, excelling at immersion but constrained by hardware requirements and cost \cite{73Wolfartsberger2011160, 31DeAmicis2009451}. \textbf{Commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) games} repurposed for education appeared in 10 studies: the Civilization series (6 studies) \cite{55Squire2004, 57Huntley2011567, 60Hoy2018, 80gottlieb2018your}, Assassin's Creed (2 studies) \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 78Rouse2022357}, and complex strategy games (Crusader Kings~II, Europa Universalis~IV, Hearts of Iron~IV) \cite{37Lewis202054}. COTS games eliminate development costs but sacrifice pedagogical control. \textbf{Non-digital platforms} --- board games, card games, and 3D-printed models --- appeared in approximately 12 studies \cite{46Stirling2022, 40Kuran2018, 13Humpire2022, 76Corbeil2011462, 38Mercik2024}, demonstrating that gamification does not require digital technology and uniquely enabling face-to-face collaboration and deliberation. \textbf{Communication platforms} (Zoom, Facebook, Moodle, Viber) supplemented gamification in multiple studies \cite{Gupta2022341, Bozic2024538}, serving as delivery channels rather than game engines.

\input{figures/platform_pedagogy_matrix}

Figure~\ref{fig:platform-pedagogy-matrix} maps these six platform categories against five pedagogical affordances: immersion, collaboration, assessment integration, scalability, and cost-effectiveness. The matrix reveals a fundamental trade-off: no single platform excels across all dimensions. Game engines and VR/AR achieve high immersion but score poorly on scalability and cost-effectiveness. Web-based platforms and communication tools offer scalability and low cost but sacrifice immersion depth. Non-digital platforms uniquely support face-to-face collaboration at minimal cost but cannot scale beyond the classroom. COTS games provide pre-built immersive worlds at no development cost but offer limited assessment integration and pedagogical control.

This systematic misalignment explains why no single platform type dominates the literature and why multi-platform designs may be necessary for comprehensive gamification \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. The present study positions itself deliberately at the low-barrier, high-scalability end of this spectrum (static HTML + Viber integration), intentionally trading immersion for accessibility --- a design choice directly informed by the barrier analysis in Section~\ref{sec:implementation-challenges}.

\subsubsection{Instructional design strategies and element alignment}

Collaborative learning was the most frequently employed instructional strategy (46 studies, 62.2\%) \cite{3Watson2011466, 49Huizenga2007127, 46Stirling2022}, followed by narrative-driven instruction (40 studies, 54.1\%) \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 74Fendt2019, 29Stiso20201165} and assessment-integrated approaches (31 studies, 41.9\%) \cite{19Chou2019, 24Ghannem2019, 34Serrano2023}. Scaffolded/guided approaches and problem/inquiry-based learning were each present in 23 studies (31.1\%), as was experiential/situated learning \cite{Bonwell1991, Constructivism}. Constructivist approaches, while theoretically prominent, were explicitly employed in only 15 studies (20.3\%) \cite{Constructivism}.

Analysis of strategy--element alignment reveals systematic co-occurrence patterns. Collaborative strategies align with collaboration/competition mechanics: 46 studies employed collaborative learning, and 36 employed collaboration/competition elements, with substantial overlap. Narrative-driven strategies align with narrative/storytelling elements (40 and 44 studies, respectively). Assessment-integrated strategies align with quiz/assessment and feedback elements (31, 19, and 17 studies). These alignments are theoretically expected --- strategies and elements that share the same pedagogical function naturally co-occur \cite{Hong2024, Toda2019}.

However, a notable gap emerges for constructivist approaches. Only 15 studies (20.3\%) explicitly adopted constructivist strategies, yet constructivist-enabling elements such as exploration (39.2\%) and decision-making scenarios were present in substantially more studies. This suggests that constructivism is \textit{under-operationalised}: the game elements that could support constructivist learning are present, but they are not being deployed within explicitly constructivist instructional frameworks \cite{Constructivism}. Content integration approaches spanned eight categories: immersive historical reconstruction \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 51Ardito2007, 31DeAmicis2009451}, narrative-driven learning \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 74Fendt2019}, problem-based learning \cite{59Kee2014270, 42Huffer201581}, simulation-based learning \cite{55Squire2004, 76Corbeil2011462}, role-playing and perspective-taking \cite{18Shih201054, 56Akkerman2009449}, artifact-based learning \cite{51Ardito2007, 62Milosz201896}, location-based learning \cite{58Shih2015173, 25Repantis2011502}, and assessment-integrated learning \cite{24Ghannem2019, 19Chou2019, 43ali2018indonesian}.

\subsubsection{Synthesis: from elements to learning outcomes}

The DMC mapping (Section 1.2.7.1), domain comparison (Section 1.2.7.2), platform analysis (Section 1.2.7.3), strategy alignment (Section 1.2.7.4), and archetype typology (Section 1.2.7.5) jointly demonstrate that game design in history education is not a matter of selecting individual elements from a menu, but of assembling coherent configurations that align with disciplinary epistemology. The field's dynamics-dominant profile is both its strength --- pedagogically rich, naturally engaging historical thinking --- and its vulnerability --- hard to implement, hard to study rigorously, hard to scale beyond individual classrooms.

\citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} theory provides a unifying interpretation: game elements are not interventions in themselves but \textit{moderators} of the relationship between instructional quality and learning outcomes. Their effect depends on the instructional quality they moderate. A poorly designed history lesson with points remains a poor lesson; a well-designed source analysis activity with narrative scaffolding can produce genuine historical thinking \cite{Werbach2012, Toda2019, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. This moderating function explains why the inverted element profile matters: narrative and immersive elements moderate \textit{the right cognitive processes} for history, whereas points moderate effort and persistence --- useful but insufficient for developing disciplinary understanding \cite{Korniienko2025aredu}.

The design principles that follow (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}) synthesise these findings into actionable guidelines. The experimental methodology in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} deliberately selects the Competitive Quizzer archetype (quiz-based, point-driven, Viber-delivered) as a test of whether lightweight gamification can produce meaningful effects in resource-constrained conditions --- trading the pedagogical richness of dynamics-level elements for the accessibility and scalability of components-level mechanics.

\subsection{Design principles} \label{sec:design-principles}

The systematic review corpus \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} yields a rich inventory of implementation features associated with positive outcomes. This section synthesises that inventory into a principled design framework: first establishing how the principles were selected from a larger candidate pool (Section~\ref{sec:principle-selection}), then grounding each principle in the theoretical frameworks introduced in Section~\ref{sec:theory} (Section~\ref{sec:principle-grounding}), analysing inter-principle interactions and their alignment with the design archetypes from Section~\ref{sec:archetypes} (Section~\ref{sec:principle-interactions}), and finally drawing implications for the experimental design of the present study (Section~\ref{sec:principle-implications}).

\subsubsection{From evidence to principles: selection rationale} \label{sec:principle-selection}

The systematic review identified 12 candidate design principles from studies reporting positive outcomes: (1)~historical authenticity, (2)~narrative integration, (3)~progressive difficulty, (4)~immediate feedback, (5)~social elements, (6)~multiple modalities, (7)~interactivity, (8)~agency/choice, (9)~clear objectives, (10)~meaningful rewards, (11)~accessibility, and (12)~replayability \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, LaineLindberg2020}. While all 12 appear in at least one effective implementation, their frequency and generalisability vary considerably.

Three criteria guided the distillation from 12 to 6 retained principles. First, \textit{positive-outcome frequency}: the principle must be implemented in more than 40\% of studies reporting positive effects on engagement or learning. Second, \textit{cross-archetype applicability}: the principle must appear in at least three of the five design archetypes identified in Section~\ref{sec:archetypes}. Third, \textit{operationalisability}: the principle must be implementable in resource-constrained settings without specialist development tools \cite{Hong2024, Toda2019}.

The six excluded principles fail one or more criteria. Interactivity is subsumed by multiple modalities and social elements; agency/choice is subsumed by narrative integration (where quests provide structured autonomy); clear objectives constitute an implementation condition rather than a design principle per se \cite{LaineLindberg2020}. Meaningful rewards are subsumed by historical authenticity --- in history education, ``meaningful'' necessarily means ``historically authentic'' \cite{Nicholson2015}. Accessibility is an implementation condition addressed in Section~\ref{sec:guidelines}. Replayability appears in only 8.1\% of studies, rendering it too infrequent to qualify as a general principle \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

This selection rationale constitutes an original analytical contribution: existing reviews present design principles without explaining why certain principles are prioritised over others or how they relate to specific disciplinary contexts.

\subsubsection{The six principles with theoretical grounding} \label{sec:principle-grounding}

Each retained principle is presented below with its empirical evidence, theoretical mechanism(s) drawn from the frameworks in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, history-specific manifestation, and violation evidence. Figure~\ref{fig:principle-theoretical-grounding} provides a matrix view of the theoretical grounding.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Historical authenticity.} The most effective implementations maintained historical accuracy while incorporating game elements \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 1Barbatsis201159, 51Ardito2007}. Superficial gamification that trivialised historical content was associated with negative outcomes in 10.8\% of studies \cite{47Clara2010156, Oceja2022419}. Authenticity encompasses factual accuracy, appropriate representation of historical complexity, ambiguity, and multiple perspectives \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}. Studies using historically accurate game environments (archaeological reconstructions, period-appropriate visual design) reported stronger learning outcomes than those using generic game formats with historical content superimposed \cite{1Barbatsis201159, 31DeAmicis2009451, 62Milosz201896}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Historical authenticity is the only principle that lacks grounding in any general motivational or design theory (Figure~\ref{fig:principle-theoretical-grounding}, top row). It derives instead from the epistemology of the discipline itself: historical thinking requires engagement with authentic sources and contexts \cite{Wineburg2001, MonteSano2012}. Within \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} framework, authenticity operates as a content validity condition --- it ensures that game elements moderate historically valid learning rather than trivial recall. This disciplinary specificity distinguishes gamification in history education from domain-general approaches.

\textit{Violation evidence.} When authenticity is absent, gamification risks producing what \cite{Clark2016} terms ``chocolate-covered broccoli'' --- superficially engaging activities that fail to develop historical understanding. The 10.8\% negative-outcome rate in the corpus is concentrated in implementations lacking historical authenticity \cite{47Clara2010156, Oceja2022419}.

\item \textbf{Narrative integration.} Using historical narratives as the framework for gamified activities enhanced both engagement and learning \cite{12Gilbert2019108, 74Fendt2019, 29Stiso20201165}. Quest-based structures aligned naturally with historical investigation, casting students as detectives uncovering evidence \cite{59Kee2014270, 42Huffer201581}. Narrative-rich environments showed the strongest effects on affective outcomes ($d = 0.61$ for attitude toward history) \cite{15Slater2022, 55Squire2004, SailerHomner2020}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Narrative integration serves SDT's autonomy need: quest structures provide meaningful choice within historically constrained parameters \cite{RyanDeci2000, vanRoyZaman2019}. Within \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory, narrative is the primary mechanism through which history education's dynamics-dominant profile (Section~\ref{sec:dmc-mapping}) achieves its effect --- narrative moderates the relationship between instructional content and affective outcomes by providing emotional and cognitive engagement with historical situations \cite{Nicholson2015}. This makes narrative integration history's primary modulator in the Landers framework.

\item \textbf{Progressive difficulty.} Scaffolded challenges that increased in complexity over time supported sustained engagement and skill development \cite{19Chou2019, 9Lin2018, 64Hsu201324}. Effective scaffolding moved from recognition tasks (matching, multiple choice) to analysis tasks (source evaluation, perspective-taking) to synthesis tasks (constructing historical narratives, arguing causation), mirroring established taxonomies of historical thinking skills \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012, Calandra2005323}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Progressive difficulty lies at the intersection of Flow theory and scaffolded learning. \citeauthor{Csikszentmihalyi1990}'s \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990} challenge--skill balance requires that difficulty increase as competence grows; simultaneously, the principle enacts Vygotskian scaffolding within the zone of proximal development \cite{Keller2010}. It also serves SDT's competence need by ensuring that mastery experiences accumulate progressively \cite{RyanDeci2000, Sailer2017}. In behaviourist terms, progressive difficulty implements shaping --- successive approximation toward complex historical reasoning.

\item \textbf{Immediate feedback.} Providing instant feedback on quiz responses and game actions facilitated self-regulated learning \cite{11Ternblad2023115, 15Slater2022, 24Ghannem2019}. Immediate feedback enabled iterative learning cycles where students corrected misconceptions in real time \cite{19Chou2019, 34Serrano2023}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Immediate feedback is the most theoretically overdetermined principle, serving multiple frameworks simultaneously. In behaviourist terms, it provides positive reinforcement that shapes response patterns. In Flow theory, it maintains flow state by keeping learners informed of their performance relative to the challenge \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990}. It signals competence within SDT \cite{Sailer2017}. However, its history-specific application reveals a tension: feedback is highly effective for factual recall but less clearly effective for complex interpretive questions where multiple valid answers exist \cite{Clark2016}. This asymmetry has implications for implementation design (Section~\ref{sec:principle-implications}).

\item \textbf{Social elements.} Collaborative and competitive elements (team challenges, leaderboards, peer review) enhanced motivation, but their effects were moderated by implementation design \cite{3Watson2011466, 49Huizenga2007127, Sailer2017}. Competition needed careful balancing to avoid discouraging lower-performing students \cite{11Ternblad2023115, 47Clara2010156}. Collaborative structures showed more consistently positive effects than purely competitive elements \cite{46Stirling2022, 39Tegos2014838}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Social elements map directly onto SDT's relatedness need \cite{RyanDeci2000, vanRoyZaman2019}. \citeauthor{Sailer2017}~\cite{Sailer2017} demonstrated experimentally that social game features (leaderboards, teams) increased relatedness need satisfaction. In history education specifically, collaborative deliberation mimics the social practices of historical discourse --- historians construct interpretations through scholarly debate, peer review, and evidential argumentation \cite{Wineburg2001}. Behaviourist social reinforcement (peer approval, competitive standing) provides a secondary mechanism \cite{BaiHewHuang2020}.

\item \textbf{Multiple modalities.} Combining text, images, maps, interactive elements, audio, and video addressed different learning preferences and supported deeper understanding \cite{10Huaman2019220, 62Milosz201896, 27Sumi2022}. Primary source images, historical maps, and audiovisual materials within gamified environments enhanced engagement across diverse learner profiles \cite{59Kee2014270, 74Fendt2019}.

\textit{Theoretical mechanism.} Multiple modalities draw on cognitive load theory (dual coding reduces extraneous load) and Universal Design for Learning (multiple means of representation) \cite{Keller2010, LaineLindberg2020}. Within \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} framework, multimodal design improves the instructional quality that game elements moderate. History is uniquely suited to multimodal gamification because primary sources are inherently multimodal --- texts, images, maps, artefacts, oral histories --- making multiple modalities a natural fit rather than an artificial addition \cite{Reisman2012, Hunicke2004}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Principle interactions and archetype alignment} \label{sec:principle-interactions}

The six principles are not independent --- they form complementary pairs and exhibit tensions that shape implementation decisions.

\textit{Synergies.} Three principle pairs produce mutually reinforcing effects. Narrative integration combined with historical authenticity creates \textit{authentic narrative} --- the strongest combination in the corpus, associated with the largest reported effect size ($d = 0.61$) on affective outcomes \cite{SailerHomner2020, 15Slater2022}. Progressive difficulty combined with immediate feedback produces an \textit{adaptive assessment loop} where scaffolded challenges and real-time correction maintain learners in the flow channel \cite{Csikszentmihalyi1990}. Social elements combined with multiple modalities create \textit{rich collaborative environments} where diverse resources support group investigation \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 46Stirling2022}.

\textit{Tensions.} Two tensions constrain implementation. First, immediate feedback can conflict with historical authenticity: instant correct/incorrect judgements are appropriate for factual recall but oversimplify interpretive questions where historical evidence supports multiple valid conclusions \cite{Clark2016, Wineburg2001}. Second, competitive social elements (leaderboards, rankings) can undermine progressive difficulty by discouraging weaker students from attempting scaffolded challenges, particularly in heterogeneous groups \cite{11Ternblad2023115, vanRoyZaman2019}. Managing these tensions requires design decisions that the archetype framework helps to structure.

\textit{Archetype alignment.} Figure~\ref{fig:principle-archetype-mapping} maps the five design archetypes from Section~\ref{sec:archetypes} onto the six principles, revealing systematic patterns. The Immersive Explorer and Narrative Investigator archetypes emphasise the history-specific principles (authenticity, narrative) but under-implement scalable mechanics (feedback, progression). The Competitive Quizzer inverts this pattern: strong on scalable, components-level principles but weak on the dynamics-level principles that define history education's disciplinary signature. The Collaborative Builder privileges social elements over all other principles. Only the Hybrid Integrator addresses all six principles, explaining both its superior effectiveness in the literature and its resource intensity \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025aredu, Sailer2017}.

\input{figures/principle_archetype_mapping}

This alignment reveals a fundamental trade-off in gamification design for history education: the principles most strongly associated with disciplinary learning outcomes (authenticity, narrative) are the most resource-intensive to implement, while the most scalable principles (feedback, progression, social elements) are the least discipline-specific. No archetype except the Hybrid Integrator resolves this tension, and the Hybrid Integrator's resource requirements place it beyond reach for most educational contexts \cite{Werbach2012, Landers2014}.

\subsubsection{Implications for experimental design} \label{sec:principle-implications}

The six principles collectively define the design space for gamification in history education; the archetype alignment reveals which principles are feasible under given constraints. The present study (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) deliberately selects the Competitive Quizzer archetype --- quiz-based, point-driven, delivered via Viber --- as a test of whether lightweight gamification can produce meaningful learning effects in resource-constrained conditions.

This selection implements four of the six principles fully: progressive difficulty (scaffolded question sequences across 23 game modules), immediate feedback (automated scoring with instant results), social elements (leaderboards and competitive standings), and multiple modalities (text, images, historical maps within Viber). Two principles are implemented partially: historical authenticity is maintained through NMT-aligned content validated against curriculum standards, and narrative integration is limited to chronological course structure rather than quest-based frameworks.

This is a \textit{deliberate trade-off}: the Competitive Quizzer sacrifices dynamics-level richness for components-level scalability (connecting back to the DMC analysis in Section~\ref{sec:dmc-mapping}). The experimental design thus tests a theoretically grounded boundary condition: can lightweight gamification (components-level, four full and two partial principles) produce meaningful effects on engagement and learning outcomes? \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Werbach2012}

\citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory generates a specific prediction: if game elements moderate rather than replace instructional quality, then high-quality instruction combined with lightweight gamification should outperform poor instruction with rich gamification. The present study's context --- a structured NMT preparatory course with experienced instruction --- provides favourable conditions for testing this prediction. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} operationalises these design choices, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} assesses each principle's implementation and its relationship to observed outcomes.

%\subsection{Conclusions to Section 1.2}

The systematic review of 74 studies on gamification in history education \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, drawn from an initial pool of 4,011 records across three databases, reveals a rapidly growing but methodologically immature field. Key findings include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Definitional ambiguity}: 85.1\% of studies lack explicit definitions of gamification, undermining construct validity and cross-study comparison;
\item \textbf{Positive engagement effects}: 70.3\% of studies report positive effects on engagement, but with limited evidence on long-term sustainability;
\item \textbf{Mixed learning outcomes}: 51.4\% report positive effects on learning, but effect sizes are small to medium (d = 0.28--0.61) and concentrated on factual recall rather than deep understanding;
\item \textbf{Design diversity}: VR/AR (25.7\%), board games (14.9\%), custom digital (13.5\%), and web-based (13.5\%) formats dominate, with digital approaches accounting for 84\% of the corpus;
\item \textbf{Geographic concentration}: Four countries (Taiwan, USA, Malaysia, Indonesia) account for 55.4\% of all studies;
\item \textbf{Methodological weaknesses}: Only 31.1\% include control groups, median sample size is 33.5, and only 1 study is an RCT;
\item \textbf{Implementation barriers}: Five categories of barriers --- student heterogeneity (59.5\%), curriculum integration (51.4\%), technical infrastructure (40.5\%), resource constraints (17.6\%), and time limitations (10.8\%) --- interact in reinforcing cycles (Figure~\ref{fig:barrier-interaction-model}). When analysed through Ertmer's \cite{Ertmer1999} first-order/second-order taxonomy, the corpus reveals systematic under-reporting of second-order barriers (teacher beliefs, TPACK deficits) and a scalability paradox whereby the most effective implementations are the most resource-intensive and thus the hardest to study rigorously;
\item \textbf{Design principles}: Six evidence-based principles --- historical authenticity, narrative integration, progressive difficulty, immediate feedback, social elements, and multiple modalities --- were distilled from 12 candidates using positive-outcome frequency, cross-archetype applicability, and operationalisability criteria. Grounded in SDT, Flow theory, and \citeauthor{Landers2014}'s~\cite{Landers2014} moderation theory (Figure~\ref{fig:principle-theoretical-grounding}), the principles reveal that historical authenticity is uniquely disciplinary (lacking general-theory support), while narrative integration serves as history's primary modulator of affective outcomes;
\item \textbf{Element--principle--outcome chain}: The DMC pyramid (Section~\ref{sec:dmc-mapping}), inverted element profile, design archetypes, principle--archetype alignment (Figure~\ref{fig:principle-archetype-mapping}), and principle framework jointly demonstrate that gamification in history education is not a toolkit of interchangeable elements but a structured design space where element choices, principle adherence, and implementation conditions jointly determine outcomes.
\end{itemize}

The six design principles, theoretically grounded and mapped to implementation archetypes, provide an actionable design framework that complements the barrier interaction model. The principle--archetype alignment reveals that the most effective configurations (Hybrid Integrator) implement all six principles but require the most resources, while lightweight configurations (Competitive Quizzer) sacrifice history-specific principles for scalability --- a trade-off that the present study deliberately tests. The DMC pyramid mapping reveals that history education operates primarily at the Dynamics level of game design, producing an inverted element profile that constitutes a disciplinary signature distinguishing it from general gamification. Together, these findings --- the design principles, the archetype framework, the barrier interaction model, and the scalability paradox --- inform the experimental design presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.


\section{Methodological quality of gamification research in history education} \label{sec:mrqs}

The depth of analysis in this section reflects one of the dissertation's original contributions: the MRQS assessment directly informs the experimental design of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} and the self-assessment framework applied in the discussion of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. If the gamification evidence base (74~studies) is to compensate for the scarcity of digital history research (6~studies), its methodological quality must first be established.

\subsection{Rationale for quality assessment}

\subsubsection{The quality--effect paradox in educational technology}

The positive findings reported in Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review} must be interpreted in light of the methodological quality of the underlying research \cite{DichevDicheva2017, KoivistoHamari2019, Clark2016}. The inverse relationship between study quality and reported effect sizes is not merely a methodological truism: it is an empirically documented, quantified phenomenon in educational technology research.

Hattie's~\cite{Hattie2009} synthesis of over 800 meta-analyses identified study quality as a significant moderator of intervention effects across education broadly. Tamim et al.~\cite{Tamim2011} confirmed this in a second-order meta-analysis of educational technology, demonstrating quality sensitivity across 25 meta-analyses. The pattern is consistent across domains: Connolly et al.~\cite{Connolly2012} documented the same relationship for computer games and serious games, while Cheung and Slavin~\cite{CheungSlavin2012} found that ``the higher the methodological quality of the studies, the lower the effect size'' in a meta-analysis of 84 reading technology interventions. Most directly, Tamim et al.~\cite{TamimMMRQG2021} demonstrated that meta-analysis reporting quality scores (MMRQG) are negatively correlated with average effect sizes across 52 educational technology meta-analyses.

The implications for Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review} are immediate. The 51.4\% positive-learning-outcome rate and effect sizes of $d = 0.28$--$0.61$ (Section~\ref{sec:effectiveness}) were computed without quality weighting. If the quality--effect relationship documented above holds for gamification in history education --- and there is no theoretical reason to expect an exception --- then the true effects are plausibly smaller than reported. This is not merely an academic concern: overestimated effectiveness can lead to misallocated educational resources and uninformed policy decisions \cite{Kraft2020}. A structured quality assessment is therefore not a methodological formality but a prerequisite for interpreting the effectiveness evidence.

\subsubsection{The landscape of quality assessment tools}

The need for quality assessment established, the question becomes which instrument to use. A survey of the major quality assessment tools reveals that none was designed for the specific methodological landscape of gamification research in history education.

The \textbf{Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument (MERSQI)} \cite{ReedBeckman2007, CookReed2015} is a 10-item multi-level scale (0--3 per item) that has become the gold standard in medical education research. However, MERSQI assumes an experimental or quasi-experimental paradigm --- a poor fit for gamification research, where 68.9\% of studies employ non-experimental designs (case studies, design-based research, qualitative inquiry). Its multi-level scoring (0--3) also introduces subjectivity in boundary cases, reducing inter-rater reliability.

The \textbf{Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT)} \cite{Hong2018} is the only instrument specifically designed to handle mixed-methods studies. This is its key strength, and it has been adopted in recent gamification reviews \cite{Lampropoulos2024}. However, MMAT is generic across all domains: it contains no items for gamification-specific reporting concerns such as definitional clarity or theoretical framework specification, which are among the most problematic aspects of the gamification literature \cite{Deterding2011, Buckley2018}.

The \textbf{Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB~2.0)} tool \cite{HigginsThomas2019} is the gold standard for assessing bias in randomised controlled trials. Yet only 1 of 74 gamification-in-history-education studies is an RCT, rendering RoB~2.0 inapplicable to 98.6\% of the corpus. The \textbf{JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists} offer design-specific tools (for cohort, cross-sectional, qualitative studies), providing good individual coverage but no cross-design comparability and no reporting focus. The \textbf{Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP)} checklists are similarly design-specific, oriented toward clinical evidence appraisal. Finally, \textbf{ROBIS} \cite{Whiting2016} was designed to assess risk of bias in systematic reviews, not primary studies, requiring substantial adaptation for individual study appraisal.

The Zeng et al.~\cite{Zeng2015} systematic review of 21 quality assessment tools confirmed that these instruments cluster by study design type (RCTs, cohort studies, case-control studies), with none designed for the mixed-methods landscape characteristic of educational technology research. Figure~\ref{fig:quality-tool-comparison} summarises the applicability of each tool across seven dimensions relevant to gamification research.

\subsubsection{The quality assessment gap in gamification research}

Beyond the general tool applicability problem, the gamification literature itself suffers from a quality audit deficit. An audit of how existing gamification reviews handle methodological quality reveals a consistent pattern of omission:

\begin{itemize}
\item Dichev and Dicheva~\cite{DichevDicheva2017}: no structured quality assessment --- effectiveness claims rest entirely on unaudited evidence.
\item Koivisto and Hamari~\cite{KoivistoHamari2019}: acknowledge quality concerns qualitatively but apply no structured instrument, noting only that ``the quality of studies varied considerably.''
\item Sailer and Homner~\cite{SailerHomner2020}: conduct risk-of-bias assessment as part of their meta-analysis protocol but treat quality as a moderator variable, not as the primary object of investigation.
\item Connolly et al.~\cite{Connolly2012}: apply quality criteria to computer games broadly, but without gamification-specific items.
\end{itemize}

Three gamification-specific reporting concerns remain uncaptured by any existing instrument. First, \textbf{definitional ambiguity}: 85.1\% of studies in the corpus lack an explicit definition of gamification (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs-framework}), yet no existing tool includes a definitional criterion. Without construct clarity, cross-study comparison is methodologically questionable \cite{Deterding2011, Buckley2018}. Second, \textbf{theoretical framework specification}: whether studies ground their gamification design in an explanatory framework is critical for understanding mechanisms, yet only domain-specific tools can capture this. Third, \textbf{the mixed-methods problem}: the corpus spans qualitative case studies, quasi-experiments, design-based research, and one RCT. No single existing tool handles this diversity without penalising non-experimental designs.

The core gap, as articulated in the MRQS development paper \citeauthor{Korniienko2025mrqs}~\cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}, is that ``no study has systematically quantified the methodological quality of the gamification-in-history-education literature using a structured assessment framework.'' The field has accumulated a growing body of evidence about \emph{whether} gamification works, but possesses no systematic account of \emph{how trustworthy} that evidence is.

\subsubsection{Design requirements for a field-specific instrument}

The gap analysis above yields five design requirements for a quality assessment instrument suited to gamification research in history education:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mixed-methods applicability}: the instrument must handle qualitative, quantitative, and mixed designs without assuming an experimental paradigm.
\item \textbf{Domain-specific items}: must capture gamification-specific reporting concerns (definitions, theoretical frameworks) alongside standard methodological criteria.
\item \textbf{Binary scoring}: must use 0/1 coding to maximise inter-rater reliability and enable codability from structured data --- addressing the subjectivity problem identified for multi-level tools such as MERSQI \cite{CookReed2015}.
\item \textbf{Complementarity with risk-of-bias assessment}: must function as a reporting quality checklist complementary to, not replacing, domain-adapted risk-of-bias tools such as ROBIS \cite{Whiting2016}.
\item \textbf{Codability from extracted data}: must be assessable from structured metadata extracted during systematic review, without requiring re-access to original study PDFs.
\end{enumerate}

These five requirements jointly define the design space within which the MRQS was developed (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs-framework}). The dual-instrument approach adopted in this dissertation --- MRQS for reporting quality, adapted ROBIS for risk of bias --- addresses a known limitation: reporting quality and internal validity are related but distinct constructs \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, Zeng2015}. The MRQS is not ancillary to the dissertation: it directly informs the experimental design of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, which deliberately addresses MRQS-identified weaknesses, and underpins the self-assessment framework applied in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{The MRQS framework} \label{sec:mrqs-framework}

To address the absence of a structured quality assessment instrument adapted for gamification research in history education, we developed the Methodological Reporting Quality Score (MRQS) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} --- a ten-item binary instrument (scored 0 or 1 per item, range 0--10) designed to assess the completeness and rigour of reporting practices. This section describes the instrument's theoretical foundations, structural architecture, coding methodology, and its integration with risk-of-bias assessment.

\subsubsection{Theoretical foundations and item selection}

The MRQS draws on four families of methodological guidelines for its item selection:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CONSORT 2010} \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}: reporting standards for intervention trials --- grounds M1 (sample size), M3 (control group), M5 (effect sizes);
\item \textbf{Cochrane Handbook} \cite{HigginsThomas2019}: systematic review methodology --- grounds M3 (comparison group), M5 (standardised measures), M7 (inferential statistics);
\item \textbf{GRADE} \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}: evidence quality grading --- grounds the hierarchical relationship between reporting completeness and evidence strength;
\item \textbf{Campbell Collaboration} \cite{Campbell2020}: social science evidence standards --- grounds the adaptation of clinical frameworks to educational research.
\end{enumerate}

These frameworks share a core epistemological principle: \emph{what is not reported cannot be evaluated} --- reporting quality is a necessary (though not sufficient) condition for evidence synthesis. \citeauthor{MaWang2020}~\cite{MaWang2020} distinguish three constructs in quality assessment: methodological quality, reporting quality, and risk of bias. The MRQS targets the second construct exclusively, complemented by the adapted ROBIS for the third (Section~\ref{sec:dual-instrument}).

The deliberate scope limitation is a design choice, not a weakness: by measuring \emph{reporting} quality rather than overall study merit, the MRQS avoids the evaluative subjectivity inherent in instruments such as MERSQI \cite{CookReed2015} while remaining fully codable from structured extraction data. A critical gap identified by \citeauthor{Lan2023}~\cite{Lan2023} underscores the need: only 34.8\% of systematic reviews in education and management used any evidence assessment tool at all, and 66 different tools were identified among those that did --- confirming both the necessity of quality assessment and the fragmentation of current approaches.

The instrument comprises 10 items, balancing comprehensiveness (covering sampling, design, measurement, statistics, and domain-specific reporting) with codability (all items binary, all assessable from structured data extraction without re-reading PDFs). The items are organised into three functional tiers reflecting their role in evidence synthesis (Figure~\ref{fig:mrqs-item-architecture}).

\subsubsection{Three-tier item architecture}

The 10 MRQS items are grouped by functional purpose rather than presented as a flat list. This three-tier taxonomy reflects the distinct roles that different reporting criteria play in systematic evidence synthesis.

\textbf{Tier~A --- Empirical design items (M2, M3, M5, M6, M7).} These five items assess whether the study design supports causal or quantitative inference. They determine whether a study can contribute to meta-analytic synthesis and whether effect estimates are trustworthy:

\begin{itemize}
\item M2 (sample $n \geq 30$): minimum for parametric inference \cite{Cohen1960};
\item M3 (control or comparison group): causal inference requirement \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, HigginsThomas2019};
\item M5 (effect sizes or standardised measures): meta-analytic prerequisite \cite{Borenstein2009};
\item M6 (pre-post or longitudinal design): within-participant change assessment;
\item M7 (inferential statistics reported): probabilistic evidence for observed differences.
\end{itemize}

A study scoring 0 on all Tier~A items has produced no quantitative evidence interpretable within standard statistical frameworks --- which describes 68.9\% of the corpus that employs non-experimental designs.

\textbf{Tier~B --- Reporting transparency items (M1, M4, M10).} These three items assess whether basic methodological information is accessible to readers and reviewers, enabling study evaluation and replication:

\begin{itemize}
\item M1 (sample size explicitly reported): minimum descriptive requirement \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT};
\item M4 (validated measurement instruments): measurement trustworthiness evidence;
\item M10 (study limitations explicitly discussed): reader calibration of confidence.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Tier~C --- Domain-specific items (M8, M9).} These two items capture gamification-specific concerns absent from all generic quality tools. They are what make the MRQS a \emph{field-specific} instrument rather than a generic checklist:

\begin{itemize}
\item M8 (explicit definition of gamification): construct validity and cross-study comparability \cite{Deterding2011, Buckley2018} --- no generic tool includes this criterion;
\item M9 (theoretical framework explicitly stated): explanatory grounding for design choices.
\end{itemize}

The taxonomy has three consequences. First, \emph{Tier~A items are design-dependent}: qualitative and case studies cannot score on M2, M3, M5, M6, or M7 --- their maximum achievable score is 5/10. This is a known and acknowledged feature, not a flaw: the MRQS measures quantitative reporting completeness, and the design-dependent ceiling identifies the reporting characteristics of the study design landscape. Second, \emph{Tier~C items are MRQS-unique}: M8 and M9 are the items that no existing tool (MERSQI, MMAT, Cochrane RoB, JBI, CASP) captures, as demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:quality-tool-comparison}. Third, \emph{each tier serves a different synthesis function}: Tier~A enables meta-analytic inclusion, Tier~B enables study evaluation and replication, Tier~C enables cross-study conceptual comparison.

\subsubsection{Coding methodology and operationalisation}

\textbf{Binary scoring rationale.} Each item is scored 0 (criterion absent) or 1 (criterion present), yielding a composite range of 0--10. Binary coding maximises inter-rater reliability by eliminating the boundary judgments inherent in multi-level scales (cf.\ MERSQI's 0--3 per item; AMSTAR-2's four-level responses) and enables automated assessment from structured extraction data. This approach aligns with the independently developed ReMarQ instrument \cite{MarquesCruz2025}, which adopted 26 dichotomous items for systematic review quality assessment, confirming that binary coding supports both efficient assessment and item response theory analysis.

\textbf{Coding from structured data.} All 10 items were coded from JSON extraction records produced during the parent systematic review \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, without re-reading original PDFs. This design constraint ensures reproducibility and enables scalability, but means MRQS prevalence rates should be interpreted as \emph{lower bounds} --- studies may have reported relevant information in sections not captured by the 21-item extraction prompt.

\textbf{Specific coding decisions} (documented for transparency):

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{M4}: coded from ROBIS signalling question Q3. Response formats varied (``yes''/``no''/``unclear'' vs.\ ``low concern''/``high concern''). Mapping: ``yes'' and ``low concern'' $\rightarrow$ 1; all others $\rightarrow$ 0.
\item \emph{M5}: keyword matching in the \texttt{statistical\_results} extraction field for: ``effect size'', ``Cohen'', ``eta'', ``$d =$'', ``Hedge'', ``partial eta''. May undercount non-standard notations.
\item \emph{M10}: coded from Q6. ``Partial'' coded as 1 --- partial discussion of limitations demonstrates awareness of methodological constraints.
\item \emph{M6, M7}: keyword matching against \texttt{study\_design}, \texttt{comparative\_methodology}, and \texttt{statistical\_results} extraction fields.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The dual-instrument approach: MRQS and adapted ROBIS} \label{sec:dual-instrument}

The MRQS is paired with an adapted ROBIS risk-of-bias tool to form a dual-instrument assessment. The theoretical justification rests on the \emph{complementarity principle}: reporting quality and risk of bias are related but distinct constructs. \cite{PerryWhitmarsh2021}, comparing ROBIS and AMSTAR-2, concluded that ``strictly speaking, one tool assesses methodological quality and the other assesses risk of bias'' --- yet both are needed because ``there is considerable overlap between the tools in terms of the signalling questions.''

The same complementarity principle applies to the MRQS + adapted ROBIS pairing:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MRQS} = reporting quality: ``Is the information present?'' (descriptive, binary, codable from data);
\item \textbf{Adapted ROBIS} = risk of bias: ``Given the information, how credible is the finding?'' (evaluative, judgment-based).
\end{itemize}

The adapted ROBIS tool \cite{Whiting2016}, modified for individual study appraisal by Iryna~S.~Mintii \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}, comprises three domains, each with two signalling questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Domain~1 --- Participant selection and study context}: (Q1) Are the criteria for selecting participants clearly described? (Q2) Is the sample representative of the target population?
\item \textbf{Domain~2 --- Data collection and analysis}: (Q3) Were valid and reliable tools used for data collection? (Q4) Are the methods of data analysis described and justified?
\item \textbf{Domain~3 --- Interpretation and reporting}: (Q5) Are interpretations and conclusions consistent with results? (Q6) Are limitations acknowledged and discussed?
\end{enumerate}

Domain-level judgments follow three rules: (1) both signalling questions ``yes'' $\rightarrow$ low risk; (2) at least one ``no'' $\rightarrow$ high risk; (3) at least one ``unclear'' with no ``no'' $\rightarrow$ unclear risk.

Two inter-linkages between the instruments are by design (Figure~\ref{fig:mrqs-robis-complementarity}). M4 and Q3 both concern instrument validation, but the MRQS checks \emph{reporting} (was validity evidence mentioned?), while the ROBIS assesses \emph{judgment} (were the tools actually valid and reliable?). Similarly, M10 and Q6 both concern limitations, but the MRQS checks \emph{presence} (were limitations discussed at all?), while the ROBIS assesses \emph{adequacy} (were the right limitations identified?). These overlaps are intentional: they enable empirical comparison of reporting vs.\ judgment assessments of the same construct.

\input{figures/mrqs_robis_complementarity}

\subsubsection{Interpretive framework and known limitations}

MRQS scores should be interpreted as \emph{reporting completeness indices}, not quality rankings. A score of 3/10 means ``three reporting criteria met,'' not ``poor study.'' The design-dependent scoring ceiling --- an RCT can theoretically score 10/10; a qualitative case study can score at most 5/10 (Tier~A items inapplicable) --- is a feature of the instrument (it identifies the reporting characteristics of the study design landscape) but must be accounted for in interpretation.

The multi-dimensional nature of the instrument is expected to produce moderate internal consistency (Cronbach's $\alpha$ in the moderate range), consistent with its design as a heterogeneous reporting checklist rather than a unidimensional psychometric scale. This distinguishes the MRQS from instruments such as MERSQI, which aim for construct homogeneity \cite{CookReed2015}.

Two anticipated failure modes are worth noting: a study may fail on \emph{empirical design} (Tier~A: small sample, no control, no effect sizes) while succeeding on \emph{conceptual reporting} (Tier~C: clear definition, stated framework) --- or vice versa. The MRQS captures both dimensions simultaneously, enabling identification of dimension-specific patterns (tested in Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}). The instrument has not been externally validated beyond the corpus of 74 studies; its generalisability to other educational technology domains remains to be established.

\subsection{Quality assessment results} \label{sec:qa-results}

\subsubsection{Overall MRQS distribution}

Across the 74 included studies, the mean MRQS was 4.31 out of 10 (SD = 1.93, Mdn = 4.0, IQR = 3--6, range = 0--9) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. No study achieved the maximum score of 10. The distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:mrqs-distribution}) was approximately unimodal, with a modal cluster at scores 4--5 accounting for 26 studies (35.2\%). Fifteen studies (20.3\%) scored in the low range (0--2), 38 (51.4\%) in the middle range (3--5), and 21 (28.4\%) in the upper range (6--9).

\input{figures/mrqs_score_distribution}

The left-skewed shape reflects the design-dependent scoring ceiling identified in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs-framework}: qualitative and design-based studies can score at most 5/10, as Tier~A items (control group, pre-post design, inferential statistics, effect sizes) are structurally inapplicable. The concentration of studies at 4--5 therefore represents the \emph{practical ceiling} for non-experimental designs that meet all applicable reporting criteria. Only one study scored 9 and one scored 8 --- both experimental designs that nonetheless failed to report effect sizes.

\subsubsection{The temporal divergence paradox}

The $\Delta$ column in Table~\ref{tab:mrqs-prevalence} conceals a striking paradox, made visible in Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-divergence}: reporting transparency items improved from the earlier (2004--2017, $n = 37$) to the later (2018--2024, $n = 37$) period, while empirical design items declined. Limitations discussion gained +18.9 percentage points, sample size reporting +10.8~pp, and instrument validation +5.4~pp. Simultaneously, control-group use fell by $-$13.5~pp, pre-post designs by $-$8.1~pp, and explicit definitions by $-$8.1~pp.

\input{figures/temporal_divergence}

This divergence reveals that ``methodological sophistication in design has not accompanied improvements in reporting transparency'' \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. The most plausible explanation is compositional: the growth in publications after 2018 was driven disproportionately by qualitative and design-based studies that structurally cannot score on Tier~A items (control group, pre-post design, effect sizes). As these studies entered the corpus, they \emph{raised} transparency metrics (they discuss limitations, state frameworks) while \emph{lowering} design metrics --- a denominator effect rather than a genuine regression in experimental practice.

Cross-domain comparison supports this interpretation: \citeauthor{Connolly2012}~\cite{Connolly2012} found a 14\% control-group rate across 129 game-based learning studies. Our overall 31.1\% is higher but declining toward that baseline, suggesting convergence with the broader educational technology field as the gamification-in-history literature diversifies methodologically.

\subsubsection{MRQS psychometric profile}

The internal consistency of the MRQS was moderate (Cronbach's $\alpha = 0.58$), below the conventional .70 psychometric threshold but appropriate for the instrument's design \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. The MRQS is not a unidimensional scale --- it measures 10 distinct reporting criteria across three tiers (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs-framework}) --- and moderate $\alpha$ is therefore expected and consistent with its function as a heterogeneous reporting checklist.

Corrected item-total correlations ranged from $r = -0.13$ (M10, limitations discussed) to $r = 0.50$ (M7, inferential statistics). A quantitative-methods cluster emerged clearly: M7 ($r = 0.50$), M6 pre-post design ($r = 0.48$), and M3 control group ($r = 0.42$) --- items that co-occur in experimental designs. Conceptual items showed near-independence from this cluster: M8 explicit definition ($r = 0.05$) and M9 theoretical framework ($r = 0.11$) contributed minimally to total score variance.

The negative correlation of M10 ($r = -0.13$) is interpretively revealing: higher-scoring studies (predominantly experimental designs) discussed limitations \emph{less often} than lower-scoring qualitative studies, where reflexive discussion of limitations is a disciplinary norm. This inversion confirms that M10 captures a distinct reporting practice orthogonal to design rigour.

Inter-item phi coefficients reinforced this structure: M6$\times$M7 = 0.60 (the strongest association, linking pre-post design with statistical testing), M3$\times$M6 = 0.48, M1$\times$M2 = 0.44 (sample size reporting with adequate sample size), and M3$\times$M7 = 0.36 \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Meanwhile, M8 and M9 remained independent of the quantitative cluster (all $\phi < 0.15$).

These patterns confirm empirically the two-dimension structure anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:mrqs-framework}: \emph{empirical design quality} (Tier~A items forming a coherent cluster) and \emph{conceptual reporting quality} (Tier~C items operating independently) constitute distinct dimensions of methodological quality \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Clark2016}. This finding directly informs the interpretation of definitional practices in Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}.

\subsubsection{Risk-of-bias results}

The overall risk-of-bias assessment revealed \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Whiting2016}:
\begin{itemize}
\item High risk of bias: 62 studies (83.8\%)
\item Moderate risk of bias: 9 studies (12.2\%)
\item Low risk of bias: 1 study (1.4\%) \cite{15Slater2022}
\item Unclear: 2 studies (2.7\%)
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:rob-domains} displays the domain-level breakdown. A clear gradient emerged from participant selection to interpretation: Domain~1 (85.1\% high) $>$ Domain~2 (63.5\% high) $>$ Domain~3 (55.4\% high) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\input{figures/rob_domain_stacked}

\textit{Domain 1 --- Participant selection and study context} was the most persistent source of weakness, with 63 of 74 studies (85.1\%) rated as high risk and none achieving low risk. The universal reliance on convenience sampling (intact classes, available groups) without discussion of how the sample relates to a broader population accounts for this finding \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}.

\textit{Domain 2 --- Data collection and analysis} showed 47 studies (63.5\%) at high risk, 10 (13.5\%) at moderate risk, and 9 (12.2\%) at low risk. The primary concerns were the use of unvalidated instruments (only 21.6\% reported validity or reliability evidence) and insufficient description of analytical procedures.

\textit{Domain 3 --- Interpretation and reporting} was the strongest domain, with 41 studies (55.4\%) at high risk but 13 (17.6\%) achieving low risk. Studies generally aligned conclusions with results, though the discussion of limitations was met by fewer than half.

By publication type, conference papers showed 90\% high overall risk versus 71\% for journal articles. No temporal improvement was observed (86\% high in 2004--2017 vs.\ 81\% in 2018--2024). Cross-domain comparison is informative: \citeauthor{Igelstrom2021}~\cite{Igelstrom2021} found 54\% serious or critical risk of bias across 124 medical systematic reviews using ROBINS-I --- our 83.8\% high rate exceeds this substantially, confirming that gamification-in-history is a less methodologically mature field than clinical research.

\subsubsection{Signalling question-level analysis}

The six signalling questions (Q1--Q6) underlying the three risk-of-bias domains provide the most granular view of methodological quality (Figure~\ref{fig:signalling-questions}) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\input{figures/signalling_question_analysis}

Q1 (selection criteria stated): 26 studies (35.1\%) received favourable ratings, 38 (51.4\%) unfavourable, and 10 (13.5\%) ambiguous. Q3 (valid measurement tools): only 16 studies (21.6\%) favourable, with 23 (31.1\%) ambiguous --- reflecting instruments whose validation was partially described but insufficient. Q4 (appropriate analysis methods): 17 studies (23.0\%) favourable, the majority unfavourable (52.7\%). Q5 (sound interpretation): 25 studies (33.8\%) favourable, with the highest ambiguity rate (41.9\%), indicating that interpretive adequacy is often difficult to judge. Q6 (limitations discussed): the highest favourable rate at 35 studies (47.3\%), consistent with this being the most commonly met Tier~C criterion.

\textbf{The Q2 universal failure.} The most striking finding across the entire instrument is Q2 (sample representativeness): not a single study out of 74 received a favourable rating (0\% favourable, 74.3\% unfavourable, 25.7\% ambiguous). This is not merely a sampling limitation --- convenience sampling from intact classrooms is structural in educational research. The reportable concern is the complete failure to even \emph{discuss} representativeness: no study considered how its sample might differ from a broader population of interest. \citeauthor{Wild2022}~\cite{Wild2022} demonstrated that student convenience samples differ from national populations at $d = 0.56$ on literacy and numeracy measures across 32 countries --- an effect size exceeding the average treatment effect found in many educational interventions. The absence of representativeness discussion across all 74 studies therefore constitutes a systematic blind spot that should be addressed in future research reporting guidelines \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}.

\subsubsection{Domain co-occurrence and systemic weakness}

The co-occurrence of domain-level risk-of-bias ratings reveals the extent to which methodological weaknesses are systemic rather than domain-specific \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Whiting2016}. The most common profile was high--high--high (H-H-H): 32 of 74 studies (43.2\%) received high risk ratings in all three domains. Additional profiles included H-H-M (5 studies), H-M-M (4), M-M-M (3), and H-L-M (3). Only 18 studies (24.3\%) achieved at least one low-risk domain.

The overlap statistics confirm systemic co-occurrence: of the 63 studies rated high on Domain~1, 43 (68.3\%) were also high on Domain~2, and 37 (58.7\%) were also high on Domain~3. This pattern indicates general methodological deficiency rather than isolated weaknesses in specific domains.

However, Domain~3 reveals an important asymmetry and the most constructive finding for practitioners: 13 of the 63 D1-high studies (20.6\%) nonetheless achieved low or moderate ratings on Domain~3. Sound interpretation and transparent limitation reporting are achievable even within the constraints that make strong participant selection impossible in educational settings. This decoupling suggests that improving interpretive practices does not require solving the structural challenges of sampling --- it requires only more careful writing.

By study characteristics, conference papers showed 90\% versus journal articles' 71\% high overall risk. Higher education studies exhibited 95\% high-risk Domain~1 versus 77\% for secondary education. The presence of an explicit definition was negligibly associated with risk of bias (81.8\% high vs.\ 84.1\% for studies without definitions), confirming that definitional clarity is orthogonal to design rigour --- a finding elaborated in Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}.

\subsection{LLM validation of quality assessment} \label{sec:llm-validation}

The use of large language models at multiple stages of the parent systematic reviews creates a natural experiment for assessing LLM reliability across tasks of varying complexity. This section presents the first unified analysis of LLM agreement data spanning three task types --- screening, factual extraction, and evaluative risk-of-bias assessment --- and proposes a \emph{task complexity gradient} that explains why published LLM benchmarks report dramatically different reliability figures.

\subsubsection{Context: LLMs in the parent systematic reviews}

The two parent reviews employed LLMs in complementary configurations \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. The gamification systematic review used Claude~3.5~Sonnet for eligibility screening (applying 7~binary inclusion criteria to 423~candidate abstracts) and GPT-4o for both data extraction (21~structured items per study) and risk-of-bias assessment (6~signalling questions across 3~ROBIS domains). The digital history systematic review used Claude~4~Opus for screening and Claude~4~Sonnet together with Grok~3 for data extraction; notably, Claude~4~Sonnet exhibited domain confusion by recording both experimental and control group results in the ``Experimental group results'' field, requiring additional verification passes.

This multi-model, multi-task deployment spans all three major stages at which LLMs have been applied in systematic reviews. \citeauthor{Blaizot2022}~\cite{Blaizot2022} found that 73\% of AI-in-systematic-review applications targeted screening, 13\% extraction, and 13\% risk-of-bias assessment; the present work is among the few to address all three within a single research programme. Throughout, LLM outputs were treated as auxiliary drafts subject to mandatory human verification, consistent with emerging best practice \cite{Khraisha2023}.

Comparison of GPT-4o's automated signalling-question responses against the final human-verified judgments across all 74~studies and 6~questions (444~question--study pairs) revealed 37.6\% overall agreement \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Cohen's $\kappa$ ranged from $-$0.04 (Q3) to 0.21 (Q6), corresponding to ``poor'' to at best ``fair'' agreement by the benchmarks of \citeauthor{LandisKoch1977}~\cite{LandisKoch1977} \cite{Cohen1960, Fleiss1971}. Conversely, Q3 (validated instruments) produced the lowest agreement (29.7\%) because instrument validation requires nuanced interpretation of partial reporting: GPT-4o systematically rated unvalidated instruments as ``unclear'' rather than ``no'', hedging where human reviewers judged definitively.

The dominant disagreement pattern was a shift from more favourable GPT-4o ratings to less favourable verified ratings. For Q2 (sample representativeness), GPT-4o judged 33~studies as ``unclear'' that were verified as ``no'', and a further 5~originally rated ``yes'' were changed to ``no''. For Q4 (analysis methods), 21~GPT-4o ``yes'' responses were changed to ``no'' during verification, and 9~were reclassified as ``moderate concern'' \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\subsubsection{Multi-model data extraction reliability}

A supplementary comparison involved three additional LLMs (Gemini~3~Pro, ChatGPT-4, and Grok~4.1-thinking) independently extracting data from all 80~candidate studies using the same 21-item prompt \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Pairwise agreement was computed on two objective fields:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Publication year:} Gemini vs.\ Grok 97.1\%, Gemini vs.\ ChatGPT-4 82.4\%. Year extraction was non-trivial for older conference papers that embedded publication dates only in copyright notices.
  \item \textbf{Publication type:} Gemini vs.\ Grok 94.9\%, Gemini vs.\ ChatGPT-4 81.0\%. Disagreements typically involved the boundary between ``conference paper'' and ``book chapter'' in edited volumes.
\end{itemize}

ChatGPT-4 showed 14--18\% disagreement with both other models across both fields, while Gemini and Grok maintained $>$94\% concordance. The key pattern --- agreement highest for unambiguous fields (year) and lower for classificatory judgments (publication type) --- foreshadows the broader task complexity gradient.

The second systematic review's extraction experience reinforced this pattern: Claude~4~Sonnet marked both experimental and control group results in the ``Experimental group results'' field, a domain confusion error that required complete re-verification of affected entries. Even within factual extraction, fields requiring structural understanding of study design proved more error-prone than simple metadata.

\subsubsection{Cross-study benchmarking: the task complexity gradient}

The present study's $\kappa = -$0.04 to 0.21 appears dramatically low in isolation. Contextualising against the rapidly growing literature on LLM-assisted systematic reviews reveals that this result is not anomalous but rather occupies a predictable position on a \emph{task complexity gradient} (Figure~\ref{fig:llm-reliability-gradient}).

\input{figures/llm_reliability_gradient}

\textbf{Tier~1 --- Factual extraction} (high reliability). The present study achieved 82--97\% pairwise agreement across three LLMs on metadata fields. \citeauthor{Kim2025bmj}~\cite{Kim2025bmj} reported 88.6\% concordance for GPT-4-assisted data extraction in urology, and \citeauthor{Purewal2025}~\cite{Purewal2025} found 100\% accuracy for meta-analytic data pooling in chronic pain research.

\textbf{Tier~2 --- Structured RoB on standardised designs} (moderate reliability). \cite{Lai2024jama} achieved $\kappa > 0.80$ in 7--8 of 8~Cochrane RoB~2 domains (84.5--89.5\% accuracy) when evaluating 30~RCTs with GPT-4o against a three-expert gold standard. \citeauthor{Taneri2025}~\cite{Taneri2025} reported $\kappa = 0.51$ on Cochrane Reviews using RoB~2. \citeauthor{Xia2025}~\cite{Xia2025} found 80.8--83.3\% accuracy on 30~cohort studies. Even \cite{Kim2025bmj}, who reported a lower $\kappa = 0.237$ for RoB, noted that human--human agreement was \emph{worse} ($\kappa = 0.094$), suggesting that GPT-4 outperformed the human baseline on structured tools.

\textbf{Tier~3 --- Adapted RoB on heterogeneous educational studies} (poor reliability). The present study's $\kappa = -$0.04 to 0.21 on 74~heterogeneous studies using an adapted ROBIS instrument with non-standard response categories represents the low end of the gradient.

The gradient is driven by three interacting factors: (1)~\emph{tool standardisation} --- Cochrane RoB~2 has well-defined binary domains with detailed signalling questions, while the adapted ROBIS required interpretive judgment at every step; (2)~\emph{study design homogeneity} --- RCTs and cohort studies share structural features that LLMs can pattern-match, while the gamification corpus mixed qualitative, quasi-experimental, design-based, and case study designs; (3)~\emph{question objectivity} --- ``Was the randomisation sequence adequate?'' is verifiable from reported methods, whereas ``Is the interpretation consistent with the results?'' requires evaluative reasoning about the relationship between findings and conclusions. This framework explains why \cite{Lai2024jama} achieved $\kappa > 0.80$ and the present study achieved $\kappa = -$0.04 --- they measured fundamentally different things. The gradient is not random variation but a predictable consequence of task complexity.

\subsubsection{Disagreement taxonomy and the leniency bias}

Decomposing the 277~disagreements between GPT-4o and human-verified assessments reveals two qualitatively distinct categories (Figure~\ref{fig:disagreement-taxonomy}).

\input{figures/llm_disagreement_taxonomy}

\textbf{Response-format mismatches} accounted for 62~of 277~disagreements (22.4\%). GPT-4o was prompted to use a three-level scheme (yes/no/unclear), while the human verification process introduced additional categories --- ``moderate concern'', ``some concerns'', ``partial'', ``low concern'' --- that were unavailable to GPT-4o \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. These mismatches inflate measured disagreement without reflecting substantive differences in evaluative judgment.

\textbf{Substantive leniency-direction shifts} dominated the remaining $\sim$215~disagreements. The consistent pattern was GPT-4o favourable $\rightarrow$ human unfavourable: on Q2, 33~``unclear'' were verified as ``no'' (GPT-4o hedged where human reviewers judged definitively); on Q4, 21~``yes'' were changed to ``no'' (GPT-4o accepted surface-level methodological descriptions at face value). Reverse shifts --- human more favourable than GPT-4o --- were rare.

This leniency bias has been independently confirmed. \citeauthor{DiPumpo2025}~\cite{DiPumpo2025} found that custom GPTs assigned ``high risk'' at half the rate of human review teams (13.9\% vs.\ 25.0\%) in a public health systematic review --- the same directional pattern in an unrelated domain. The convergence across independent studies strengthens the conclusion that leniency is a systematic property of current LLMs on evaluative RoB tasks, not an artifact of any single study's methodology \cite{MalekiKarami2024}.

A methodological caveat applies: all verified assessments in the present study reflect one researcher's post-hoc judgments applied to LLM-generated first drafts, introducing unquantified single-rater subjectivity. This limitation is inherent to any single-rater verification design, not specific to the present study. Leniency bias is particularly consequential because it systematically inflates the apparent quality of the evidence base --- reviews relying on unsupervised LLM RoB assessment would underestimate the prevalence of high-risk studies.

\subsubsection{Methodological implications for hybrid systematic review workflows}

The task complexity gradient yields a tiered framework for LLM deployment in systematic reviews \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tier~1 tasks} (factual extraction): LLMs may be used freely for metadata fields --- publication year, country, sample size, publication type --- where agreement rates of 82--100\% support unsupervised extraction with spot-check verification.
  \item \textbf{Tier~2 tasks} (structured RoB on homogeneous designs): LLMs serve as a useful first-pass tool with mandatory human verification. The time savings from LLM pre-screening are substantial, and agreement rates ($\kappa = 0.24$--$0.80+$) are sufficient for initial triage.
  \item \textbf{Tier~3 tasks} (adapted RoB on heterogeneous corpora): LLMs should not be used without complete human re-assessment. Near-chance agreement ($\kappa \approx 0$) means that LLM output provides no informational value for these tasks; the ``draft'' it produces may be worse than starting from scratch because it anchors the human reviewer toward more lenient judgments.
\end{enumerate}

Three additional recommendations emerge from the empirical findings \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Khraisha2023}. First, response schemes must be identical for both LLM and human raters --- introducing new categories during verification confounds agreement measurement and inflates apparent disagreement. Second, LLM agreement statistics (percentage agreement, $\kappa$) should be reported alongside substantive findings to ensure transparency about data provenance. Third, when multiple LLMs are used, pairwise agreement should be computed to identify items requiring closer scrutiny \cite{Rubinstein2025}.

An automation paradox emerges from this analysis: the tasks where LLMs are most needed --- evaluative judgment on large heterogeneous corpora, exactly the bottleneck in educational technology systematic reviews --- are precisely the tasks where they are least reliable. This paradox frames the need for purpose-built tools designed specifically for educational systematic review workflows \cite{Wang2025aera, Blaizot2022} rather than the application of general-purpose LLMs to tasks that exceed their current evaluative capabilities.

\subsection{Definitional practices and quality} \label{sec:def-quality}

Section~\ref{sec:definitional-analysis} documented that 85.1\% of gamification-in-history studies lack explicit definitions. This section examines the theoretical significance of that finding by analysing how definitional practices interact with methodological quality across multiple dimensions and, ultimately, how definitional ambiguity undermines the cumulative knowledge-building capacity of the field.

\subsubsection{The definitional-quality nexus}

The association between definitional practices and MRQS scores (5.36 vs.\ 4.13, $p = .065$) and the non-association with risk-of-bias ratings (81.8\% vs.\ 84.1\%) are not contradictory findings --- they are complementary evidence for a two-dimension model of methodological quality \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

Studies providing explicit gamification definitions scored higher on \emph{Tier~C items} --- sample reporting (M1), definition (M8), framework (M9), and limitations (M10) --- all reporter-level practices reflecting conscientiousness. However, definitional studies showed \emph{no advantage on Tier~A items} --- control groups (M3), pre-post designs (M6), and statistical testing (M7) --- design-level features reflecting resource constraints and disciplinary conventions. The psychometric independence of M8 ($r = 0.05$ with all other MRQS items) confirms quantitatively that definitional clarity operates on a dimension separate from empirical design quality \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

This distinction carries a practical implication: definitional clarity is a \emph{reporter-level trait} (or research-tradition trait), not a \emph{study-level methodological feature}. Interventions targeting definitional practices --- such as journal guidelines requiring definitions --- would improve reporting transparency but would not, by themselves, improve study design quality. The latter requires different interventions: training in experimental methods, access to larger samples, and institutional support for rigorous designs \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Clark2016}.

\subsubsection{The normalisation paradox}

The decline in definitional clarity from the earlier to the later period ($-$8.1 percentage points: from 18.9\% in 2004--2017 to 10.8\% in 2018--2024) is counterintuitive: field maturation should increase conceptual precision \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Three mechanisms explain this paradox:

\textbf{Terminological normalisation.} As gamification enters mainstream educational discourse, authors assume shared understanding. The term functions as a boundary object --- flexibly interpreted across communities without formal negotiation of meaning \cite{Deterding2011}. Each community projects its own understanding onto the same word, and the lack of friction creates an illusion of consensus.

\textbf{Compositional dilution.} The post-2018 growth in the corpus was driven by newer entrants from history education and computer science who lack the definitional conventions of the gamification-specialist traditions. The same compositional mechanism that explains the temporal divergence paradox in Section~\ref{sec:qa-results} --- concurrent improvements in some reporting dimensions alongside declines in others --- also explains the definitional decline \cite{Buckley2018}.

\textbf{Definitional exhaustion.} \citeauthor{Landers2019}~\cite{Landers2019} observed that after a decade of debate about what gamification ``really means,'' researchers experience definitional fatigue --- treating the question as settled despite persistent disagreement. The declining rate signals that the debate has been abandoned, not resolved.

The normalisation paradox has a direct parallel in the construct validity literature: \citeauthor{Cronbach1955}~\cite{Cronbach1955} argued that construct validity requires continuous definitional refinement as evidence accumulates. The gamification field has done the opposite --- reducing definitional effort as the evidence base grows --- creating a widening gap between empirical volume and conceptual precision.

\subsubsection{The construct validity cascade}

Definitional ambiguity is not a merely taxonomic problem --- it triggers a cascade of methodological consequences that undermine the entire evidence base. Figure~\ref{fig:construct-validity-cascade} presents this five-stage model, each quantitatively anchored to findings from the 74-study corpus.

\textbf{Stage~1 --- Definitional ambiguity} (85.1\% of studies). Without explicit definitions, it is unclear whether ``gamification'' refers to adding quiz points to a lecture (structural gamification), building a historical role-play narrative (content gamification), using \emph{Assassin's Creed} in class (game-based learning), or deploying a purpose-built simulation (serious games). The four conceptual approaches documented in Section~\ref{sec:definitional-analysis} describe genuinely different interventions \cite{Deterding2011, Becker2021}.

\textbf{Stage~2 --- Construct heterogeneity.} When studies use the same label for different interventions, the 74-study corpus is not a homogeneous evidence base but a mixture of at least four distinct constructs. The teacher adding quiz points (structural gamification, $n \approx 15$) and the developer building a VR historical simulation (serious games, $n \approx 20$) are studying different causal mechanisms --- yet both appear under ``gamification'' \cite{Buckley2018, DichevDicheva2017}.

\textbf{Stage~3 --- Measurement incommensurability.} Different constructs require different outcome measures. Structural gamification's effect on motivation is appropriately measured by engagement scales; a VR simulation's effect on historical empathy requires perspective-taking instruments. With only 21.6\% of studies using validated instruments (M4) and 9.5\% reporting effect sizes (M5), the corpus employs ad hoc measures that cannot be compared across construct boundaries \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Borenstein2009}.

\textbf{Stage~4 --- Synthesis failure.} Meta-analytic pooling requires comparable constructs, comparable measures, and reported effect sizes. The cascade from Stage~1 through Stage~3 forecloses all three requirements. The present review --- like all preceding reviews of gamification in history education --- is limited to narrative synthesis, not because of methodological choice but because the evidence base is structurally incompatible with quantitative synthesis.

\textbf{Stage~5 --- Knowledge gap persistence.} After two decades and 74 studies, the field cannot answer with confidence the basic question: ``Does gamification improve history learning?'' The evidence base grows in volume but not in cumulative knowledge, because each study contributes to a different sub-construct without acknowledging it. This is a \emph{pre-paradigmatic} state --- multiple incommensurable frameworks coexist without convergence \cite{Landers2019, Cronbach1955}.

\input{figures/construct_validity_cascade}

The cascade is not inevitable. Breaking the chain at Stage~1 --- requiring explicit definitions --- would propagate improvements through all subsequent stages. This is why definitional analysis is not a ``merely semantic'' exercise but a prerequisite for the field's transition from pre-paradigmatic accumulation to cumulative knowledge-building \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsubsection{Implications for construct consolidation}

The construct validity cascade identifies the precise intervention point: Stage~1. Three theoretical principles for construct consolidation emerge from this analysis:

\textbf{Definitional minimalism over maximalism.} The field does not need one universal definition of gamification. Following \cite{Landers2019}, studies need only specify which of the four conceptual approaches their intervention instantiates and provide a working definition sufficient for replication. This is analogous to the ``minimum reporting'' philosophy of CONSORT \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}: specify enough to enable comparison, not enough to settle philosophical debates.

\textbf{Construct-measurement alignment.} Once the conceptual approach is specified, outcome measures must be selected that are appropriate for that specific construct. Structural gamification studies should measure motivation and engagement; content gamification studies should measure deeper learning outcomes; GBL studies should measure game-specific learning objectives. The current practice of using ad hoc measures regardless of construct type contributes directly to Stage~3 of the cascade \cite{Borenstein2009, 36Scholz2021}.

\textbf{Synthesis-ready reporting.} Even without definitional consensus, studies that report (a)~their conceptual approach, (b)~validated outcome measures, and (c)~standardised effect sizes would enable construct-specific meta-analyses --- partial syntheses within each approach that would be far more informative than the current field-wide narrative synthesis \cite{HuotariHamari2017, Korniienko2025mrqs}. The proposed reporting checklist in Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist} operationalises these principles.

The automation paradox from Section~\ref{sec:llm-validation} acquires additional significance here: LLMs could potentially automate the classification of studies into conceptual-approach categories based on intervention descriptions, enabling retrospective construct-specific synthesis --- but only if the original studies provide sufficient intervention detail to support classification.

\subsection{Detailed bias analysis by category} \label{sec:detailed-bias}

Beyond the aggregate statistics documented in Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}, the risk-of-bias assessment reveals specific patterns whose interactions and cumulative consequences merit detailed analysis. This section elevates the examination from descriptive inventory to systematic validity analysis, culminating in a formal evidence certainty assessment.

\subsubsection{Validity threat mapping}

The bias categories documented below can be organised according to four types of validity threat: statistical conclusion validity, internal validity, construct validity, and external validity \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Cronbach1955}. All four are compromised simultaneously in the gamification-in-history corpus --- a pattern atypical of mature research fields.

The mapping is as follows. \emph{Statistical conclusion validity} is threatened by small sample sizes (66.1\% with $n < 50$) and the near-universal failure to report effect sizes (90.5\%) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. \emph{Internal validity} is compromised by the absence of control groups (31\% of studies), uncontrolled confounding variables, and the novelty effect. \emph{Construct validity} is undermined by the rarity of validated instruments (21.6\%) and pervasive definitional ambiguity (85.1\% --- the foundational weakness elaborated in the construct validity cascade, Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}). \emph{External validity} is limited by the universal failure on the representativeness criterion (Q2: 0\% favourable, as documented in Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}) and geographic concentration (55.4\% of studies in four countries).

The simultaneous compromise of all four validity types means that no inferential pathway from the corpus is secure: not from data to effect estimate (statistical conclusion), not from correlation to cause (internal), not from measure to construct (construct), not from sample to population (external). The remainder of this section unpacks each threat in detail before analysing their interactions and cumulative implications.

\subsubsection{Sample size and statistical power}

At the corpus median of $n = 34$, a two-group study can detect only $d \approx 0.98$ --- an effect approximately five times larger than Kraft's (2020) recalibrated ``large'' benchmark ($d = 0.20$) for scalable educational interventions \cite{Kraft2020}. No study in the corpus approaches the $n \approx 394$ per group needed to detect $d = 0.20$. Even the paired pre-post design at median $n$ detects only $d \approx 0.49$, still 2.5$\times$ above the large-effect threshold.

The consequences for the published literature are predictable. Underpowered studies with null results go unpublished; those achieving statistical significance --- whether by chance or by capitalising on inflated effect estimates --- are overrepresented. This creates precisely the conditions that \citeauthor{Ioannidis2005}~\cite{Ioannidis2005} identified as producing predominantly false-positive findings: low statistical power, flexible analytic designs, and strong researcher incentive to report positive results.

\subsubsection{Research design and implementation}

As documented in Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}, 31\% of studies lack control groups entirely, control group usage has declined temporally ($-$13.5~pp from 2004--2017 to 2018--2024), and only one study employed a randomised controlled design \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Schulz2010CONSORT}. These design limitations create a structural ceiling on evidence strength: studies without control groups cannot support causal inference regardless of their quality on other dimensions \cite{HigginsThomas2019}.

\textbf{The Slater paradox.} The sole study rated at low overall risk of bias \cite{15Slater2022} was qualitative. Paradoxically, the methodology best meeting the adapted ROBIS quality criteria \citeauthor{Whiting2016}~\cite{Whiting2016} generates the least generalisable evidence. This occurs because the signalling questions assessing reporting completeness are more readily satisfied by detailed qualitative accounts than by thinly-reported experiments --- an artefact of the assessment framework that nevertheless reveals the reporting deficit in quantitative studies.

\textbf{Implementation fidelity} introduces two distinct bias directions. \emph{Technical fidelity} threats --- GPS/connectivity failures, hardware limitations, AR recognition issues \cite{58Shih2015173, 51Ardito2007, 25Repantis2011502} --- reduce the intervention ``dose'' delivered. \emph{Pedagogical fidelity} threats --- teachers using games ``merely as visual aids'' rather than engaging students in active historical inquiry \cite{61Karsenti202027} --- substitute a lower-intensity treatment for the intended intervention. Both produce \emph{attenuation bias} (systematic underestimation of the true effect). Time constraints compound this problem: insufficient class time for adequate implementation produces incomplete student experiences \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 46Stirling2022}.

Crucially, attenuation bias from implementation failures operates in the opposite direction to inflation bias from absent controls and publication selection. The net direction of the combined bias is indeterminate --- the corpus cannot tell us whether it overestimates or underestimates the true effect, only that the estimate is unreliable.

\subsubsection{Construct and external validity threats}

\textbf{Measurement validity.} Only 21.6\% of studies used validated instruments, and 9.5\% reported effect sizes \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Self-report measures introduce directional bias toward overestimation: ``perceived learning'' \cite{48Moketar2021, 21Ghulamani2017} measures student \emph{beliefs about learning}, not learning itself \cite{5kazanidis2018alpha, 20Erandaru2014}. The near-absence of effect-size reporting (90.5\%) prevents cross-study comparison of magnitude, rendering even directionally consistent findings incommensurable \cite{Borenstein2009}.

\textbf{The history-specific measurement challenge.} Unlike STEM fields, history education lacks standardised achievement tests. Historical thinking --- source analysis, contextualisation, corroboration, perspective-taking --- requires constructed-response assessments that do not yet exist in validated, cross-culturally comparable form. The measurement gap is therefore partly \emph{structural}: even well-resourced studies face an instrument deficit that is specific to the disciplinary domain \cite{Cronbach1955}.

\textbf{External validity and the Q2 problem.} The universal failure on Q2 (representativeness), documented in Section~\ref{sec:qa-results} and benchmarked against \cite{Wild2022}, acquires special significance for a \emph{culturally embedded} subject. History curricula differ fundamentally across national contexts --- what counts as ``history'' in Taiwan differs from the USA or Ukraine. The 55.4\% geographic concentration in four countries \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} is therefore not merely a sampling limitation but a \emph{construct limitation}: the interventions teach different content to students with different prior knowledge and cultural frames. The novelty effect \cite{DichevDicheva2017, Hamari2014}, the impossibility of blinding participants to gamified interventions, and uncontrolled prior gaming experience \cite{49Huizenga2007127, 45Yu2016727} further moderate effects in ways the current evidence base cannot quantify.

\subsubsection{Bias compounding and interaction}

The preceding threats do not operate independently --- they interact multiplicatively. The domain co-occurrence data from Section~\ref{sec:qa-results} provide quantitative evidence: 43.2\% of studies are rated high risk across all three ROBIS domains simultaneously, compared to 29.9\% expected under statistical independence ($0.851 \times 0.635 \times 0.554$) \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, Whiting2016}. The observed rate significantly exceeds the independent expectation, confirming systematic co-occurrence of validity threats.

\input{figures/bias_interaction_network}

Figure~\ref{fig:bias-interaction-network} maps the directed interaction pathways. Each arrow represents a mechanism by which weakness in one validity type amplifies weakness in another: small samples inflate effects that absent controls cannot correct (statistical conclusion $\to$ internal); unvalidated instruments conflate treatment effects with novelty (construct $\to$ internal); design limitations preclude generalisable causal claims (internal $\to$ external); definitional ambiguity ensures that the wrong construct is measured (construct $\to$ statistical conclusion); and geographic concentration produces culturally-specific instruments (external $\to$ construct) \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Borenstein2009}.

\textbf{The ``triple threat.''} The combination of small sample + no control group + unvalidated instrument describes a study that cannot reliably detect effects, cannot attribute detected effects to the intervention, and cannot be certain what was measured. At the corpus level, publication bias + underpowered studies + no effect-size reporting means the magnitude of overestimation (or underestimation, given implementation fidelity threats) is unknown \cite{Ioannidis2005, Hattie2009}.

\textbf{The reinforcing cycle.} Positive findings from underpowered studies encourage further small studies using similar unvalidated instruments, perpetuating the same weaknesses. The corpus grows in volume but not in information content --- a pattern consistent with the ``knowledge gap persistence'' identified as Stage~5 of the construct validity cascade (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}).

\subsubsection{Evidence certainty assessment}

Following SWiM Item~9 \cite{Campbell2020}, evidence certainty is assessed using a modified GRADE approach adapted for narrative synthesis \cite{GuyattGRADE2008, GRADE}. The starting point for the predominantly observational and pre-experimental designs in the corpus is \emph{Low}.

\input{figures/evidence_certainty_grade}

Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-certainty-grade} presents the downgrading cascade across five GRADE domains and four outcome categories:

\emph{Engagement and motivation} (Very Low): Findings are consistently positive (no inconsistency downgrade), but serious risk of bias (self-report measures, no blinding), serious indirectness (novelty confound), very serious imprecision (median $n = 34$), and suspected publication bias each warrant downgrading.

\emph{Knowledge acquisition} (Very Low): Results are inconsistent across study designs (pre-post vs.\ controlled), with serious risk of bias, serious indirectness (construct ambiguity), and very serious imprecision.

\emph{Historical thinking} (Very Low): Near-absence of direct evidence. The few studies addressing historical thinking skills use proxy measures (e.g., content recall rather than source analysis), producing serious indirectness and very serious imprecision.

\emph{Long-term retention} (Very Low): Almost no studies include delayed post-tests. The evidence is too thin to assess consistency or magnitude.

``Very Low'' certainty means that any estimate of effect is very uncertain. This does not mean gamification is ineffective --- it means the evidence base cannot reliably distinguish between a true positive, null, or negative effect. The field produces \emph{signals} but not \emph{evidence} in the GRADE sense \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}.

The simultaneous compromise of all four validity types (Section~\ref{sec:detailed-bias}) and the Very Low evidence certainty across all outcome domains confirm the pre-paradigmatic status identified through the construct validity cascade (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}). The implications for future research design are addressed in Section~\ref{sec:implications-field}; remedial reporting steps are operationalised in the proposed checklist (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}).

\subsection{Implications for the field} \label{sec:implications-field}

\subsubsection{Interpretive caution and the effect-size inflation problem}

The Very Low GRADE certainty established in Section~\ref{sec:detailed-bias} demands that reported effect sizes be interpreted not as point estimates of true effects but as upper bounds whose direction cannot be reliably determined. The practical consequences of this for practitioners and policy-makers are substantial.

In educational technology, the calibration standards for interpreting effect sizes have been progressively refined. \cite{Tamim2011} meta-meta-analysis of 25 years of technology-enhanced learning found an average effect of $d = 0.35$. \citeauthor{Hattie2009}~\cite{Hattie2009} established $d = 0.40$ as the ``hinge point'' above which effects become educationally meaningful. More recently, \cite{Kraft2020} recalibrated expectations for scalable educational interventions, arguing that $d = 0.20$ should be considered ``large'' in real-world settings. Yet, as demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:detailed-bias}, the typical study in the gamification-in-history corpus cannot detect effects below $d \approx 0.98$ due to inadequate sample sizes. This means the corpus can only detect effects approximately five times larger than what mature educational research considers ``large.''

Published positive findings therefore likely represent one of three sources: (a) true effects that happen to be very large --- plausible for short-term engagement, implausible for sustained learning gains; (b) inflated estimates produced by small samples, absent controls, and selective reporting; or (c) false positives \citep{Ioannidis2005}. Without effect-size reporting (90.5\% absence, Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}), the relative contribution of each source is unknowable. Practitioners adopting gamification on this evidence base are making decisions under deep uncertainty --- not necessarily wrong decisions, but uninformed ones \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\subsubsection{Structural drivers of methodological weakness}

The preceding sections established \emph{what} is wrong with the evidence base. This section addresses \emph{why} these weaknesses persist. The argument is that methodological weakness in gamification-in-history research is not primarily a failure of individual researchers but a predictable outcome of institutional and disciplinary structures.

\cite{Chalmers2009} estimated that 85\% of biomedical research investment is wasted due to poor design, poor reporting, and non-publication. The gamification-in-history corpus exhibits all three waste pathways simultaneously: underpowered designs (66.1\% with $n < 50$), inadequate reporting (mean MRQS 4.31/10), and suspected publication bias (Section~\ref{sec:detailed-bias}). Crucially, these pathways are mutually reinforcing: underpowered studies that achieve significance are published; their inflated effects set expectations for the next generation of studies; reviewers acculturated to low-quality norms accept the next wave of underpowered work; and the cycle perpetuates.

Conference-paper dominance compounds the problem. As established in the MRQS analysis, 54.1\% of the corpus consists of conference papers, which undergo less rigorous peer review, impose strict page limits that preclude methodological detail, and are often published as preliminary findings that are never followed by full journal articles \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. This publication structure systematically selects for underdeveloped methodology.

The field's interdisciplinary nature creates additional fragmentation. The corpus spans educational technology, computer science, history education, and game design --- each with different methodological norms \cite{CookReed2015}. History education researchers may not be trained in experimental design; computer science researchers may treat pedagogical evaluation as secondary to system development; educational technologists may lack subject-matter expertise in historical thinking. The quality-tool comparison (Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}, Figure~\ref{fig:quality-tool-comparison}) confirmed that no existing assessment tool bridges all these traditions --- which is precisely why the MRQS was developed \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

Finally, there is an improvement paradox: each individual quality improvement --- larger sample, control group, validated instrument, effect-size reporting --- increases study cost and complexity substantially. A study achieving all four requires institutional resources, multi-site collaboration, and longitudinal commitment that few gamification projects command. The field's weakness is partly an infrastructure problem, not merely a skills problem \cite{Glasziou2014, Clark2016, Connolly2012, KoivistoHamari2019}.

\subsubsection{The history education specificity problem}

General educational technology recommendations, while necessary, are insufficient for gamification in history. The discipline faces three unique challenges that explain why cross-domain prescriptions have limited traction.

\emph{The measurement deficit.} Unlike mathematics or science, history education lacks standardised achievement tests with cross-cultural validity. Historical thinking --- source analysis, contextualisation, corroboration, perspective-taking --- involves interpretive skills that resist multiple-choice assessment \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}. Existing instruments are context-specific and have not been cross-culturally validated. This is not researcher negligence but a structural feature of the discipline: the ``content'' of history is interpretive, not factual, and interpretation standards vary across historiographical traditions \cite{Crymble2021}.

\emph{Cultural embeddedness.} History curricula are nationally specific --- the subject matter differs fundamentally across educational systems. A gamification intervention teaching the American Revolution has no direct comparability with one teaching the Ottoman Empire or Ukrainian independence \cite{Wild2022, Torgerson202237}. This makes cross-study comparison inherently more problematic than in STEM, where the content (Newton's laws, photosynthesis) is culturally invariant. The Q2 universal failure (0\% representativeness, Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}) is partly a consequence of this structural feature rather than poor sampling practice alone \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\emph{The authenticity--engagement tension.} Gamification in history faces a discipline-specific trade-off: game mechanics (points, leaderboards, time pressure) may conflict with historical thinking goals (careful source analysis, nuanced interpretation, tolerance of ambiguity) \cite{Ditlmann2025}. Studies that optimise for engagement may inadvertently undermine disciplinary learning; studies that optimise for historical thinking may produce results that appear ``negative'' on engagement metrics. This tension is largely absent in STEM gamification and requires discipline-specific outcome frameworks that the current evidence base does not provide \cite{Clark2016, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

Second, no RCTs exist for any outcome domain that provide quantitative effect estimates; cost-effectiveness is a total evidence void. These gaps are not random omissions but systematic consequences of the structural drivers identified above: history-specific outcomes are harder to measure, and rigorous designs require resources the field's infrastructure does not routinely provide.

Based on this analysis, four research design priorities emerge in descending order of yield: (1)~adequately powered quasi-experimental studies of knowledge acquisition and historical thinking --- the highest-yield cell in the evidence gap map; (2)~delayed post-tests for any outcome domain to address the long-term retention void; (3)~multi-site studies enabling the representativeness that Q2's universal failure currently precludes; and (4)~cost-effectiveness analysis, currently absent from all 74 studies \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Borenstein2009, Schulz2010CONSORT, Campbell2020}. For AI-assisted reviews, the LLM validation results (Section~\ref{sec:llm-validation}) indicate that large language models should be restricted to factual extraction; human judgment remains essential for evaluative tasks \cite{MalekiKarami2024, Taneri2025, Rubinstein2025}.

\subsection{Proposed reporting checklist} \label{sec:reporting-checklist}

\subsubsection{Positioning within existing standards}

Multiple reporting standards exist for research quality: CONSORT for randomised controlled trials \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}, PRISMA for systematic reviews, MERSQI and MMAT for mixed methods, and JBI checklists for various designs \cite{HigginsThomas2019, Whiting2016}. As demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:qa-results} (Figure~\ref{fig:quality-tool-comparison}), none adequately addresses the specific requirements of gamification research in education: they either assume experimental designs that most studies in this field do not use, lack domain-specific items for game design elements, or do not accommodate the definitional heterogeneity problem identified in Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}.

The proposed checklist is derived empirically from the MRQS analysis \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs} --- each item targets a specific weakness identified in the 74-study corpus. Unlike general-purpose tools, it is calibrated to the actual failure modes of gamification-in-history research. It functions as a ``minimum viable reporting standard'' \cite{CookReed2015}: the smallest set of items that would move the field from Very Low evidence certainty toward assessable and potentially synthesisable evidence.

\subsubsection{The tiered checklist}

Items are organised into three priority tiers based on two criteria: (a)~current prevalence in the corpus (lower prevalence indicates greater improvement potential) and (b)~impact on evidence synthesis (items enabling meta-analysis or GRADE upgrading are prioritised). Figure~\ref{fig:checklist-priority-tiers} visualises this tiered structure.

\input{figures/checklist_priority_tiers}

The revised 12-item checklist comprises the original 10 empirically derived items, each tagged with its priority tier, current prevalence, and the specific MRQS item it addresses, plus two history-education-specific additions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{T1.}] Report effect sizes (Cohen's $d$, $\eta^2$, or equivalent) alongside inferential statistics \cite{Borenstein2009, Cohen1960}. \emph{Prevalence: 9.5\%. Enables: meta-analytic synthesis.}
\item[\textbf{T1.}] Describe measurement instruments and provide evidence of validity and/or reliability. \emph{Prevalence: 21.6\%. Addresses: Stage~3 of the construct validity cascade (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}).}
\item[\textbf{T1.}] Provide an explicit definition of gamification (or game-based learning, serious games, etc.) and position the study within a recognised taxonomy \cite{Deterding2011, 36Scholz2021, Buckley2018}. \emph{Prevalence: 14.9\%. Addresses: Stage~1 of the construct validity cascade.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Include a comparison condition where the research question involves effectiveness claims \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, HigginsThomas2019}. \emph{Prevalence: 31.1\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Report inferential test statistics with exact $p$-values. \emph{Prevalence: 39.2\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Employ a pre--post design with clearly timed assessments. \emph{Prevalence: 39.2\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Achieve sample sizes of $n \geq 30$ per condition, with a target of $n \geq 64$ for medium effects ($d = 0.50$, $\alpha = .05$, power $= .80$). \emph{Prevalence: 50.0\%.}
\item[\textbf{T2.}] Discuss study limitations explicitly, including threats to internal and external validity. \emph{Prevalence: 47.3\%.}
\item[\textbf{T3.}] Report the sample size, sampling method, and available demographic characteristics \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT}. \emph{Prevalence: 83.8\%.}
\item[\textbf{T3.}] Specify the theoretical framework guiding the design and interpretation. Describe game design elements employed and the rationale for their selection \cite{SEABORN201514, Sailer2017}. \emph{Prevalence: 94.6\%.}
\item[\textbf{H.}] Describe the historical content domain and its cultural specificity; acknowledge limitations on cross-cultural transferability. \emph{Rationale: the Q2 universal failure (Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}) is partly structural --- history's cultural embeddedness means representativeness requires explicit scoping.}
\item[\textbf{H.}] If measuring historical thinking, specify which dimensions (source analysis, contextualisation, corroboration, perspective-taking) and report the validation status of instruments used \cite{MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}. \emph{Rationale: the disciplinary measurement deficit (Section~\ref{sec:implications-field}) means that instrument transparency is especially critical.}
\end{enumerate}

Adoption of Tier~1 items alone would transform the field's evidence base: effect sizes would enable meta-analysis; validated instruments would address Stage~3 of the construct validity cascade; and explicit definitions would address Stage~1. The remaining tiers strengthen individual study quality, but the Tier~1 items are the prerequisites for cumulative knowledge-building \cite{Glasziou2014}.

\subsubsection{Validation pathway and feasibility}

The checklist is an analytical derivation from the MRQS corpus \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}, not an empirically validated instrument. Validation would require prospective application: future systematic reviews could score new studies against these 12 items and correlate checklist compliance with GRADE upgrading potential \cite{GuyattGRADE2008}. Such validation is beyond the scope of the present thesis but constitutes a natural next step.

Journal page limits may constrain full compliance; however, supplementary materials and data repositories can accommodate detailed reporting without affecting word counts. The Tier~1 items --- effect sizes, definitions, instrument validation --- add minimal word count, as they require precision, not volume.

The ``research waste'' argument provides the strongest feasibility case. \cite{Glasziou2014} estimated that incomplete reporting wastes approximately 50\% of research investment. The cost of implementing this checklist is negligible relative to the cost of producing another 74 studies whose evidence cannot be synthesised \cite{Chalmers2009}. The checklist is not an additional burden but a minimum standard for the research investment to generate usable knowledge.

%\subsection{Conclusions to Section 1.3}

The systematic quality assessment of 74 studies using the MRQS \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs, KorniienkoSemerikov2024} reveals a field in a pre-paradigmatic state. The quantitative portrait is stark: 83.8\% of studies carry high risk of bias, the mean MRQS is 4.31/10, 90.5\% of studies fail to report effect sizes \cite{Borenstein2009}, only 21.6\% use validated instruments, and 85.1\% lack explicit gamification definitions \cite{Deterding2011, Buckley2018}. Domain-level analysis \cite{Whiting2016} identifies participant selection as the most persistent weakness (85.1\% high risk), with no study meeting the representativeness criterion ($Q_2 = 0\%$). The LLM validation study confirms that automated risk-of-bias assessment achieves only slight agreement with human reviewers ($\kappa = -0.04$ to $0.21$) \cite{LandisKoch1977, Fleiss1971}, while factual extraction remains highly reliable (82--97\% agreement) \cite{MalekiKarami2024, Taneri2025, Rubinstein2025}.

The simultaneous compromise of all four validity types --- statistical conclusion, internal, construct, and external --- and the Very Low GRADE certainty across all outcome domains confirm the pre-paradigmatic status identified through the construct validity cascade (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}). This is not a failure of individual researchers but a consequence of the field's structural conditions: interdisciplinary fragmentation, conference-paper dominance, the measurement deficit inherent in history education, and the cost of rigorous experimental design \cite{Chalmers2009, Glasziou2014}. The proposed reporting checklist (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}) offers a minimum viable pathway from pre-paradigmatic accumulation toward cumulative knowledge-building, with three Tier~1 items --- effect sizes, validated instruments, and explicit definitions --- identified as the highest-leverage interventions \cite{Landers2019, Cronbach1955}.

These findings directly inform the design of the experimental study presented in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology}--\ref{ch:results}. The systematic review has identified six design requirements that the experimental study must satisfy to avoid replicating the corpus's weaknesses:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Explicit definition of the gamification approach, addressing Tier~1 and Stage~1 of the construct validity cascade;
\item Pre--post measurement with delayed post-test, addressing the long-term retention evidence void (Table~\ref{tab:evidence-gap-map});
\item Validated or transparently described instruments with reliability reporting (Tier~1);
\item Effect-size reporting alongside inferential statistics (Tier~1);
\item Detailed description of game design elements and pedagogical rationale (Tier~2);
\item Self-assessment against the proposed 12-item reporting checklist (Tiers~1--3 and history-specific items).
\end{enumerate}
The degree to which the experimental study meets these requirements is explicitly evaluated in the Discussion (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

Before presenting the experimental study, Section~\ref{sec:digcomp} examines the relationship between gamification and digital competence development through the DigComp~2.0 framework --- connecting the quality critique of Section~1.3 to the broader pedagogical context within which the experimental intervention operates.


\section{Gamification and digital competence: implementation through DigComp 2.0} \label{sec:digcomp}

\subsection{The DigComp framework family}

The European Digital Competence Framework for Citizens (DigComp) is not a single document but an evolving framework family. DigComp~1.0 (2013) established the foundational five-area, 21-competence structure. DigComp~2.0 \citep{Vuorikari2016} updated the conceptual reference model and vocabulary descriptors. DigComp~2.1 \citep{Carretero2017} introduced eight proficiency levels (Foundation~1--2, Intermediate~3--4, Advanced~5--6, Highly Specialised~7--8), inspired by the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR). DigComp~2.2 \citep{Vuorikari2022} added over 250 new examples of knowledge, skills, and attitudes covering emerging topics such as artificial intelligence literacy, remote work, and disinformation --- without altering the structural framework itself. Most recently, DigComp~3.0 \citep{Cosgrove2025}, published in 2025, maintains the same five-area, 21-competence structure but introduces over 500 learning outcomes, systematic AI competence integration across all areas, and consolidates proficiency levels from eight to four (basic, intermediate, advanced, highly advanced).

Distinct from the citizen-oriented DigComp, the European Framework for the Digital Competence of Educators (DigCompEdu) \citep{Redecker2017} addresses educator-specific competences across 6~areas and 22~competences. Its Area~6 (``Facilitating Learners' Digital Competence'') explicitly links teacher digital competence to student digital competence development, making it relevant for pedagogical framing. However, the primary reference in this thesis remains DigComp, because the study evaluates \textit{student} competence development, not teacher competence.

In the Ukrainian context, \citeauthor{Ovcharuk2023}~\cite{Ovcharuk2023} surveyed 54,254 educators using the DigComp framework and identified three critical challenges: lacking ICT equipment, unstable internet access, and insufficient digital competence. These challenges directly mirror the conditions documented in the present experimental study (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}), where power outages, internet disruptions, and air-raid alerts shaped digital learning. The present study thus contributes to a small but growing body of evidence on DigComp implementation under crisis conditions \citep{UNESCO2023}.

This thesis uses DigComp~2.0 as the primary conceptual reference (consistent with \cite{Titova2025cte}), supplemented by proficiency levels from DigComp~2.1 and updated examples from DigComp~2.2. The experimental study (October 2024 -- May 2025) was designed and conducted before the publication of DigComp~3.0; nevertheless, all versions from 2.0 to 3.0 maintain the same 5-area, 21-competence structure, so the findings remain structurally compatible with the latest iteration. The differences across versions lie in granularity, illustration, and --- in the case of 3.0 --- learning outcomes and AI integration, not in conceptual architecture (Figure~\ref{fig:digcomp-framework-landscape}).

\subsection{The discipline-blindness problem: towards history-specific digital competence}

This discipline-blindness affects both the nascent digital history literature (6~studies, none with competence frameworks) and the larger gamification corpus (74~studies, only 21.6\% using validated instruments), reinforcing the need for a history-specific competence-based approach.

DigComp was designed as a citizen-level framework applicable across all domains. This generality is its strength for policy purposes but its weakness for disciplinary education. ``Information and data literacy'' means something fundamentally different for a historian evaluating a digitised primary source than for a general citizen browsing a search engine. The measurement deficit identified in the quality assessment (Section~\ref{sec:qa-results}) --- the absence of standardised history-specific assessment instruments --- is partly a consequence of this discipline-blindness: without a history-specific competence description, there is nothing discipline-specific to measure. The systematic review (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}) found that only 21.6\% of studies used validated instruments, and the evidence gap map (Table~\ref{tab:evidence-gap-map}) shows historical thinking as a critical gap. This section proposes the first history-specific interpretation of each DigComp competence area, addressing this gap.

\textbf{Area~1 --- Information and data literacy.} For historians, this area requires evaluating the authenticity of digitised primary sources, assessing the provenance and reliability of digital archives, distinguishing primary from secondary sources in online environments, and critically analysing how digitalisation processes may alter or decontextualise historical evidence \citep{Crymble2021, ROMEIN}. This is epistemologically distinct from generic ``browsing, searching, and filtering.''

\textbf{Area~2 --- Communication and collaboration.} For historians, this means engaging in scholarly discourse about contested interpretations through digital platforms, sharing primary sources with appropriate contextualisation and attribution, participating in digital public history, and practising the disciplinary norms of historical argumentation in digital spaces \citep{Graham20151}.

\textbf{Area~3 --- Digital content creation.} For historians, this means creating digital narratives, building interactive timelines, producing map-based visualisations of historical processes, and curating digital exhibitions --- activities requiring both technical proficiency and deep knowledge of historiographical conventions \citep{Crymble2021, MonteSano2012}.

\textbf{Area~4 --- Safety.} Standard DigComp safety competences apply, but this thesis adds a dimension absent from all existing DigComp implementations: navigating digital learning under active armed conflict, managing information security in politically sensitive contexts, and protecting physical well-being when digital participation occurs during air raids and power outages \citep{UNESCO2023, Ovcharuk2023}.

\textbf{Area~5 --- Problem solving.} For historians, this means selecting appropriate digital tools for specific historical questions (GIS for spatial analysis, text mining for corpus analysis, database management for prosopography), adapting analytical methods when primary sources are incomplete or contradictory, and recognising the limitations of computational approaches to interpretive questions \citep{Graham20151, Reisman2012}.

This reinterpretation has methodological consequences. Generic DigComp assessment instruments cannot capture whether a history student has developed the ability to evaluate a digitised primary source or to construct a digital historical narrative. Discipline-specific instruments are needed, yet the evidence gap map shows historical thinking as a critical gap. The proposed reporting checklist (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}) addresses this by requiring specification of historical thinking dimensions and instrument validation status. The history-specific DigComp interpretation developed here provides the conceptual vocabulary for such instruments (Figure~\ref{fig:digcomp-history-adaptation}).

\subsection{Gamification as a vehicle for digital competence development}

The dual evidence gap identified in Sections~\ref{sec:dh-review}--\ref{sec:mrqs} --- few digital history studies and methodologically weak gamification studies --- makes explicit competence-based design essential rather than optional.

The relationship between gamification and digital competence is not unidirectional. Gamification develops digital competence by requiring students to navigate digital environments, process digital information, and engage with digital assessment tools. Conversely, digital competence enables more effective engagement with gamified learning environments --- digitally competent students navigate complex platforms, troubleshoot technical issues, and exploit the full range of gamification affordances. This reciprocal relationship creates a virtuous cycle that the integration model formalises.

Recent systematic reviews support this model. \citeauthor{BarrosoTristan2025}~\cite{BarrosoTristan2025} reviewed gamification's role in developing teachers' digital competence and confirmed positive effects across multiple DigComp areas. \citeauthor{Titova2025cte}~\cite{Titova2025cte} demonstrated through the PC Building Simulator case study that simulation-based gamification can simultaneously address all five DigComp areas, with particularly strong effects on information literacy and problem-solving competences. The broader gamification literature reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review} confirms that gamification can develop transversal competencies beyond subject-specific knowledge, with engagement (70.3\% of studies) and knowledge acquisition (60.8\%) as the most frequently measured outcomes.

Building on \cite{Titova2025cte} and integrating findings from the systematic review (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}) and quality assessment (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}), we propose a three-layer integration model for gamification-mediated digital competence development in history education:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Layer~1 (Input):} Gamification design elements --- the six principles identified in the systematic review (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}): historical authenticity, narrative integration, progressive difficulty, immediate feedback, social elements, and multiple modalities.
\item \textbf{Layer~2 (Framework):} DigComp competence areas, reinterpreted for history education as developed above, providing the vocabulary for specifying which competences each design element develops.
\item \textbf{Layer~3 (Outcomes):} Discipline-specific learning outcomes aligned with the evidence gap map (Table~\ref{tab:evidence-gap-map}): engagement and motivation, knowledge acquisition, attitudes and satisfaction, and historical thinking.
\item \textbf{Cross-cutting dimension:} Wartime resilience --- affecting all three layers by constraining design choices (Layer~1), adding safety competence requirements (Layer~2), and introducing infrastructure-related outcome variability (Layer~3).
\end{itemize}

The model is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:gamification-digcomp-integration}. Its primary value is to make explicit the developmental pathways through which specific gamification elements contribute to specific competence outcomes --- connections that remain implicit or untheorised in the existing literature \citep{Deterding2011, Sailer2017, KorniienkoSemerikov2024}.

\subsection{Implementation guidelines} \label{sec:guidelines}

Based on the synthesis of the systematic reviews and the DigComp alignment analysis, the following guidelines for implementing gamification in history education were developed \citep{Korniienko2025cte, Vuorikari2016}. Each guideline is grounded in the evidence base established in Sections~\ref{sec:dh-review}--\ref{sec:mrqs}, connected to specific DigComp competence areas, and linked to the design principles identified in Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Start with clear learning objectives} (DigComp Areas~1,~5; Design Principle: historical authenticity): Define specific historical knowledge outcomes and digital competencies before selecting gamification elements. The learning objectives should specify: (a)~the historical content to be mastered, (b)~the historical thinking skills to be developed, and (c)~the digital competencies to be practised. \textit{Evidence:} The MRQS analysis showed that studies with explicit theoretical frameworks (94.6\%) produced more interpretable results.

\item \textbf{Use progressive complexity} (DigComp Area~5; Design Principle: progressive difficulty): Structure gamified activities from recognition tasks (matching, multiple choice) through analysis (source evaluation, chronological sequencing) to synthesis (constructing narratives, arguing causation). \textit{Evidence:} This progression mirrors cognitive development sequences identified in the design principles (Section~\ref{sec:design-principles}).

\item \textbf{Maintain historical rigour} (DigComp Area~1; Design Principle: historical authenticity): Ensure that game elements do not trivialise or distort historical content. All quiz questions should be based on verified facts and aligned with curriculum standards. \textit{Evidence:} 10.8\% of reviewed studies reported negative outcomes, often associated with superficial gamification.

\item \textbf{Provide multiple pathways} (DigComp Areas~3,~5; Design Principle: multiple modalities): Offer both required and optional gamified activities to accommodate different ability levels. The HistoryLikeGame platform exemplifies this with nine historical periods plus bonus sections and an Omega comprehensive review. \textit{Evidence:} Differentiated design addresses the engagement-rigour tension.

\item \textbf{Integrate assessment with learning} (DigComp Areas~1,~5; Design Principle: immediate feedback): Use gamified quizzes as both formative assessment and learning activities, providing immediate feedback and retry opportunities. \textit{Evidence:} The case of Talash~D. (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}), who submitted 7~attempts with progressively improving scores, demonstrates iterative mastery learning.

\item \textbf{Ensure inclusivity, accessibility, and resilience} (DigComp Area~4; Design Principle: multiple modalities): Design for diverse learner needs and connectivity conditions. Implement inclusive features (theme switching, font scaling, motion reduction, high contrast) that accommodate visual, motor, and cognitive diversity~\cite{McCrindle2013}. The HistoryLikeGame platform's static HTML hub-and-spoke architecture ($\approx$88~KB shared infrastructure) provides a model, combining built-in inclusivity features with offline capability for conflict-zone resilience. \textit{Evidence:} In wartime contexts, the five-layer resilience model ensures educational continuity.

\item \textbf{Build community through gamification} (DigComp Area~2; Design Principle: social elements): Use creative naming, shared references, and collaborative elements to foster belonging. \textit{Evidence:} Thematic naming conventions (gemstones, Greek mythology, flowers, minerals) created cultural enrichment alongside history content.

\item \textbf{Document systematically} (DigComp Areas~1,~3; MRQS finding): Maintain detailed records of implementation (chat logs, assessment data, platform analytics). \textit{Evidence:} Only 47.3\% of reviewed studies discussed limitations and 9.5\% reported effect sizes --- systematic documentation addresses these critical reporting gaps (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}).
\end{enumerate}

These guidelines operationalise the six design principles from the gamification review \citep{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} through the DigComp competence framework and the quality standards from the MRQS assessment \citep{Korniienko2025mrqs}. Their application in the experimental study is documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Conclusions to Chapter 1} \label{sec:ch1-conclusions}

This chapter has pursued a progressive narrowing strategy: from digital history pedagogy as an emerging field (Section~\ref{sec:dh-review}) to gamification as its most-researched component (Section~\ref{sec:gamification-review}), through quality assessment exposing critical methodological weaknesses (Section~\ref{sec:mrqs}), to a competence framework providing theoretical grounding for intervention design (Section~\ref{sec:digcomp}). The analysis rests on two PRISMA-guided systematic reviews encompassing 80~studies (6~+~74), one novel quality assessment instrument (MRQS), and one three-layer integration model --- collectively yielding the following findings.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The research--practice gap.} Only 6~pedagogical studies address digital history education \cite{Bell2016, Coleborne2011, Davis2017, Lee2014159, McLean20144, Soh2013} versus 74~in gamification in history education \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024}. Gamification dominates the evidence base, yet the broader field of digital history pedagogy lacks the critical mass needed for evidence-based practice. This gap itself justifies the present study's dual focus on gamification and digital competence development.

\item \textbf{Definitional and methodological crisis.} 85.1\% of gamification studies lack explicit definitions \cite{Deterding2011, Buckley2018}; 83.8\% carry a high risk of bias under MRQS assessment \cite{Korniienko2025mrqs}. These are not independent weaknesses: the construct validity cascade (Section~\ref{sec:def-quality}) shows how definitional ambiguity propagates through measurement, analysis, and interpretation. The temporal divergence paradox --- more recent studies scoring lower on reporting quality despite increased methodological awareness in the field --- indicates that the corpus is growing faster than its quality infrastructure.

\item \textbf{The evidence paradox.} Despite pervasive methodological weakness, consistent patterns emerge: engagement effects are robust (70.3\% of studies), knowledge effects moderate (51.4\%, $d = 0.28$--$0.61$, concentrated on factual recall), and 81.1\% of studies report at least one harmful effect \cite{KorniienkoSemerikov2024} --- a finding rarely acknowledged in optimistic literature summaries. Effect-size inflation due to low-quality designs means that even positive findings require interpretive caution.

\item \textbf{The disciplinary signature of history.} History education exhibits a distinctive gamification profile that differentiates it from other domains: narrative and immersive elements dominate over points-and-badges mechanics (inverted element distribution), epistemic harms --- colonialist narratives, historical inaccuracies --- are unique to the discipline, and only 9.5\% of studies cite history-discipline theory \cite{Seixas2013, Wineburg2001}. This disconnection between gamification practice and historical thinking scholarship has direct implications for intervention design.

\item \textbf{Three theoretical contributions.} The chapter advances three novel theoretical constructs: (a)~the first discipline-specific interpretation of DigComp for history education, addressing the framework's documented discipline-blindness through five area-specific adaptations grounded in historical epistemology \cite{Crymble2021, MonteSano2012, Reisman2012}; (b)~a three-layer integration model connecting gamification design elements, DigComp competence areas, and discipline-specific learning outcomes; (c)~the identification of wartime resilience as a cross-cutting dimension affecting design, competence development, and outcome measurement --- absent from all existing DigComp implementations \cite{Ovcharuk2023, UNESCO2023}.

\item \textbf{Three methodological and practical contributions.} The chapter also contributes: (a)~the MRQS quality assessment instrument with LLM validation \cite{MalekiKarami2024, Taneri2025, Rubinstein2025}, addressing the absence of domain-specific quality tools; (b)~a 12-item reporting checklist (Section~\ref{sec:reporting-checklist}) aligned with CONSORT/PRISMA standards \cite{Schulz2010CONSORT, Page2021}; (c)~eight evidence-grounded implementation guidelines (Section~\ref{sec:guidelines}) operationalising the six design principles through the DigComp competence framework \cite{Korniienko2025cte, KorniienkoSemerikov2024, Korniienko2025mrqs}.

\item \textbf{DigComp framework compatibility.} The DigComp framework family --- from version~2.0 (2016) through~3.0 (2025) \cite{Vuorikari2016, Vuorikari2022, Cosgrove2025} --- maintains structural consistency across all versions (5~areas, 21~competences), ensuring that the history-specific interpretation and the integration model remain valid as the framework evolves.
\end{enumerate}

These findings collectively define the design space for the experimental study. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents a gamification-enhanced digital history course that: implements the eight guidelines from Section~\ref{sec:guidelines}; targets all five DigComp areas with proficiency-level evaluation based on DigComp~2.1 \cite{Carretero2017}; addresses the six design requirements derived from the quality critique (Section~\ref{sec:implications-field}); and is documented with sufficient methodological detail --- including effect sizes, limitation discussion, and an explicit gamification definition --- to address the reporting gaps that characterise the field. The wartime educational context provides a naturalistic setting that tests both the integration model and the resilience dimension identified as absent from prior research.
